The Norm

in print · № XXVI · 2 of 12 plates

No law tells us where to stand, whom to thank, what to find shameful, or which promises bind in honor though not in court. Yet these unwritten rules govern conduct as firmly as any statute — and feel not like rules at all, but like the way things simply are. This volume is about the norm: the convention that masquerades as nature, the custom mistaken for morality, and the quiet contest, beneath every done thing, over whose way of life shall count as the way.

The question of the volume the unwritten rules of conduct feel like the dictates of nature, or of morality itself — but are they more than conventions, kept because they are kept, that we have mistaken for necessities?
The argument

There is a vast body of law that no one ever wrote down. It tells you how close to stand when you speak to a stranger; whom to thank, and how; what to wear to a wedding and what to a funeral; when to arrive and when to leave; which promises bind you in honor though no court would hear them; and a thousand things besides, governing nearly every moment of your dealings with others. No legislature passed this law, no police enforce it, and most of it you could not recite if asked — yet you obey it almost without exception, and the rare soul who breaks it is marked at once as strange, or rude, or worse. This is the realm of the norm: the unwritten rule of conduct, conformed to and expected and quietly enforced, that does more to order social and economic life than all the statutes in the books. This volume is about how such rules work, where they come from, and why the most powerful of them are precisely the ones we have stopped seeing as rules at all.

Begin with the simplest kind, the convention. Why do we drive on the right — or, in much of the world, the left? Not because one is safer; whichever all adopt is safe, and whichever a lone driver defies is lethal. We keep to our side for one reason only: that everyone else does, and we would rather coordinate than collide. David Hume caught the essence of it two centuries before the theory existed: two men row a boat in perfect time by a convention, though they never exchanged a promise; the modern study of games gave the idea its rigor, showing such conventions to be equilibria — points of rest from which no one, so long as the others hold their course, has reason to stray. A convention is thus arbitrary in origin, for it could have settled otherwise, and adamant in its hold, for once in place each of us is pinned by the expectations of all the rest. And conventions are everywhere: in the words we greet with, the units we measure by, the side of the page we begin on. Much of what we call custom is convention of this kind — a solution to the problem of doing together what none can do alone.

It would be an error to think such rules a mere social lubricant, an etiquette atop the real business of law and market. They are load-bearing. Norms coordinate, settling the countless questions on which collective life depends and to which reason returns no answer. They sustain cooperation, the norm of reciprocity turning the one-shot encounter, in which betrayal pays, into an ongoing relation, in which it does not, and so underwriting the trust without which exchange would grind to a halt. They economize on enforcement, sparing us a contract and a constable for every dealing, since most people keep their word from habit and conscience rather than fear. The economists who study institutions count norms among them — the informal institutions, in North's phrase, that stand beside the formal ones of law and state and often do the heavier share of the work; and the students of the commons found, with Ostrom, that communities which govern their shared resources without ruin do so largely by norms of their own slow making. Strip the norms away and no law could fill the gap, for the law itself rests upon them.

But now the feature that makes the norm the deepest of the orders, and the proper subject of these volumes. A price announces itself as a number, a law as a command; both wear their made and human character on their face, and may be haggled or repealed. A norm does something stranger: at the height of its power it ceases to present itself as a rule at all. We do not experience the distance we keep from a stranger, or the gift we feel bound to answer, as conventions we are following; we experience them as facts — about what is natural, what is decent, what a reasonable person simply knows. The norm, doing its work most perfectly, vanishes into the furniture of the world, and what is merely customary comes to feel necessary, even moral, and so passes beyond question. Sumner, who gave the study of such things its early vocabulary, put it starkly: the folkways, he said, can make anything seem right.

And here the norm yields the argument of the whole work in its most intimate form. A price is a verdict on a hidden contest, mistaken for a fact of nature — and a norm is the same verdict, rendered in custom and pressed deeper into us. For a norm is a convention: one of many arrangements that might have served, selected by history and accident and the weight of mutual expectation, kept in being for no reason but that it is kept. Nothing in nature decrees the code a given people lives by; another, across a border or a century, lives by another, and neither is the dictate of the world. Yet the norm presents its contingent commands as the necessary order of things — the way decent people behave, the way things are done — so that the local is taken for the universal, the arbitrary for the right, and the made for the given. The way things are done is mistaken for the way they must be; and that mistake, worked not upon a price or a law but upon the very sense of what is proper, is the master mistake at its most thorough — pressed into the conscience of the one who makes it.

Nor, behind that naturalized command, is there only innocent coordination. A price is a verdict on a hidden contest, mistaken for a fact of nature; and beneath the norm too lies a contest — over which of the many possible conventions shall prevail, and, more consequentially, over whose interests it shall serve. For norms do not merely coordinate; they also rank and subordinate, and their genius for seeming natural is a genius for making subordination feel like propriety. The conventions that kept the woman from the ballot, that fixed each caste in its appointed place, that bent the servant's eyes down and taught him to feel the bending as fitting — these ruled not by the policeman but by everyone's settled sense of how things ought to be, the dominated enforcing on themselves the rules that bound them. A domination written into custom is the most secure of all: it needs no enforcement and permits no argument, being simply the way of the world. To see that the norm is made is therefore no idle correction but the first stir of freedom — the recovery of a question, who decreed this and whom does it serve, that the naturalized norm exists to suppress.

This sets the norm in its place among the orders these volumes have anatomized. The volume on the rule examined the written institutions — property, contract, the law, enforced by the power of the state; this one examines their unwritten twin, the conventions enforced by nothing but each other's expectation; the two together frame every price. But the norm is the more insidious of the pair, exactly because it is the less visible. A law can be read, debated, and struck down; one knows it for a human imposition even while obeying it. A norm felt as nature is obeyed without the awareness that one is obeying — and a rule that has captured the very faculty by which we might question it is a rule almost beyond reach. That is why the labor of denaturalizing a custom — of seeing the merely done as merely done — is so slow, and why it is, again and again, the precondition of every reform that follows.

The twelve plates anatomize the norm in this light. They set out the convention and the naturalization that hides it; the focal point by which one rule is chosen from many, and the favor by which reciprocity sustains it; the shame that enforces from without and the conscience from within; the yoke of the norms that entrench power; the cascade by which a norm thought eternal collapses in a generation; the unwritten code beside the written law; the handshake of trust on which the economy silently runs; the taboo that fences off what may not be sold; and, at the last, the compass — whether, if every ought is a convention, anything remains of morality, or some standpoint survives from which the customs might be judged. The norm is where the master thesis reaches into the self. For of all the made things mistaken for natural, none is mistaken so intimately as the norm, which we obey not because we are commanded but because we have made its commands our own — and to recognize it as a thing we made is to begin, however slightly, to be free of it. The argument remains open.
