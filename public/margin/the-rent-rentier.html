Rn xii

The Rentier

in print · The Rent · plate xii

Living on ownership and receiving economic rent are overlapping descriptions, not interchangeable ones. Capital costs and the price paid for a title complicate the visible receipt; Keynes’s prospect of abundant capital supplies a conditional route beyond its scarcity return. The final inquiry separates the creation of rent from the distribution of its proceeds and asks what a different arrangement would preserve.

An ordinary yield on a purchased title can carry an extraordinary privilege underneath it.The argument of this plate, after Keynes, Mill and Barkai

The rentier is the person who can live on having. The income arrives because a title is held: a field let, a bond owned, a business share inherited, a right licensed to someone who wants to use it. The figure is easy to recognize and harder to analyze. Some of those receipts repay the cost of producing something useful; some compensate exposure to loss; some replace an asset wearing out. Others are sustained by an advantage its holder need do nothing to reproduce. An economy can pay all of them through ownership. It does not follow that all are the same income, or that calling the recipient passive has already explained the payment.

That distinction prevents the volume’s argument from becoming a complaint against every return to capital. A machine must be made and maintained. An uncertain undertaking may fail, and someone bears the loss. The price of a title may embody the value of services its owner provides through finance and judgment, or the expected return to a privilege secured elsewhere. Economic rent is the surplus above what is required to keep the relevant resource or activity available. A dividend, interest payment or property bill is an observed form of receipt. The form does not by itself reveal how much of it is surplus.

Barkai, “Declining Labor and Capital Shares” (2020), introduction and §I, pp. 2421–2426 separates the required costs of capital from pure profits and emphasizes the difficulty of measuring those costs. The required return is part of the cost comparison, not automatically a pure profit.

Imagine an asset that costs one thousand to reproduce and yields eighty a year after labor, materials and operating expenses. Suppose twenty must be reserved for its annual loss of value, and forty is the appropriate required return on the capital employed, including the stipulated compensation for risk. Twenty remains after those costs. If the required return were sixty instead, the residual would vanish. If depreciation had been omitted, the apparent surplus would be overstated. The exercise is deliberately simple, but its lesson is exact: a cash receipt cannot identify a rent until the comparison has been made. In actual accounts, unmeasured investment, uncertain risk and expected changes in asset prices can alter that comparison.

Barkai, §I.A–C, pp. 2425–2426 constructs capital costs before pure profits, allowing for depreciation, financing and expected capital-price changes. The numbers here are hypothetical: 80 − 20 − 40 = 20; replacing 40 with 60 gives zero. They are not the paper’s estimated magnitudes.

There is a second complication. A scarcity can yield rent even when a new buyer earns only an ordinary return. A site paying one hundred a year indefinitely will sell for two thousand at a five-percent discount rate, given certainty and unchanged conditions. The buyer who pays that price earns five percent. Nothing in the purchase has removed the original source of the payment: other people still need access to the location. It has capitalized the future receipts and paid them to the seller. The buyer’s yield and the scarcity income are two views of the same transaction at different points in time, not rival proofs that the rent does or does not exist.

Schwerhoff, Edenhofer and Fleurbaey, IMF WP/22/263 (2022), §2.1, p. 4 set out the capitalization relation; Mill, V.II §§5–6 discusses the purchase of future expectations. The illustration holds the annual site rent and discount rate constant.

Keynes imagined a way the rentier’s position might weaken without abolishing productive capital. In the concluding chapter of the General Theory, he distinguished the cost of replacing worn-out equipment, bearing risk and exercising skill from the bonus conferred by capital’s scarcity. If investment made reproducible capital sufficiently abundant, that scarcity return could diminish. His imagined change was gradual, supported by policies maintaining employment and investment. It did not mean that machines would last forever or that organizing production would become free. Those functions, and payment for them, survived the argument.

Keynes, The General Theory (1936), chapter 24, §II expressly retains allowances for wastage, obsolescence, risk, skill and supervision while imagining the disappearance of the scarcity return. His description is a proposed development under stated conditions, not a claim that it had already occurred.

Nor did Keynes supply a calendar on which this must happen. The same section considers how consumption, saving and the response to a lower interest rate could alter the speed of accumulation. Reaching full employment might be possible without the further investment needed to make capital abundant in his stronger sense. His social philosophy consequently involved choices about the resources one generation should devote to the next. To read the rentier’s disappearance as an automatic law would remove the policy and behavioral conditions on which his account depends. A society may reproduce productive equipment while continuing to maintain scarce titles to locations or other controlled opportunities.

Keynes, chapter 24, §II, especially its final three paragraphs distinguishes full employment from the further accumulation contemplated by the argument and leaves the scale and means of that accumulation for decision. Section III distinguishes guiding investment from state ownership of every productive asset.

The distribution among people adds another dimension. A rise in income paid to owners need not tell us whether those owners are becoming more concentrated, just as a common rate of return does not make unequal fortunes equal. Suppose one person owns one hundred and another nine hundred. If each reinvests a net five percent for one year, their holdings become one hundred and five and nine hundred and forty-five. The absolute gap grows from eight hundred to eight hundred and forty; their shares remain one-tenth and nine-tenths. If the first instead consumes the five while the second reinvests forty-five, the shares do change. Saving, transfers at death, taxes and differing returns belong in the account before the trend in ownership can be inferred.

Keynes, chapter 24, §§I–II connects saving, taxation and inheritance to the distribution of wealth. The two-person calculation here is an independent illustration, not Keynes’s model: proportional growth preserves shares, while unequal reinvestment can change them.

This is why the accusation that an economy rewards owning more than making requires a specified comparison. The return on a new invention during a period of uncertain development, the rent of an irreplaceable site, and a fee protected by a rule barring entry do not raise identical policy questions. Reducing the reward promised for an additional useful act can discourage that act. Reducing a payment attached to a fixed advantage need not. Opening a restricted market may remove the receipt by increasing the alternatives, while taxing a fixed rent may leave the receipt’s source in place and change its recipient. The object is to identify what sustains the income before choosing how to alter it.

Ricardo, chapter X separates improvements from pure land rent; George, VIII.3, section I and note 55 discusses distinct monopolies and the problem of separating their returns from production. George’s later note revises his treatment of patents and copyright, rather than treating every exclusive right as equivalent.

The institution that collects the income also matters. A public body can retain a land rent; a pension fund can distribute ownership income among workers; a small saver can buy a claim whose underlying return comes partly from restricted access. Neither the virtue of the recipient nor the breadth of ownership establishes that the restriction is efficient. Conversely, broad ownership can change who benefits even when the underlying price stays the same. The amount of rent, the way it is created and the distribution of its proceeds are separate dimensions. A reform that succeeds on one should say whether it changes the others.

George, VIII.2 proposes redirecting land rent while retaining possession and transfer; Mill, V.II §6 describes a public claim to part of the rent. The pension and saver cases are conceptual applications separating the source of income from its recipients.

The economy that lives on having is therefore neither proved innocent by the market price of its titles nor proved exhausted by the existence of them. Some titles organize contributions that must continue; some preserve an advantage while making the payment for it look like a cost nobody can question. The strongest inquiry follows the counterfactual. What disappears if the receipt is reduced: a useful investment, the care of an asset, a risk someone must bear, or simply the current holder’s claim on a surplus? And if a real contribution would disappear, could a different arrangement preserve it at a lower cost to everyone else?

Barkai, introduction and §I, pp. 2421–2426 makes the separation of capital costs and pure profits central to measurement. The counterfactual questions extend that distinction to institutional judgment; a measured residual alone does not answer them.

That returns us to the field with which the volume began. The soil’s advantage was not invented by the person holding it, though improvements and reliance could create claims of their own. As rent spread beyond the field, the same inquiry became less visible and more necessary. We have to look through the title to the service, the scarcity and the rule; then through the receipt to the person who ultimately keeps it. Only then can we say whether ownership is enabling production or collecting more than its continuation requires.

An ordinary yield on a purchased title can carry an extraordinary privilege underneath it.

An economy’s treatment of rent is not its destiny written in advance. It is an arrangement repeatedly renewed through investment, inheritance, competition and the rules of access. The next volume turns to the rate, where payment for the use of capital acquires another name and another set of justifications. The question travels with it. What is the payment needed to bring forth, and what is it permitted to take simply because the claim is already held? The argument remains open.
