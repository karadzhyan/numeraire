The Levy

in print · The Stock · plate vii

A fortune can confer command while producing little taxable cash. Reaching that capacity requires a choice among taxes on holding, income, gains and transfer, each with a different base and different practical limits. Following the levy from principle to assessment reveals why returns, valuation, debt, liquidity and enforceability belong in the same argument—and why the answer depends on the whole arrangement of public contribution.

The stock can confer what the flow does not reveal.The argument of this plate

A fortune can sustain its owner through a year in which very little reaches his income account. He can live in what he owns, borrow against it, sell a portion, or leave an appreciating asset untouched while its value enlarges the position from which he bargains. A tax confined to particular receipts will see some of these advantages and miss others. That is the opening case for a levy on wealth: the capacity to contribute may reside in the stock even when the measured flow is small. Yet a tax that reaches the stock also reaches people whose returns are poor, whose assets are difficult to sell, and whose valuations are uncertain. The question joins a judgment about what ownership owes to the commonwealth with a practical account of how that obligation can be assessed.

The base must be named before the burden can be judged. An annual net wealth tax charges a fraction of assets less allowable debts, usually above an exemption. A capital income tax charges income from ownership; a gains tax reaches a change in value under its own timing rules; a transfer tax reaches the passage of an estate or gift. A property tax may reach one asset class without deducting its owner's debts. These instruments overlap, but their occasions differ. A person may pay one without paying another, and the absence of a separate wealth tax tells us little until the rest of the system is known. The argument for reaching a fortune cannot be settled by counting the names of taxes imposed upon it.

The distinctions follow the OECD's The Role and Design of Net Wealth Taxes (2018), chapter 3, pp. 48–51. This is an analytical comparison of tax bases; the report's country rules belong to its historical reference period.

A small percentage of wealth can be a large percentage of the return. Imagine, solely for illustration, a person with $2 million of net assets facing an annual tax of 1 percent on the amount above $1 million. The bill is $10,000. If the assets yield $20,000 that year, it absorbs half the yield; if they yield $100,000, it absorbs a tenth. If they lose money but remain worth $2 million at the assessment date, the bill still falls due. These are nominal amounts, before any other tax, with the assessed stock held fixed to isolate the difference. A rate on the stock is therefore no ready equivalent of a single rate on actual income. The more successful investment can bear the smaller tax relative to its earnings.

The reason for tolerating that difference would have to concern possession itself. Two people with equal incomes may have unequal means of meeting a setback, financing a long project, or refusing a demand. The owner of a large reserve can consume principal as well as yield; the worker with no reserve must keep the flow arriving. If public contribution is meant to track that wider capacity, a stock can belong in the assessment. But the objective matters. Raising revenue, reducing entrenched ownership, and equalizing the treatment of investment returns are distinct purposes. A tax may serve one while making another harder. The appeal to ability to pay starts the argument; it does not choose the rate, the exemption, or the treatment of a loss.

The argument about benefits beyond income is developed in the OECD report, p. 53. Its discussion of low returns and liquidity, pp. 63–66, supplies the corresponding qualification. The numerical comparison above is invented, rather than an estimate of anyone's effective tax rate.

The competing instruments deserve to be compared on the same ground. If the problem is untaxed appreciation, a tax that reaches gains more consistently may address it more directly. If the problem is inherited advantage, the transfer is a distinct occasion on which to act. If the problem is the continuing power of a large holding, waiting for sale or death may leave the relevant condition untouched for decades. Each choice has costs of measurement, collection and behavior. Even the familiar objection that the same resources have been taxed before requires an account of the total burden and the reasons for imposing it: expenditure out of taxed earnings is routinely taxed again. The useful comparison is between complete arrangements, including their gaps, rather than between a troubled proposal and an imagined tax system without trouble.

Chapter 3 of the 2018 OECD report concludes that the case for an additional net wealth tax is weaker where capital income, gains and inheritances are already taxed comprehensively, and stronger where it serves as an imperfect substitute. The comparison is conditional on the surrounding system, pp. 70–71.

Valuation turns the judgment into an administrative undertaking. A quoted share supplies a price; an unlisted business requires an estimate of what its assets, prospects and obligations would command. A common valuation date makes the assessment reproducible without making the future certain. Averaging, periodic reassessment and a right to challenge the estimate can temper particular errors, while also creating choices about which fluctuations the tax will recognize. A rule that always favors the hardest asset to value rewards the form in which wealth is held. A rule that demands a sale to establish every value abandons the very stock it set out to reach. The workable question is what evidence can support a sufficiently consistent assessment at a tolerable cost.

The report’s complete valuation discussion is in chapter 4, pp. 85–87. It distinguishes quoted securities from unlisted holdings and discusses market estimates, reassessment intervals and consistency across asset classes. An assessment remains an estimate; uncertainty alone does not make all estimates equally defensible.

Exemptions can move the burden without moving the underlying wealth. Suppose another invented owner begins with $2 million in taxable investments, then borrows $1 million to buy an asset that the tax exempts. Gross assets rise to $3 million and debt to $1 million; net wealth remains $2 million. If all the debt is deductible against the taxable investments, the assessed base falls to $1 million. The owner has acquired a tax advantage through the treatment of the portfolio, even though borrowing has created no net wealth. Restricting deductions to debts associated with taxable assets can answer this particular route, but the example shows why the base and its deductions must be designed together. An exemption justified by one hardship can become a general shelter when its boundaries fail to follow its reason.

A sound valuation still does not put cash in the owner's hand. Consider a debt-free business interest assessed at $4 million, with only $5,000 of cash available for a levy of 1 percent above a $1 million exemption. The $30,000 obligation exceeds the cash by $25,000. That arithmetic establishes a payment problem; it does not establish either that the business is worthless or that the obligation is effortless to meet. Borrowing, a distribution or a sale may have real costs. Installments or deferral can separate the time of assessment from the time of payment, preserving a claim while acknowledging those costs. Their terms also matter: an indefinitely deferred, unenforced liability is different from an obligation that remains collectible. A permanent exemption and temporary accommodation answer different difficulties.

Both examples are invented and omit other taxes and transaction costs. The OECD treats debt deductibility on p. 85, liquidity on p. 64, and payment procedures on pp. 89–90. A low realized income can be relevant to payment capacity without exhausting the inquiry into wealth.

The reach of the tax depends on institutions as well as geography. Moving a security's custodian abroad, changing the owner's residence, and hiding beneficial ownership are different acts, with different consequences under any specified system. A physical asset may remain in place while the claims upon it cross borders. Reporting, valuation authority and cooperation determine how much of that structure the assessor can see. The possibility of avoidance gives a reason to examine the collection mechanism closely; it supplies no single estimate of revenue lost or investment deterred. Nor does the legal power to assess establish the practical power to collect. The state must be able to connect an identifiable owner, an assessable interest and an enforceable obligation.

The OECD separates capital movement, residence changes, reporting and enforcement in chapter 3, pp. 66–70, and cautions against treating observed departures as a simple measure of tax causation. The earlier Collected Base frames the choice of what to tax; the Margin Tax shows why a levy on fixed land rent has a different incidence problem from a levy on a broad portfolio.

A tax on possession must answer for what possession confers.

The stock is a defensible object of public judgment because it can confer an enduring capacity that a year's receipts do not reveal. Whether an annual levy is the best means of acting on that judgment depends on what the rest of the system already reaches, what the assessment can measure, and what the collection rules can sustain. Those conditions give the argument substance. They also keep it open to remedies besides a single named tax. A commonwealth need not mistake a quiet income account for an empty fortune; neither can it turn a moral conclusion into a collectible bill merely by writing a percentage beside the stock. The argument remains open.
