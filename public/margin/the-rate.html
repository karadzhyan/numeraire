The Rate

in print · № III · 2 of 12 plates

The interest rate is the price of time: the measure of how much more a dollar today is worth than a dollar tomorrow, and the number on which the whole architecture of finance — every loan, bond, and mortgage, the cost of a nation's debt — is raised. This volume anatomizes it. It weighs the theories that make the rate a fact of nature — the reward for abstinence, the premium of impatience and productivity — against the recognition that the price of time is set, not given: a number a central bank moves, and the verdict of a contest, older than economics, between those who can wait and those who must pay for not waiting.

The question of the volume the rate looks like the natural discount the future must suffer — but who decides what an hour of waiting is worth, and in whose favor is the price of time set?
The argument

Of all the prices in this table, none is at once so abstract and so consequential as the price of time. The interest rate is what a dollar today fetches when lent until tomorrow, and what a dollar promised for tomorrow is worth if claimed today; it is the rate at which the future is discounted into the present and the present compounded into the future. On it the entire edifice of finance is raised — every loan and bond and mortgage, every weighing of a cost borne now against a benefit reaped later, every valuation of a company or a claim. To move it is to move the worth of all these at once. And yet, for all its reach, the rate is a single number, and the question this volume presses is the oldest and simplest one about it: where does that number come from, and who decides what an hour of waiting is worth?

Why the future should trade at a discount at all has been answered, by the classical tradition, from the side of nature and necessity. Men are impatient: they prefer their enjoyments now, and must be tempted to defer them. One who lends abstains from the present use of his wealth, and interest, Nassau Senior held, is the just reward of that abstinence, as wages are the reward of labor. And present goods are genuinely more fruitful than future ones, for Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk argued that production which takes the long way round — building tools before bread, machines before cloth — yields more in the end, so that command over resources now is worth a premium over the same command later. Irving Fisher gathered these threads into the modern theory: the rate of interest is the price that reconciles impatience, the preference for the present, with opportunity, the return that present resources can earn if invested — the meeting point of human desire and the world's productivity across time.

On these accounts the rate is about as natural as anything the science treats. It springs from the deep facts of the human relation to time and from the technical fruitfulness of capital, not from anyone's will; it is discovered, like the freezing point of water, rather than chosen. A positive rate, on this view, is no imposition but a reading of reality — the price the world attaches to waiting, written into the order of things. There would be interest, the classical economist could say, on a desert island or in a state of nature, wherever a present good could be turned to future advantage and wherever men preferred sooner to later. The rate is the price of time, and time's price, like time itself, seems to belong to nature and not to us.

But the appearance of nature does not survive a closer look. John Maynard Keynes denied that the rate rewards waiting at all: saving alone, he argued, earns nothing; what earns interest is the surrender of liquidity — the giving up of money's unique safety and flexibility — so that the rate is the price of parting with money, governed by the demand to hold it against the supply, a monetary phenomenon and not a real one. Knut Wicksell had already split the rate in two: a natural rate, which would balance saving and investment and hold prices steady, and the market rate that banks in fact charge, which can stray from it and drive a cumulative inflation or slump until the two are forced back together. But the natural rate, it emerged, cannot be observed; it can only be guessed at. What actually rules an economy is the market rate — and the market rate is set, by a banking system and, at its center, by a central bank. The price of time, on inspection, is not read from nature. It is made.

And what is made by some is borne by others. A central bank that raises the rate enriches those who hold claims and squeezes those who owe; one that lowers it does the reverse, punishing the saver to relieve the borrower and lifting the price of every asset along the way. There is no setting of the rate that is neutral among the parties, for the price of time is also the rate at which the future is traded for the present, and every level of it favors someone. A price is a verdict on a hidden contest, mistaken for a fact of nature; and the interest rate, presented as the objective discount the future must suffer, is in truth the administered outcome of a contest — between creditor and debtor, lender and borrower, the patient and the pressed — over who shall command resources now and who shall wait. The power to set the price of time is among the greatest in any economy, and it is wielded, not found.

That the price of time is a matter of power, and not merely of nature, is no modern discovery; it is the oldest intuition about interest there is. For most of recorded history, to lend at interest was condemned as usury: Aristotle pronounced money barren and its breeding unnatural, the medieval Church forbade the taking of interest outright, and the prohibition, however freely evaded, rested on a conviction that to charge a man for the time he needs to repay is to feed upon his necessity rather than to earn by one's own labor. Jeremy Bentham answered, in his defense of usury, that time is truly valuable and the free bargain over its price no business of the law. The quarrel runs unsettled still. But it was never merely technical, for beneath it lay a question of power: the lender's leverage over the borrower's need. A price is a verdict on a hidden contest, mistaken for a fact of nature; and the rate that wears the look of patience rewarded is the visible settlement of who possesses the means to wait, and who must pay for lacking them.

None of this renders the rate arbitrary, or the real theories idle, and the volume will not pretend otherwise. Impatience is a genuine fact of the human heart; capital employed in roundabout ways is genuinely productive; a society that consumed its whole substance and saved nothing would build no future, and the rate that coaxes some to wait answers to a real need. The central bank's power over the rate is itself hedged — by the discipline of inflation, by the flight of capital, by the expectations of those it would steer. The price of time is neither a pure fact of nature nor a pure act of will, but a thing made at the meeting of the two: shaped by real impatience and real productivity, and set, within the room those forces leave, by a power that chooses. The volume's work is to mark that meeting precisely — to separate what the rate genuinely reflects about the scarcity of present resources from what it conceals about the contest over their command, and the policy that settles it.

The twelve plates follow the price of time from its simplest statement to its gravest stakes. They set out the discount itself — why tomorrow's dollar is worth less than today's — and the usury that asks whether interest is the just price of waiting or the measure of power over need; the abstinence by which the classics justified the reward of the saver, the impatience through which Fisher reconciled desire with opportunity, and the detour by which Böhm-Bawerk found interest in the productivity of capital; the hoard from which Keynes drew the rate, and the natural rate by which Wicksell divided the real from the monetary; the lever by which a central bank moves the whole architecture of interest, the bond in which the rate is made visible as the price of a promise, and the zero below which the price of time was once thought it could not fall; the compound through which interest transfers, without rest, from those who owe to those who own; and, at the last, the future, and what rate, if any, may justly weigh the welfare of those not yet born. Other volumes border this one: the money the rate is the price of lending, anatomized in the volume the table is named for; the stock, which is a flow of income capitalized at the rate; the cycle, in which the rate figures as both cause and cure; and the premium, the price of risk that the next of the prices will take up where this one, the price of time, leaves off. The interest rate is the price of time, and the question of who sets it, and in whose favor, is the question the volume holds open. The argument remains open.

Volume III, The Rate, opens with two plates in print — The Discount and The Usury, the price of time and the ancient contest over who sets it. The third element of the table, and the twenty-first of its volumes to appear; it joins The Wage and The Rent among the prices — the wage the price of labor, the rent the return to ownership, and now the rate the price of time itself. It borders the volume the table is named for, The Money, for the rate is the price of lending money; The Stock, since any fortune is a flow of income capitalized at the rate; and The Cycle, in which the rate figures as both the cause of the slump and its attempted cure. The thesis is that the rate, which wears the look of a natural discount the future must suffer, is in truth set — a number a central bank moves, and the verdict of a contest between those who can wait and those who must pay for not waiting.
