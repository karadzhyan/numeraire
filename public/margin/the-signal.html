As ii

The Signal

in print · The Asymmetry · plate ii

If the lemon market fails because the good cannot tell itself from the bad, the cure is a way for quality to prove itself — and Michael Spence showed what such proof requires. A signal works only if it is costly, and differentially so: dear enough that the genuine article will pay it and the impostor will not. This plate sets out the signal — the diploma, the warranty, the costly display — and the strange truth that the expense is the message.

Promise, large promise, is the soul of an advertisement. Samuel Johnson, The Idler (1759)

A word means nothing when everyone has an interest in saying it. The good worker and the bad both claim to be diligent; the sound firm and the shaky both call their product reliable; and because the claim costs nothing and serves everyone, it persuades no one. What the listener trusts instead is the thing that would be too expensive to fake — the action only the genuine article would find worth taking. This is a signal: not a statement of one's quality but a demonstration of it, made credible precisely by its cost. The diploma, the warranty, the unhedged guarantee, the willingness to be held to one's word — each works not by what it says but by what only the real thing could afford to do.

Recall the impasse at which the market for lemons left us. The seller of a good car wishes nothing more than to reveal its quality, for which a buyer would gladly pay; but he has no way to do so, since whatever he says in its favor the seller of a lemon will say in favor of his, and the buyer, knowing this, discounts both equally. The honest assurance and the dishonest are, on their face, identical — words, costless to utter and therefore worthless as evidence, what the theorists of communication call cheap talk. The good seller's problem is not that he lacks something to say but that saying it changes nothing; his quality is real, and invisible, and his protestations of it are drowned in the indistinguishable chorus of everyone making the same claim. What he needs is not a better assertion but a different kind of act — one that the lemon-seller, for all his willingness to say anything, would find it against his interest to copy.

Cheap talk is the technical name, from a model of Vincent Crawford and Joel Sobel, for communication that is costless and non-binding — words that commit the speaker to nothing and cost him nothing to utter. Its lesson is that when interests diverge, cheap talk conveys little or nothing, because the speaker will say whatever serves him regardless of the truth, and the listener, anticipating this, discounts it. The good seller's honest assurance is cheap talk, indistinguishable from the lemon-seller's dishonest one; which is exactly why a credible signal must be its opposite — costly, and so unfakeable.

Michael Spence, in work of the early 1970s that completed the edifice Akerlof had begun and earned the two a shared Nobel, identified precisely what such an act must be. A signal can separate the good from the bad only if it is costly, and — this is the crux — differentially costly, cheaper for the high type to send than for the low. Then, and only then, will the high type find it worthwhile and the low type not, so that the mere fact of the signal's being sent reveals the sender's quality, as words never could. Spence's illustration, which has shadowed the economics of education ever since, was the academic degree. Suppose schooling teaches nothing of use to an employer, but that the abler student finds it less arduous, less costly in effort and in risk of failure, to obtain a given credential. Then the able will acquire the degree and the less able will not — not because it has made them productive but because it is cheaper for the productive to get; and an employer, unable to observe ability directly, can read it off the diploma. The signal works without conveying a single fact about its own content, by the sheer logic of who can afford to send it.

Spence's 1973 paper Job Market Signaling modeled education as a pure signal, assuming it adds nothing to productivity, the better to isolate signaling from content. The striking result is that a separating equilibrium can exist in which the able buy the credential and the less able do not, sustained entirely by the differential cost — the abler facing lower effort and lower risk of failure. Real education surely both teaches and signals; the value of the pure model is to show that part of the wage premium to schooling may be a return to signaling rather than to skill, a possibility that has occupied labor economists since.

Once the principle is grasped, signals are seen to be everywhere, quietly doing the work that words cannot. The manufacturer who offers a long and unconditional warranty signals the soundness of his product, for a warranty is cheap to give on goods that rarely fail and ruinous on goods that fail often, so that only a confident maker will extend it. The firm that spends lavishly on advertising may be signaling quality by the spending itself, as the economist Phillip Nelson argued — not by what the advertisement says, which is cheap talk, but by the conspicuous outlay, which a fly-by-night seller expecting no repeat custom would never recoup and so would not undertake. The shopkeeper who builds an expensive storefront, the professional who frames his credentials, the borrower who pledges collateral, the seller who stakes a costly reputation on every sale: each spends, visibly, in a way that would not pay unless his quality were real. The expense is not incidental to the message. The expense is the message.

Phillip Nelson's insight, from the 1970s, was that advertising can signal quality even when its content is uninformative, through the expenditure alone. A firm selling a good that buyers will repurchase if satisfied profits from advertising only if the repeat custom materializes — which it will only if the product is good; so heavy advertising is a credible sign of a quality the firm expects to be vindicated by experience. The argument distinguishes search goods, whose quality is checkable before purchase, from experience goods, known only after, and explains why the latter are advertised in the costly, content-light way they are.

This is the feature that makes signaling so counterintuitive, and so revealing. In the ordinary way of thinking, the cost of a signal is waste — money or effort that buys nothing of substance, that a more efficient world would dispense with. But the cost is not a regrettable accident of the signal; it is the whole of what makes it work. A signal that were cheap would at once be imitated by every pretender, and so would cease to separate anyone from anyone, conveying nothing; only the costly signal, too dear for the impostor, can be believed. Biology discovered the same logic, in what is called the handicap principle: the peacock's preposterous tail, useless and metabolically ruinous, is credible as a sign of fitness precisely because only a truly fit bird could afford to grow and drag it. Veblen had seen it in the human world long before, in the conspicuous consumption by which the rich signal their standing through expenditure whose very point is to be visibly beyond the reach of others. To signal is, in a sense, to burn money where it can be seen — and the fire is the proof.

The handicap principle, proposed by the biologist Amotz Zahavi in 1975 and long resisted, holds that costly, fitness-reducing ornaments are stable signals precisely because the unfit cannot bear their cost. The peacock's tail and the gazelle's conspicuous leap before a predator are the stock examples. Veblen's conspicuous consumption, set out in 1899, is the human economic counterpart — expenditure undertaken not for use but for the display of the means to waste, which only the genuinely wealthy can sustain. Costly signaling, it turns out, is a principle of life at large, not of markets alone.

And now the prices that signaling commands array themselves in the familiar disguise. A price is a verdict on a hidden contest, mistaken for a fact of nature — and the wage a degree commands, the premium a trusted brand earns, the higher sum a warranted good fetches, present themselves as the market's reward to genuine quality, as if the credentialed worker were simply worth more and the price were nature's recognition of the fact. But signaling tells a subtler and more disquieting story. The reward attaches not to quality itself, which remains as invisible as ever, but to the capacity to signal it — to having sent the costly sign — and that capacity is the product of a signaling equilibrium, a contingent arrangement of who can afford to prove what, and not a fact of nature at all. The graduate's premium is, in part, the return not to anything the schooling instilled but to the diploma's power to separate; the brand's markup is the return to the costly history that makes the brand believed. What looks like the price of quality is the price of provable quality once again — but now we see that even the proof is bought, and that its cost is folded into every figure the market quotes.

The distinction is between the return to a skill and the return to the signal of a skill. If a credential raised productivity, its wage premium would be the market paying for the added output — a return to human capital. If the credential only signals a pre-existing ability, its premium is a return to the signal, a payment for sorting, not for any value the credential created. Empirically the two are hard to separate, and the truth is surely a mixture; but the signaling share, whatever its size, rewards the costly act of proving quality rather than quality itself, and would vanish if quality could be shown more cheaply.

The objection presses itself with unusual force here, and from two directions at once. The first is waste: if the signal conveys quality without creating it — if the degree, in the pure model, teaches nothing — then the vast resources poured into sending it are a dead loss, an arms race in which each credentials harder to stay visible above the rest, all running faster only to keep their places, while the credential required for a given job ratchets ever upward in the inflation pure signaling breeds. The second is justice: a signal sorts by the cost of sending it, and if that cost is lower for the already advantaged — who can afford the long schooling, the unpaid internship, the polished accent and manner — then the signal may track privilege as faithfully as merit, and dress an inherited head start in the colors of demonstrated worth. Faced with this, one may ask whether signaling is a solution at all, or a wasteful and regressive sham. The reply does not deny the charge; it absorbs it. For where signaling is wasteful, or where it launders advantage into apparent merit, that is no refutation of the thesis but its sharpest confirmation: it shows beyond doubt that the credential's reward is the artifact of an institution — a signaling game with its own costs and its own beneficiaries — and not the wage that nature pays to worth. To see the waste and the injustice is precisely to see the price for the contingent, contestable thing it is.

Two pathologies of signaling have drawn particular attention. The first is the credentialing arms race, or credential inflation: as more candidates acquire a signal, its power to distinguish erodes and the level required rises, so that ever more schooling is needed to secure work that once required less — effort expended only to stay in place. The second is the regressive sorting that results when the cost of a signal is lower for the privileged, who can finance the long education or the unpaid internship that the signal demands; the signal then certifies advantage as if it were merit. Both are reasons the signal, though it answers the lemon, is no unmixed good.

So the signal completes what the lemon began. The first plate showed that in a market of hidden quality the good cannot reveal itself, and the trade decays; the second shows that it can reveal itself after all, but only by spending — by burning resource to prove what a costless word could never establish — and that the price which results reflects the signaling arrangement as much as the quality beneath it. Between them the two plates redraw the meaning of quality in a market: it is never simply present, exerting a natural pull on price, but must be made visible through costly signs, so that what the market pays for is always quality-as-signaled, the thing wrapped in the expensive proof of itself. The lemon is the wound and the signal the first suture; but the suture, we have seen, leaves a scar of its own — the cost of the signal, the inflation of the credential, the advantage it grants those best placed to send it. The market does not get its information for free, and the bill appears, disguised, in every price it sets.

The deeper conceptual shift is that quality, as a market magnitude, ceases to be a property of the good and becomes a property of the good-plus-its-proof. A reliable car with no way to demonstrate its reliability commands the price of an unreliable one; the same car with a credible warranty commands more — yet the car is unchanged, and what is paid for is the proof. In a world of asymmetric information there is no such thing as an unsignaled quality fetching its true price; quality reaches the price only through the costly channel of its signal, and the cost of that channel is borne, invisibly, by buyer and seller alike.

For here is where the second of the volume's contests comes to rest. A price is a verdict on a hidden contest, mistaken for a fact of nature; and in the signal the contest is one of revelation — over who can credibly show what they are, and at what cost. The verdict it returns, the credentialed wage and the branded premium, is read as the market's measure of quality, as though quality declared itself and the price merely recorded the declaration; but quality declares nothing, and the figure is in truth the toll of the signaling game — a game with its own price of entry, its own waste, and its own winners, which a different arrangement of screening or certification or law could redraw entirely. The diploma's wage is not what the able are worth; it is what it costs to prove that one is able, in a world that has settled on the diploma as the proof. Change the proof, and the price changes, though no one's quality has moved an inch. The seller can now be heard — but only because he has paid to speak, and the price he commands is the price of the speaking.

The thought experiment that fixes the point: imagine a cheap, perfectly reliable test of the quality a credential now signals — a costless certification of the ability the diploma stands for. The credential's wage premium, in the part that was a return to signaling, would collapse, for the able could prove their ability without the costly schooling, and employers would no longer pay for the diploma's sorting. The workers' qualities would be exactly what they were; only the price of proving them would have fallen, and the wage with it. That the premium depends on the cost of the proof, and not on the quality alone, is the whole of the signal's lesson and the volume's thesis in little.

A signal is credible only because it is costly — and so the market, which could not hear quality speak for free, hears it speak at last, but charges, in every price, for the proof.

With the signal the volume's opening pair is complete. The lemon and the signal — the failure and its first answer — together set the frame for all that follows: that in a market of unequal knowledge, both the breakdown and the remedy are made things, institutions and not facts of nature, and the price between buyer and seller records neither quality plainly nor its plain absence, but the state of the long contest to reveal it. What remains of the volume is further answers, each with its own ingenuity and its own cost — the screen by which the uninformed make the types sort themselves, the seal by which a trusted third party vouches, the mandate by which the law drags the truth into the open. Each is another way of letting quality be known where it could not be; each leaves its mark on the price. The good can be told from the bad, in the end — but never for nothing, and never quite for certain. The argument remains open.

The second plate of Volume XII, The Asymmetry, and the answer to the first: Michael Spence's theory of signaling, the means by which hidden quality reveals itself when words will not serve. A signal is a costly action — costlier to fake than to make truthfully — so that only the genuine type will undertake it: the education the able find worth the candle and the rest do not, the warranty only a sound product can afford, the brand and the advertisement built at an expense that confidence in quality alone repays. The counterintuitive heart of the matter, that the cost is not a flaw but the very source of credibility; the master thesis, that the wage a credential commands and the premium a brand earns look like the price of quality but are the return to the power to prove it; and the costs of the signal — the credential arms race, the waste, the advantage it grants those who can most afford to send it. The plate completes the pair begun by The Lemon, the problem to which the signal replies.
