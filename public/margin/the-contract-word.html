The Word

in print · The Contract · plate vi

A written agreement can remain in the drawer while the parties adjust their dealings around it. Macaulay’s study reveals both informal cooperation and the continuing uses of formal terms. A constructed relationship shows when future business can restrain a present temptation, and why the relevant loss depends on available alternatives. Trust, reputation and legal position then enter the same account of what makes a word worth keeping.

The next deal gives today’s word something to lose.The argument of this plate

The document can remain in a drawer while the agreement changes every day. A delivery is delayed, a specification needs correction, an order becomes less useful than it seemed when placed. The parties may settle these matters without opening the clause that someone once drafted for them. They know how the work is done, have something to lose by making the other party’s work impossible, and expect to meet again after the present difficulty passes. The word exchanged between them carries that history and that expectation. To understand its force, one must look both at the terms that could be invoked and at the relationship in which invoking them is only one available choice.

Macaulay’s inquiry found a practical distinction between planning an exchange and insisting on its legal terms. His 1963 preliminary study drew on interviews with businesspeople and lawyers, principally around manufacturing firms with Wisconsin plants. It found substantial formal planning, especially for important transactions, alongside routine dealings in which operating personnel gave little attention to standard terms. Adjustments and disputes were often handled with still less reference to legal sanctions. Some parties negotiated a solution even when a detailed agreement addressed the problem. The finding gives the earlier Handshake its force: the existence of a written instrument does not tell us which mechanisms ordinarily sustain performance. Macaulay’s account also preserves the formal planning that a story of business conducted wholly without documents would leave out.

Stewart Macaulay, “Non-Contractual Relations in Business: A Preliminary Study” (1963). The University of Wisconsin repository’s reprint has its own pagination: study scope and definition, pp. 1–4; planning and adjustment, pp. 5–12. Macaulay expressly describes the findings as preliminary and notes possible sampling bias. The study does not establish a present-day frequency for all business contracts.

The continuing relationship can make a concession worth more than the immediate claim surrendered. A seller may accept a cancellation because forcing an unwanted delivery would damage future trade. A buyer may accommodate a genuine production difficulty because an able supplier is costly to replace. Macaulay records repeat orders, reputation, personal contacts, internal pressures and bargaining positions among the forces supporting such accommodations. These are not all the same mechanism. A salesperson may preserve a relationship because the firm values it, because his own work depends on it, or because he regards the adjustment as decent. The practical agreement can be held together by several motives at once, with their relative importance becoming visible when those motives cease to point in the same direction.

The prospect of future dealings supplies one mechanism that can be stated exactly. Consider a newly constructed relationship in which cooperation gives each party four per round, measured as a gain over its outside alternative. A unilateral betrayal gives the betrayer ten in that round and is observed without error. Thereafter the parties can withdraw, each receiving zero; assume neither would profit by resuming unilaterally after that withdrawal. With each next round weighted at three-quarters of the present one, continued cooperation is worth sixteen. Betrayal is worth ten. The additional six available today is smaller than the twelve of future gains that would be lost. If the weight falls to one-half, cooperation is worth only eight and betrayal becomes preferable. The word is supported by the value it puts in danger.

The figures are invented. Cooperation has present value 4/(1 − δ); a deviation gives 10 followed by zero. Cooperation is strictly preferred when δ > 3/5, with indifference at 3/5. At δ = ¾ the values are 16 and 10; at δ = ½ they are 8 and 10. Perfect observation, credible withdrawal and no alternative continuation gain are stipulated. For the underlying continuing-exchange reasoning, see Schelling, “An Essay on Bargaining” (1956), § IV, pp. 300–301.

The future that disciplines the bargain is the future a departure would actually forfeit. Suppose four is now the gross per-round benefit of staying, but another counterparty would provide three after departure. At the same three-quarter weight, the future advantage of this particular relationship is only three: the discounted stream of one-unit differences. It cannot deter a current extra gain of six. A promise of long acquaintance is consequently less informative than an account of what each party would lose by breaking faith. The same discipline must be applied to both parties. A buyer who expects to depart soon may have little future business to place behind an informal assurance, even while the supplier remains deeply dependent on its continued orders. Repetition creates an opportunity for enforcement; it does not distribute that opportunity equally.

The interpretation of a disappointment matters as much as its size. The numerical relationship assumed that betrayal could be seen without error. In practice a defective shipment can reflect neglect, a supplier’s mistake or a difficulty neither party could prevent. Ending every relationship after any bad outcome can destroy valuable cooperation; forgiving every outcome can leave a profitable deviation untouched. Information, explanation, inspection and a chance to cure can help distinguish the cases. They also give the parties a way to agree what the promise meant. Macaulay describes trade practices and shared technical understandings filling parts of that space. Familiarity can make an adjustment easier because the parties know the work, though familiarity alone cannot ensure that their unspoken assumptions coincide.

Macaulay (1963), repository-reprint pp. 4–9 and 12–15, distinguishes tacit agreement from inconsistent unexpressed assumptions and discusses specifications, trade customs and nonlegal sanctions. The observation-error argument here is an extension of the constructed relationship; it is not an estimated finding or a theorem that any particular forgiveness rule is optimal.

The next deal can keep today’s word only to the extent that today’s breach would put the next deal at risk.

Reputation can carry a consequence beyond the particular pair when information about conduct travels. A seller who will never see this customer again may still care what later customers will hear. Macaulay’s account describes business gossip, credit reports and judgments about demanding buyers as well as unreliable sellers. That wider audience changes the thing a party risks losing. Its discipline depends on identification, communication and a credible connection between conduct and future treatment. A false accusation can damage a name; a concealed failure can escape it; a newcomer may lack the history that established firms can offer. The possibility of reputational enforcement reaches beyond repeated dealings with the same person, without making every market an all-seeing community.

Macaulay (1963), repository-reprint pp. 13–15, describes both bilateral and wider reputational pressures in the business relationships studied. The distinctions concerning inaccurate reports, concealed conduct and newcomers state conditions on the mechanism; no claim about their prevalence is made. Schelling’s discussion of audiences and effective promises appears at pp. 299–300.

The written terms can work inside this relationship even when neither party reads them aloud. Macaulay expressly notes that a legal position can affect negotiations without legal rights or litigation being mentioned. A document can also communicate the bargain to production staff, control concessions by a salesperson and coordinate departments whose memories and priorities differ. Its use begins before a dispute and can continue beneath a negotiated accommodation. Conversely, a term imposed by a powerful customer may give the supplier little protection when cooperation ends. The relative weight of writing and relationship therefore depends on what each supplies: evidence, coordination, a credible claim, an exit, or a reason to make the next concession.

Macaulay (1963), repository-reprint p. 12 on legal positions influencing negotiation; pp. 16–19 on communication within firms, significant risks, terminated relationships, occupational roles and relative bargaining power. These historical observations do not determine the legal effect of a particular present-day contract. The distinct functions of planning, potential sanction and actual litigation should not be collapsed.

A relationship that makes cooperation durable can also make objection costly. The threat of losing future business may induce faithful performance, but it can also make a weaker party accept a demand it would otherwise contest. Calling the arrangement trusting does not resolve that distribution. One must ask who can replace whom, who has invested in assets useful only here, and whether the threat of departure is believable on both sides. Nor does a long relationship guarantee a permanent future. Where the simple model has a known final round and no other sanction or motive, its future discipline disappears in that round and the usual backward reasoning can undo the earlier cooperation. A real relationship may have other supports; naming them is part of explaining why it lasts.

The word binds through a combination whose proportions the document alone cannot reveal. What has been written can coordinate and protect; what the parties know about one another can make adjustment possible; what they expect to lose can make a promise worth keeping. These forces may reinforce each other or pull apart. The task is to identify the working arrangement, rather than to count pages and declare either law or trust the victor. The hardest test often arrives after one party has made an investment that cannot readily move elsewhere. Then the prospect of staying may be a source of cooperation and a source of leverage at once. The next plate asks what becomes of the word when that exposure can be used to reopen the price.
