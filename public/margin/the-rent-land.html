The Land

in print · The Rent · plate vii

The title joins together the ground, the work done on it and the value created beyond its boundary. Paine’s distinction between natural inheritance and improvements leads through the rights an enclosure changes to the investment a secure claim can sustain. The argument then asks how possession, common use and a purchaser’s reliance bear on the right to collect the rent.

A title can protect what its holder has made without settling the claim to what no one made.The argument of this plate, after Paine, Ricardo, Schlager and Ostrom

A field can be drained, a wall built, a house repaired. Someone must pay for the work, and a system that lets strangers take the finished thing without payment will find fewer people willing to begin it. But the field also has a location. It occupies a piece of the earth that the builder did not make, and its usefulness may owe as much to the river, the road and the neighboring town as to anything done inside its fence. The difficulty begins when the title gathers these different claims into one. A payment for the owner’s work, a return on the money advanced and a charge for permission to stand on the ground arrive in the same envelope. The Land asks what remains of the justification when we open it.

The earlier inquiry into the soil explained the differential: better ground can yield a surplus over the ground barely worth using. That explanation tells us why a tenant will pay, and why the payment can survive the owner’s complete inactivity. It does not tell us who should receive it. Nor does the distinction run neatly between farm and city. A shop’s advantage may lie in the people passing its door; a farm’s in soil, drainage and access mixed together. The analytical task is to separate the site from the improvements without pretending that the world has already separated them for the assessor. A plot can be physically fixed while the services produced on it change considerably.

Ricardo, Principles, chapter X (1821 third edition)

Thomas Paine made that separation the hinge of Agrarian Justice. His starting proposition was a common natural inheritance: no person had made the earth, and birth into a cultivated society should not extinguish the claim of those who found its ground already possessed. Yet Paine also defended the value added by cultivation, including improvements inherited or purchased. His argument was not that the person who built a house had no claim to the house. It was that the house did not settle the claim to everything beneath it. Cultivation had joined two things in practice; justice still had to distinguish them in thought.

Paine, Agrarian Justice (Philadelphia printing, 1797), pp. 7–11 distinguishes the common natural inheritance from improvements. His account of the natural state is part of his argument, not a necessary historical premise of the distinction made here.

Paine’s proposed remedy also matters. He did not require every new generation to tear down the fences and start dividing acres again. He proposed a fund, collected as property passed at death, to provide a payment at adulthood and an income in old age. Land supplied one justification; his treatment of personal property supplied another, resting on society’s contribution to accumulation. The proposal preserved possession while making it answerable to a wider claim. One may contest his assessment of what is owed without missing the institutional possibility: an owner’s authority to use and improve a site need not include an unconditional right to keep every part of its value.

Paine, pp. 11–17 and 24–25 sets out the fund, its collection at death and the distinct social argument concerning personal property. This is his historical proposal, rather than a description of an annual land-value tax.

The fence itself therefore needs closer inspection. It may keep a stranger from taking a crop, protect an orchard long enough for the trees to bear, or bar a village from a pasture its households had previously used. These acts need not have the same consequence merely because each is called enclosure. Before asking whether property has been created, one must ask whose rights existed before the change, which have been strengthened, and which have disappeared. A gain in output does not by itself identify the people who gain. A transfer can increase the product while depriving former users of a claim they depended upon; compensation is a separate action, not something contained in the word improvement.

Schlager and Ostrom, “Property-Rights Regimes and Natural Resources” (1992), §§I–II, pp. 249–254 distinguish access, withdrawal, management, exclusion and alienation. Its resource-rights analysis supports asking which powers change; it is not an estimate of the effects of any particular historical enclosure.

Consider an imagined parcel whose existing use yields fifty a year. Drainage costing two hundred would add thirty a year indefinitely. At a five-percent discount rate, with certainty and no maintenance cost, the addition is worth six hundred; the improvement creates four hundred more than it costs. If the improver can retain the added return, undertaking the work is worthwhile. If the added return can immediately be taken by someone else, that calculation no longer gives the improver a reason to pay. Security over the improvement has a clear function. But the calculation does not require the improver to receive the original fifty as well. A secure right to the added thirty and a separate charge for the site can preserve precisely the incentive the example needs.

Ricardo, chapter X distinguishes compensation for the original land from compensation for capital put into it. The figures here are hypothetical: 30 ÷ .05 − 200 = 400. They assume an indefinite flow and a securely retained improvement return.

Change the assumptions and the case becomes more revealing. If only ten a year can be retained from the improvement, its present value is two hundred and the net gain is zero. If a new annual charge of twenty attaches to the drainage itself, it has the same effect in this example. If the twenty is instead charged for the site whether drained or undrained, it cancels when the owner compares the two uses; the improvement still adds thirty. The relevant distinction is between rules that take away the return to an additional act and rules that allocate a return already available without that act.

Ricardo, chapter X supplies the land-versus-improvements distinction. The comparison holds the site charge fixed across uses, below the existing site return; it does not assume that every tax described as a land tax satisfies those conditions.

Nor must security always take the form of one person’s unrestricted, transferable title. Schlager and Ostrom distinguish the authority to enter, harvest, manage, exclude and sell. Their framework allows a community to exclude outsiders while its members share use and management, and it allows substantial investment incentives without every member possessing every right. Common property is consequently different from a resource to which nobody can regulate access. The pertinent question is whether the arrangement can sustain the work and restraint required, resolve disagreements and keep its promises. A title can help do those things. Its name alone cannot demonstrate that it does them better than every alternative.

Schlager and Ostrom, §§II–IV, pp. 250–257 treat the rights separately and examine investment incentives under different bundles. The authors expressly decline to equate common property with open access or to reserve long-term investment exclusively to holders of full ownership rights.

A purchase adds another claim without removing the first. The present owner may have paid the full market value, using wages saved over years. That payment is real; it gives a reform consequences for this person that differ from the consequences for an original recipient of a grant. But the payment went to a previous holder. It did not create the location or reimburse every person whose presence makes it useful. A succession of purchases can establish good faith and reliance while carrying an old distribution forward. To recognize one is not to have answered the other. This is why the design of a transition belongs beside the argument about the right itself.

Mill, Principles, V.II §§5–6 (Ashley edition, 1909) considers both the public claim to increments and the value purchasers have paid for existing income and future expectations. His treatment makes the transition a distinct question.

Land is the clearest place to see this because a person cannot opt out of location. Farming, sleeping, making and meeting all require somewhere to happen. Yet even here the supply that matters must be specified. More floors can be built on a site; access can improve; a different district can become a substitute; regulation can permit or forbid uses. The quantity of earth is not the quantity of housing, and the existence of scarcity does not prove that a particular restriction is necessary. The defense of ownership is strongest when it explains the investment, coordination or stewardship a right sustains. It is weakest when the right to exclude is offered as its own justification.

Ricardo, chapter X keeps the return on produced improvements separate from land rent; Schlager and Ostrom, §IV, pp. 256–257 make the incentive consequences depend on the actual bundle of rights. The application to floors and alternative sites follows that distinction.

The rent of land is therefore a measure of an advantage and the record of an allocation. It can tell us what others will pay to use a place under the existing rules. It cannot, by repeating that price, tell us which rules ought to exist. The owner may have supplied something deserving protection, bought something deserving a careful transition, and received something whose source lies outside the boundary of the deed. The serious argument begins by keeping all three in view.

A title can protect what its holder has made without settling the claim to what no one made.

The earth was made by no one; the arrangements through which we inhabit it are made and maintained by people. Their justification must account for both the work they make possible and the claims they exclude. That leaves room for possession, improvement and exchange, but it also leaves room for a community to ask what it is giving away when it assigns the ground. The payment at the gate is visible. The rights on either side of it remain the subject of the inquiry. The argument remains open.
