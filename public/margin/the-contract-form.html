The Form

in print · The Contract · plate ix

The form saves the work of negotiating a common transaction and concentrates the authority to write its terms. Historical evidence about software licenses and explicit comparisons of reading and warranty costs separate inattention from indifference. Notice, unconscionability and reasonable expectations then reveal distinct legal questions about what was agreed and what the agreement can accomplish.

A common form still has an author.The argument of this plate, after Kessler

The bargain arrives already written. The customer can choose the product, compare the price, and decide whether to proceed; the document has already decided how a failure will be handled, which promises survive, and where a dispute will go. The standard form assigns the work of drafting to one party and the decision to accept to another. Where a stronger party presents substantially nonnegotiable terms, the familiar name is a contract of adhesion. Its question is larger than whether someone signed. What was offered, what could be understood, what alternatives existed, and who acquired authority over the parts of the exchange that received the least attention?

Repetition gives the form a productive purpose. A business that makes a thousand similar transactions need not negotiate a thousand complete sets of operating rules. Common terms can make obligations easier to administer and risks easier to calculate. Kessler’s 1943 account of adhesion recognized these economies while examining the power of the enterprise that writes the terms. A repair warranty can be coordinated with the service department; an insurance policy can define the risks against which reserves must be held. The form can save real work. The separate question is how that saving, and the authority to select the terms, are distributed between its author and its recipients.

Friedrich Kessler, “Contracts of Adhesion—Some Thoughts About Freedom of Contract,” Columbia Law Review 43 (1943), pp. 631–633 and 640–642, develops standardization, risk allocation, bargaining inequality, and contractual lawmaking. His historical argument is distinguished here from the particular rules applied by later courts.

Suppose preparing one common form costs a thousand, and a thousand transactions can use it without further drafting. The drafting cost is one per transaction. If separately negotiating each agreement would cost ten, common drafting saves nine thousand across the group. These are hypothetical resource costs; no term has yet been judged beneficial or harmful. The same standardized document could contain a useful warranty, an expensive warranty, or an exclusion that its customers would reject if they understood it. Efficiency in producing the document is one achievement. Efficiency in the choices the document makes requires a further account.

The unread form is more than a literary figure, although evidence about it has a date and a market. In a study published in 2014, Bakos, Marotta-Wurgler, and Trossen followed the January 2007 browsing of 48,154 visitors to ninety software companies. Their principal estimate was that only one or two of every thousand retail software shoppers accessed the license agreement; most accesses were too brief for a full reading. The relevant sites made opening the terms possible but optional. Access could be measured more readily than comprehension, and a person might have learned something through experience or another source. The result identifies a weak channel of scrutiny in that setting, without making every customer or every form identical.

Yannis Bakos, Florencia Marotta-Wurgler, and David R. Trossen, “Does Anyone Read the Fine Print? Consumer Attention to Standard-Form Contracts,” Journal of Legal Studies 43 (2014), pp. 1–5, 10–13, 19–22, and 32–33. The linked collection reproduces the published article at PDF pp. 3–37. The authors expressly separate limited readership from conclusions about whether reading is rational or the terms efficient.

Leaving the words unread can itself be an intelligible choice. Retain the earlier example of a risk-neutral customer for whom reading and comparing a warranty costs six in time and effort. Suppose doing so permits a costless switch that avoids a loss of a hundred in an event with probability one in a hundred. The expected protection is one, so spending six to obtain it reduces the customer’s expected return by five. Across ten thousand transactions, the possible losses still amount to ten thousand in expectation. Raise the individual probability to one in five, and reading produces twenty of expected protection, fourteen after its cost. Attention follows the stakes and the possibility of acting on what is learned; it cannot be made abundant merely by declaring the words available.

A minority that compares terms can sometimes improve the bargain for those who do not. That possibility depends on whether losing the attentive customers costs the seller enough, and whether the seller supplies a common package to both groups. The software study questioned whether readership alone was doing this work in its market; it did not establish that reputation, legal enforcement, or other constraints had disappeared. The question is institutional. A customer who trusts a recognizable seller, an independent adviser who compares the exclusions, and a court that declines to enforce a term can each constrain the drafter through a different channel. An account of the form needs to identify which channel actually carries the burden.

Bakos and colleagues, pp. 3–5 and 32–33, state the informed-minority argument and the study’s limits. The reading-cost example is retained from the later Utility Boilerplate, with risk neutrality, correct probabilities, avoidable loss, and costless switching stated explicitly.

The terms’ value must also be separated from the price adjustment that accompanies them. Suppose a warranty is worth fifteen to the buyer and costs six to provide. Remove it and cut the price by two: the buyer loses thirteen, the seller gains four, and together they lose nine. Fully informed comparison could make supplying the protection profitable, because its value exceeds its cost. Now suppose that the same protection costs twenty. Removing it while reducing the price by seventeen leaves the buyer two better off and the seller three better off. Five of resources are saved beyond the protection’s value. The two cases explain why a shorter form or narrower warranty cannot be judged by its length alone. Price, risk, performance, and the ability to compare belong in the same bargain.

Legal assent asks a related but distinct question. In Berman v. Freedom Financial Network, the Ninth Circuit in 2022 rejected an attempt to bind website users to arbitration through inconspicuous notices and continuation buttons. In the absence of demonstrated actual knowledge, the court required reasonably conspicuous notice and an action unambiguously manifesting assent to the offered terms. The users’ clicks did not perform that work in the circumstances before it. The case makes the presentation consequential without requiring a separately negotiated sentence for every obligation. A document can be accessible without having been effectively offered for acceptance, just as an accepted document can contain consequences its recipient never studied.

Apr. 15, 2025), slip pp. 12–18, applies the two requirements under California law and explains that conspicuousness depends on the transaction’s full circumstances, rather than a fixed design checklist.

Once an agreement exists, its content can face another inquiry. Delaware’s sales law permits a court that finds a contract or clause unconscionable when made to refuse enforcement, enforce the rest without the offending clause, or limit that clause’s application to avoid an unconscionable result. The statute also requires a reasonable opportunity for the parties to present evidence about the commercial setting, purpose, and effect. That is an inquiry into a term in its setting, not a declaration that every standard form fails or that every signature validates every term. It recognizes that the efficiency of preparing a common document does not settle the enforceability of each provision placed inside it.

6 Del. C. §2-302(1)–(2), the enacted Delaware UCC rule for sales, consulted October 20, 2026 in the official Code incorporating acts through October 1, 2026. The provision supplies the listed remedial options and the opportunity for contextual evidence; this account does not extend it to every transaction.

Reasonable expectations provide a further, carefully bounded way of examining what the form claims to contain. In the Arizona Supreme Court’s 1984 Darner decision, the court adopted the Restatement’s approach to standardized agreements while considering insurance terms that allegedly contradicted the coverage discussed with the agent. The approach generally binds a recipient to standard terms even without detailed knowledge, but excludes a particular term when the drafter has reason to believe the recipient would not assent if aware of it. The court distinguished expectations induced by the parties’ promises or conduct from the hope that every loss will be covered. The inquiry protects an agreement’s substance without making an unexpressed wish the measure of the bargain.

Darner Motor Sales, Inc. v. Universal Underwriters Insurance Co., 140 Ariz. 383, 389–394 (1984), adopting Restatement (Second) of Contracts §211 and explaining its scope. The court reversed summary judgment; it did not find every disputed representation proved. The account identifies this Arizona decision’s reasoning, not a uniform national rule.

Kessler’s question remains after these distinctions have been made. The enterprise uses the form to organize its business, but also to determine the conditions on which others may deal with it. The customer’s choice becomes more substantial where terms can be found, compared, understood, and refused without surrendering an indispensable service. Those capacities are unevenly distributed. Better disclosure can improve one of them without supplying the others; a substantive protection can guard a valuable interest while imposing costs that must still be considered. The form’s legitimacy cannot be read off its economy or its signature alone. It rests on what the document does and on the institutions through which the people affected can contest it.

The economy of the form leaves open the authority of its author.

The standard agreement lets a complicated economy transact without negotiating itself from the beginning every morning. Its convenience is real, and so is the power concentrated in the hand that prepares it. The task is to preserve the useful common language while examining whose risks it assigns, whose understanding it presumes, and which promises its smaller print takes away. Even a well-founded agreement will sometimes fail in performance. The next plate asks what the injured party should then receive, and what a remedy makes the original promise mean. The argument remains open.
