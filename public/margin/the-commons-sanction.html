The Sanction

in print · The Commons · plate vii

A common rule needs users who can tell when it has been broken and institutions able to answer the breach. Following the monitor, the penalty, the mistaken finding and the cost of enforcement shows how self-government becomes effective and why its own authority must remain answerable. The inquiry ends where an obsolete quota must be revised even if everyone has obeyed it.

A common rule needs an answerable keeper.The argument of this plate

A rule for the pasture becomes real at the moment someone must answer for breaking it. Until then it may be an agreement sincerely made, a notice beside the gate, a limit every household approves in principle. The animal entering after the allotted number has been reached asks a harder question. Who will notice? Who will speak? Who will decide whether there has been a breach, and what will happen if the owner refuses to put it right? The community that has agreed to restrain itself must organize these acts as carefully as it organizes the grazing. Otherwise the cost of keeping the agreement falls on whoever is least willing to watch it fail, while the advantage of breaking it remains with whoever is most willing to try.

The first condition of an enforceable rule is that the commoners can tell what it requires. A boundary identifies the resource and the people entitled to use it; an allocation specifies the turn, the quantity or the season. The rule must also reach the work that replenishes the common wealth: clearing the channel, repairing the gate, maintaining the record. Ostrom’s design principles connect these tasks to participation, accountable monitoring, graduated sanctions and accessible ways of resolving disputes. They describe recurring institutional features, with room for very different local rules. The pasture needs an arrangement its users can understand and revise, sufficiently definite that a disagreement can be brought to a decision without every dispute reopening the existence of the commons itself.

Elinor Ostrom, “Beyond Markets and States”, Nobel lecture (2009), §4.E, pp. 421–423. Her updated principles distinguish users from resource boundaries and monitoring conduct from monitoring the resource; the discussion expressly resists rigid interpretation.

The watcher’s work has its own commons problem. Everyone gains when the agreement holds, but the person who investigates an extra withdrawal gives up time and may acquire an enemy. Leaving enforcement to spontaneous indignation makes the institution depend on an uncertain supply of courage. A rotating duty distributes the work; a common contribution can pay a monitor; observation built into ordinary use can reduce its cost. Each arrangement raises a further question of accountability. A monitor paid by the largest grazier may hesitate at his gate. A rotation that everyone evades exists only on paper. The users need a means of seeing whether the watching itself is being done, without requiring a second inspector to shadow every first one. A public record, a shared reading of the meter or the right to challenge an omitted inspection can make ordinary supervision possible.

The size of the penalty matters through the prospect that it will actually follow. Suppose, in an invented one-period comparison, an unauthorized withdrawal gives its taker a net private gain of three units. A reliable inspection detects one-quarter of such withdrawals, and a detected breach brings a fine of sixteen. For a risk-neutral user who expects collection, the expected penalty is four, so the withdrawal loses one on his own account. If the chance of detection falls to one-tenth, the expected penalty falls to 1.6 and the same withdrawal becomes profitable. The notice beside the gate has not changed. The institution has. This calculation holds the gain, detection and collection rules fixed; it shows why the printed severity of a sanction cannot establish its practical force.

The example is constructed here: 3 − (0.25 × 16) = −1, while 3 − (0.10 × 16) = 1.4. Detection is conditional on a breach, the fine is collectible, and the user has no additional reputational or moral payoff. These assumptions isolate one deterrence mechanism; the figures estimate no actual commons.

A larger fine also makes a mistaken finding more costly. If an innocent user has a five-percent chance of being fined sixteen under the same imagined procedure, his expected loss from error is 0.8. Raising the fine to forty raises that loss to two, even though he has taken nothing beyond his allotment. The arithmetic gives the community a reason to improve evidence as well as punishment. A declining water level may reflect an unauthorized withdrawal, a broken gate or a dry season; a harvest reported late may conceal a breach or reveal a record that is too difficult to keep. An institution that treats every bad outcome as proof of disobedience will punish some of the people whose cooperation it needs. A right to explain the event and a practical means of correcting the account belong inside enforcement.

Graduated sanctions give the community room to distinguish a lapse from a continuing refusal. A warning can identify a misunderstood turn and give the user an opportunity to repair the harm. A repeated breach may justify a fine, a shorter allocation or temporary exclusion. The sequence preserves a path back to ordinary membership while making persistence more costly. Its leniency nevertheless needs judgment. A deliberately destructive act may require immediate restraint; a profitable first offense may recur if every offender can purchase a cheap warning. The appropriate response depends on the harm, the evidence and the prospect of repetition. What must remain credible is the community’s ability to move beyond the warning when the warning has failed.

Ostrom, §4.E, pp. 422–423, joins graduated sanctions to low-cost conflict resolution. The distinctions among mistakes, deliberate acts and repeat violations here develop the earlier Collected Charter; they do not prescribe a universal penalty schedule.

The consequence is felt by observers as well as by the person sanctioned. A user who restrains his own taking needs some reason to believe that others will do the same. When an evident breach is investigated and answered, that belief acquires evidence. When the powerful are excused, the rule teaches a different lesson: restraint is a burden imposed on those who cannot escape it. Selective enforcement can therefore damage the agreement even where the immediate extra withdrawal is small. The commoners are watching whether they belong to the same institution. Their willingness to contribute can depend on the answer, and their ability to question a monitor or a committee can help keep the answer from being decided by wealth alone.

The costs of punishment must appear in the common account. A fine paid into the community treasury transfers purchasing power from the offender; time spent investigating, collecting and disputing it uses resources. Imagine an enforcement arrangement that prevents damage valued at twenty units and consumes seven in monitoring and administration. Its stipulated net resource gain is thirteen. If it also collects twelve in fines, adding those twelve again would mistake a transfer for newly created wealth. The distribution still matters: the fine may support the monitoring budget and may fall heavily on a poor household. But the institution cannot establish its social value by celebrating the size of the account into which it pays its penalties. Its purpose is a resource better governed.

This second constructed comparison assumes damage of twenty is actually prevented and seven units of real enforcement cost are incurred: 20 − 7 = 13. The twelve-unit fine is a transfer within the group. Thomas Dietz, Ostrom and Paul Stern, “The Struggle to Govern the Commons” (2003), pp. 1908–1910, treat compliance, legitimacy, information and institutional costs as connected governance problems.

A village’s authority also depends on what happens beyond its boundary. An outsider who can take the resource while ignoring the local rule can undo the commoners’ restraint. A distant authority that refuses to recognize their institution can remove the means of answering him. Conversely, an outside court or public agency can provide a place to contest abuses that the village itself has become unwilling to hear. Local self-government needs room to act and connections through which its own conduct can be questioned. Ostrom’s principles accordingly include recognition of the users’ right to organize and, for connected systems, arrangements at more than one level. The community is strongest when its authority fits the resource and its obligations extend to the people its decisions affect.

The resource itself supplies a further test of the rule. Perfect compliance with an obsolete quota can still empty a fishery. A change in rainfall may make yesterday’s water shares impossible; a new technology may make an old limit on equipment an ineffective limit on taking. Monitoring conduct and monitoring conditions therefore do different work. One asks whether people obeyed; the other asks whether the arrangement still protects what they share. A procedure for revising the rule makes that distinction usable. Without it, the institution can become increasingly severe in defense of an agreement whose terms no longer accomplish their purpose.

Dietz, Ostrom and Stern, pp. 1908–1910, develop the requirements of adaptive governance and the need to revise rules as social and ecological conditions change. The illustrations here are hypothetical; compliance and resource condition are separate objects of observation.

The rule holds when the commoners can make conduct answerable.

The sanction earns its place by helping an agreement survive use, conflict and correction. Clear boundaries make a breach identifiable; watchfulness makes it visible; a proportionate consequence makes it costly; a hearing makes the judgment answerable; revision keeps the agreement fitted to the thing it governs. None of these acts is supplied by the word community. They are work the commoners must be able to organize and sustain. The pasture is governed when the person at the gate can be called to account, and when the people calling him to account can be called to account in turn. The argument remains open.
