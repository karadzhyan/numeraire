The Spread

in print · № V · 2 of 12 plates

The spread is the price of standing between: the cut the middleman takes for connecting a buyer to a seller — the broker's commission, the bid-ask, the platform's take rate, the merchant's markup, the banker's margin. This volume anatomizes it, weighing the real services intermediation provides — matching, liquidity, trust, the reduction of the costs of finding and vouching for a counterparty — against the suspicion that has shadowed the middleman since antiquity: that his cut may be less the price of a service than a toll levied on a passage he has contrived to control. Its question is the oldest about the man in the middle: when does he earn his cut, and when does he merely levy it?

The question of the volume the spread looks like the fair price of connection, the middleman's wage for bringing buyer and seller together — but when does he earn his cut by the value he creates, and when does he merely levy a toll on a passage he controls?
The argument

The prices the table has weighed so far have each been paid by one party to another — the wage from employer to worker, the rent from tenant to owner, the interest from borrower to lender, the premium from the insured to the bearer of his risk. This last of the prices is paid to neither party but to a third: the one who stands between the two who would trade, and takes a cut for the standing. Call it the spread — the gap between what the buyer pays and what the seller receives, captured by the middleman in between. It wears a hundred names: the broker's commission, the dealer's bid-ask, the platform's take rate, the merchant's markup, the banker's margin between the rate he pays and the rate he charges. In almost every exchange of any complexity, someone stands in the middle and is paid for it; and this volume is the anatomy of what he is paid, and of the oldest question about the man in the middle — whether his cut is the price of a service, or the toll of a position.

Why should standing between command a price at all? Because the middleman, at his best, does work the parties cannot easily do for themselves. He matches: he finds the buyer for the seller, sparing each the costly search through a world of strangers. He provides liquidity: he stands ready to trade at once, so that neither party need wait for the other to appear — the dealer who will always buy and always sell, the market that is open when you are. He bears the interval: he holds the goods, or the position, or the risk, between the moment one party is ready and the moment the other is. And he vouches: he interposes his own credit between strangers who could not trust each other, so that trade which mutual suspicion would have killed can go forward. Each of these is a genuine service with a real cost, and the spread is, in the first place, its wage. The middleman is paid because finding, waiting, trusting, and bearing are hard, and he makes them easy.

The thought that the middleman earns his keep is old, and so is the thought that he does not. Richard Cantillon, in the eighteenth century, made the merchant the very type of the entrepreneur — the man who buys at a known price to sell at an unknown one, bearing the uncertainty between, and earning his profit as the wage of that risk. The arbitrageur who carries grain from the glutted province to the starving raises its price where it is low and lowers it where it is high, knitting separate markets into one. Yet the suspicion is older still. Aristotle distinguished the natural getting of wealth by producing from the unnatural getting of it by exchange, and thought the trader who buys only to sell dearer produced nothing. The medieval moralist and the modern populist alike have hated the middleman as a parasite who toils not but skims. Frédéric Bastiat gave the merchant his most spirited defense — cut him out, and the work he did does not vanish but falls, done worse and dearer, on you — and the quarrel between his view and the parasite's is unsettled still.

And the middleman has not withered with the centuries; he has grown. The greatest intermediaries of the age are platforms — marketplaces owned by a third party who matches the two sides and skims a share of every exchange between them. A network effect makes such a platform more useful, and more unavoidable, the more people use it, until the buyers are where the sellers are and the sellers where the buyers are, and to leave is to leave the market itself. The financial intermediary stands between all the savers and all the borrowers, taking a margin on the passage of money through him. The supply chain interposes a procession of middlemen — exporter, importer, wholesaler, distributor, retailer — between the maker of a thing and its user, each adding a spread. Intermediation has become vaster, more layered, and more concentrated than any earlier age could have imagined, and the share of all economic value captured in spreads, by those who make nothing but stand between those who do, has risen with it.

Here the volume's question opens. The spread presents itself as the price of a service, fair payment for the real work of connecting, of providing liquidity, of vouching — and often that is exactly what it is. But a price is a verdict on a hidden contest, mistaken for a fact of nature; and the spread conceals a contest over whether the middleman earns his cut by the value he creates or merely levies it by his command of the passage between the parties. A service has a cost; a cut can run far above it; and what fixes the difference is not, in general, the worth of the work but the strength of the position. The middleman has every incentive to make himself necessary — to insert himself where he might be bypassed, to keep the buyer and the seller from finding one another, to widen the spread to whatever his place between them will bear. The line between the fee he earns and the toll he exacts is real, but it is never drawn where he says it is.

Consider the difference between a fee and a toll, for it is the heart of the matter. A fee is paid for something done; a toll is paid for being let past, to one who has placed himself across the road. The middleman who controls the only bridge may charge far more than the bridge costs to keep — not for his labor but for his location, rent drawn from the mere fact of standing where all must pass. A price is a verdict on a hidden contest, mistaken for a fact of nature; and the spread is the verdict of a contest between the middleman and the two he stands between, over how much of the gain from their trade he can capture by virtue of his position. For the spread is always a slice of the surplus that buyer and seller create by dealing at all, and the size of the slice measures, beyond the cost of his service, the power his place affords — the platform none can leave, the exchange all must use, the broker who alone knows where to find the other side.

None of this is the populist's case against the middleman, a fool's case the volume will not make. Bastiat was right: the functions the intermediary performs are real, and abolishing him does not abolish them. Cut out the broker and you must find the buyer yourself; cut out the bank and the saver must judge the borrower alone. Disintermediation fails as often as attempted, because the search, the liquidity, and the trust do not vanish when the middleman does — they revert to the parties, who do them worse. The market-maker's spread is the price of a liquidity you would miss the instant it was gone; the platform's take buys a marketplace you could not cheaply build alone. Skill in intermediation is real, and the finest middlemen enlarge the gains from trade — more deals, at lower total cost — rather than merely taxing them. Much of the spread, perhaps most of it, is honestly earned. The argument is not that the middleman is a thief, but that his cut is a verdict, and that the share of it which is toll rather than fee is hidden in the same number as the rest.

The twelve plates follow the price of standing between from the service to the toll. The Middleman sets out the spread and the services it pays for; The Tribute asks when those services explain it and when only the middleman's power does. The Match anatomizes the reducing of search, The Dealer the liquidity priced in the bid-ask, The Hub the platform whose network effect makes it inescapable. The Arbitrage weighs the trade that equalizes prices or contrives the gap it exploits; The Escrow, the manufacture of trust that lets strangers deal; The Chain, the procession of middlemen between maker and user; The Merchant, the markup of the oldest of them all. The Banker takes up financial intermediation, The Bypass the dream of cutting the middleman out and his recurring return, and The Passage the politics of the toll — whether the passage between us belongs to the one who stands in it or to those who must pass. Other volumes border this one: the power, for the middleman's grip is a kind of monopoly; the rent, for his toll is an unearned return to position; the surplus, whose gains of trade he slices; and the tax, his cut a private wedge between buyer and seller. Whether the man in the middle earns his cut or merely levies it is the question this volume holds open. The argument remains open.

Volume V, The Spread, opens with two plates in print — The Middleman and The Tribute, the price of standing between and the contest over whether the man in the middle earns his cut or merely levies it. The fifth element of the table, and the twenty-third of its volumes to appear; with it the prices stand complete. It joins The Wage, The Rent, The Rate, and The Premium — the price of labor, the return to ownership, the price of time, the price of risk, and now the spread the price of intermediation, the cut of the third party who stands between buyer and seller. It borders The Power, for the middleman's grip on the passage is a kind of monopoly; The Rent again, for the toll he levies is an unearned return to position; and The Surplus, for the spread is a slice of the gains every exchange creates. The thesis is that the spread, which wears the look of the fair price of connection, is in truth a verdict — on whether the middleman creates the value he charges for, or merely controls the passage and collects a toll.
