The Increment

in print · The Rent · plate x

A public improvement can enter a private balance sheet before construction is complete. Mill’s proposed treatment of future increments brings the public claim into contact with improvements, purchased expectations and the task of valuation. A simple railway account then distinguishes site uplift from the project’s wider benefits, and a charge on the gain from a tax on the whole land value.

The increment is the place where public effort enters a private account.The argument of this plate, after Mill and George

A railway opens, and a field becomes an address. The owner need not have laid the track, financed the station or brought the passengers; it is enough that the field is near the place where they now arrive. Buyers see a different future for it, and the price changes before a brick has been added. The improvement happened beyond the boundary, but part of its value appears within the title. This is the unearned increment in its most arresting form: a community spends, moves and gathers, and a private asset rises to meet it. The question is not whether the owner has done anything in the course of life. It is what this particular increase pays for.

The distinction is easier to make by holding the owner’s acts still. Suppose the same site, in the same condition, can now support a more profitable use because journeys to it have become shorter. The additional payment a tenant will offer reflects that access. It differs from the return on a warehouse the owner builds, even if both enter the price of the property together. The rise is not inevitable: a railway may bypass the site, bring noise instead of customers, or provide such good access elsewhere that the old location loses its advantage. Community action can create and destroy location value. The increment is a change to be explained, not a law that land always appreciates.

Ricardo, Principles, chapter X distinguishes the original land return from the return on improvements. The railway comparison is an illustrative application: physical condition and the owner’s expenditure are held constant while access changes.

John Stuart Mill gave the public claim a particularly careful form. Where a class gained income through changes requiring neither its effort nor its expenditure, society could claim a share of the increase. But Mill immediately encountered the difficulty of identification. An individual estate’s higher rent might reflect its proprietor’s skill or investment; simply taking the observed increase would confound two sources. His proposed general valuation and cautious estimate of subsequent spontaneous growth were attempts to preserve the distinction. The challenge was already there in the argument’s classical form: community-created value is a reason for a levy, not an instruction that every rise in a property’s price should be treated alike.

Mill, Principles, V.II §5 discusses the public claim, owner-produced improvements and a general valuation. The linked text is the 1909 Ashley edition; the proposal is historical and does not supply a present assessment procedure.

A small account makes the stakes clearer. Imagine that an otherwise unchanged site can yield a certain annual net rent of one hundred, beginning one year from now and continuing forever. At five percent its value is two thousand. A public connection adds twenty to that annual flow. If the discount rate and every other condition remain fixed, the site is now worth twenty-four hundred: an increment of four hundred. The new owner could buy the income at its new price, but the person holding the title when the news arrived receives the capital gain. No extra act by that person is required for the arithmetic to work.

Schwerhoff, Edenhofer and Fleurbaey, Equity and Efficiency Effects of Land Value Taxation, IMF WP/22/263 (2022), §2.1, p. 4 state the constant-flow capitalization relation. The figures here are invented: 100 ÷ .05 = 2,000; 120 ÷ .05 = 2,400.

The four hundred must not be confused with the whole benefit of the connection. Travelers may save time without renting near the station. Some of the new site demand may be diverted from other places. Construction and operation use real resources, and neighbors may bear costs. Even the observed price increase needs a counterfactual: if the discount rate fell from five to four percent while the original rent remained one hundred, the value would rise to twenty-five hundred without any improvement in access. A rising valuation alone cannot tell us how much the public work created. To claim the increment one must identify it; to defend the project one must compare its benefits and costs.

Schwerhoff and coauthors, §§1–2.1, pp. 2–4 distinguish project-related value capture from taxing the whole land value and show how capitalization depends on the discount rate. The counterfactual examples here hold rent constant or separate local value from wider effects; they are not estimates of a railway project.

Once identified, the increment can be divided in more than one way. If the public connection in our example costs three hundred once, a charge of fifteen a year on its added site rent has a present value of three hundred at the same discount rate. It can finance that cost while leaving the owner five a year of the added twenty, worth one hundred. This is an account of who pays, not a theorem that a public work must finance itself from nearby land. A project costing five hundred would exceed the four hundred of site uplift even if its other benefits made it worth building. Conversely, a large local uplift would not erase harms imposed elsewhere.

Schwerhoff and coauthors, §1, p. 3, and §2.1, p. 4 separate land-value capture from a tax on the whole base. In the example, 15 ÷ .05 = 300 and 5 ÷ .05 = 100. All flows, timing and costs are stipulated, with no risk or further fiscal effects.

A betterment charge and a general land-value tax accordingly answer related but different questions. The first seeks some of the gain attributable to a specified change; its difficulty is the comparison with what would have happened without that change. The second taxes the value of the site as a whole, regardless of whether its advantage came from a station, a harbor or many generations of growth. Henry George’s remedy belongs to the broader category. He wanted the public to receive the land’s rent while leaving possession and the ownership of improvements in private hands. The title could remain; what changed was the division of the income it carried.

George, Progress and Poverty, VIII.2 (1912 edition of the work first published in 1879) proposes retaining the forms of possession while appropriating rent through taxation. The contrast with project-specific capture concerns the base of the charge, not two names for the same instrument.

Mill’s further qualification concerns time. A buyer may have paid not only for today’s rent but for expected growth. If the new railway had long been anticipated, some or all of the four hundred might already be in the purchase price before construction began. An unexpected levy can then reduce wealth that the present owner bought rather than simply intercept a surprise windfall. Mill expressly considered this problem and sought to protect the present market value, which included future expectations. A reform can instead choose a different transition, but it should name the choice. Calling a tax prospective does not establish that it has no effect on an asset’s value today.

Mill, V.II §5, especially the paragraph beginning “But though there could be no question” addresses purchasers’ expectations and protection of present market price. Section 6 then distinguishes a long-established public charge already attached to the title from a new demand on its holder.

Nor does sending a bill to the owner settle every incidence question. A genuinely fixed site charge in a competitive rental market does not create extra willingness to pay among tenants; the owner’s wish to recover it cannot itself raise the market rent. Yet people can still be displaced by the access improvement that raised demand, by rebuilding, or by a different tax imposed on the improvements. Public recovery of value and the position of existing residents must therefore be considered together. Revenue can be returned to the people who pay for the railway, spent on the service, or used for some other purpose. Each choice changes the distribution even when the gross levy is identical.

Ricardo, chapter X states the distinction between incidence on pure rent and on produced improvements; Schwerhoff and coauthors, §7.3, pp. 20–21 discuss transition concerns for tenants. The surrounding passage does not infer automatic protection from the existence of a value-capture levy.

The older argument’s force survives these distinctions. The owner’s title determines where the gain appears; it does not establish that the owner created it. Equally, the public’s contribution establishes a claim worth considering without telling us that every penny of every increase has one source. The work is to separate the sources, choose a baseline, and say what happens to people who relied on the previous settlement. These are the practical contents of the question “to whom?” Once they are specified, the apparent natural yield of property becomes a decision about the way a community shares its own development.

The increment is the place where public effort enters a private account.

A community can build the connection and then purchase its benefit back through higher rents, or it can arrange to retain part of the value it creates. Neither result follows from the rails themselves. The difference lies in the terms under which the surrounding ground is held, and in whether those terms change before or after the expectation has acquired a price. The next question is how a tax might follow that value into the owner’s account without taxing the work that brought it into being. The argument remains open.
