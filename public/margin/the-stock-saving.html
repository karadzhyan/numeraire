The Saving

in print · The Stock · plate xi

Saving can build a reserve, but the final balance does not show how much came from income left unspent, a change in prices or a transfer from someone else. Separating those entries preserves the practical value of thrift while testing its use as an explanation of fortunes. Life-cycle provision, retained capital income and a dated study of accumulation each disclose a different part of the account.

Accumulation is an account, not a measure of virtue.The argument of this plate

A person who spends less than his income can add to what he owns, and the discipline required may be considerable. That familiar route from a flow to a stock is real. It does not follow that the size of the stock measures the discipline, or that every addition passed through the owner's income before reaching his balance sheet. A fortune can rise because its assets are repriced or because someone transfers property to it. It can also grow because earnings from the fortune itself are saved. The question of thrift therefore requires two accounts: the arithmetic of how wealth changed, and the judgment of what the owner did to bring about that change. Confusing them turns an observable sum into a biography that the sum cannot supply.

The arithmetic begins with several distinct entries. Over an accounting period, net worth can change through saving, net capital transfers, holding gains or losses, and other changes in the volume of assets. Saving records income left after consumption, with the appropriate accounting adjustments. A holding gain records a revaluation; an inheritance adds property to the recipient without requiring him to earn it. Destruction can remove an asset without anyone having consumed it. Keeping these entries apart explains how a household can become wealthier while saving little, or save diligently while becoming poorer. The closing balance sheet records the joint result. It does not identify the cause unless the intervening changes are also known.

The System of National Accounts 2008 separates saving and capital transfers in §§10.18–10.21, revaluation in §§12.73–12.86, and the reconciliation of balance sheets in §§13.8–13.13. Its saving measure includes an adjustment for pension entitlements where relevant (§9.28). “Gross” and “net” in national accounting also distinguish whether consumption of fixed capital has been deducted; that convention should not be confused with an author's separate treatment of capital gains.

An invented household makes the separation visible. It begins the year with $100,000 of net worth, receives $60,000 of wages and $4,000 of dividends, and consumes $58,000. Its saving is $6,000. Suppose, in addition, that its existing assets rise in value by $10,000 and it receives a $20,000 inheritance, with no other changes, taxes, depreciation or pension adjustments. It ends with $136,000. The $36,000 increase contains $6,000 of saving, $10,000 of gains and $20,000 transferred from someone else. Calling the whole increase thrift would collapse three different events. Calling the saved dividends something other than saving would make the opposite mistake: capital income that is received and left unconsumed can itself build the stock.

A gain can enlarge wealth without making a payment or producing another physical asset. A house revalued from one price to another remains the same house. The owner can be richer in terms of what a sale would command, although using that value may require selling, borrowing or moving. If the owner needs an equivalent home whose price has risen too, the gain does not translate into an equally large improvement in housing possibilities. Some nominal appreciation also compensates for a change in the general price level. These distinctions do not erase the gain. They explain what kind of gain it is, whose opportunities it changes, and why a rise in market wealth need not be a matching increase in society's capacity to produce.

The SNA’s §§12.74–12.80 distinguish nominal, neutral and real holding gains, as well as realized and unrealized gains. A sale changes the form in which the owner holds value; it does not make the entire accumulated gain new saving in the period of sale.

The empirical importance of that distinction can be substantial without becoming a universal law. In a 2019 working paper using Norwegian administrative records, Fagereng, Holm, Moll and Natvik found that saving rates excluding capital gains were roughly flat across much of the positive-wealth distribution, while rates including gains rose with wealth. Asset holdings and the decision to retain them were central to the difference. The result describes their sample and measures; it does not establish that increasing someone's wealth would cause a particular saving response. The treatment of unlisted businesses also limits what the measured gains reveal at the very top. The study's lesson for the present question is precise: an account of accumulation can change markedly when revaluation is allowed its own place.

“Saving Behavior Across the Wealth Distribution: The Importance of Capital Gains,” NBER Working Paper 26588 (December 2019), sections 3–4. The main saving-rate sample is 2005–2015. Private-business values are based on assessed book-related values, with no separate capital-gain estimate; household observations with very low disposable income or formation/dissolution are excluded. “Gross” in this paper includes capital gains.

Inheritance changes the person who commands existing wealth before it changes the amount available to society. In the household example, the recipient's $20,000 addition has a corresponding transfer out of the donor's estate. If both are inside the group being counted, and taxes, costs and subsequent behavior are set aside, their combined net worth is unchanged by the transfer. The recipient gains an advantage without an act of saving of his own. The donor may have saved, inherited or gained the property earlier; tracing that history asks a different question. Aggregation can cancel the transfer between accounts while leaving its distributional consequence entirely intact. The same total can now support a different person's life.

SNA §§10.19–10.21 record the recipient's increase and the payer's corresponding decrease in net worth; §10.212(e) includes legacies and large gifts among other capital transfers. This transfer arithmetic holds the group boundary and other changes fixed. An inheritance from abroad would cross that boundary.

Compounding, too, requires an account of what is being retained. If a $100,000 asset pays a certain 5 percent each year and every payment is reinvested at the same rate, it reaches $162,889.46 after ten years. If every payment is consumed and the principal's value stays fixed, the stock remains $100,000. These invented alternatives omit taxes, inflation, risk and fees. In the first, retained capital income is saving; in the second, it is consumed. If the asset instead rises in market price, the increase enters through revaluation. A company may retain income within its own accounts while the shareholder sees a change in the value of a claim. One must specify whose saving is being measured and avoid counting the same underlying accumulation twice. Wealth does not explain its own growth merely by existing.

The ordinary case for thrift survives these distinctions because a reserve can be the means of moving resources across a life. Imagine forty working years at $60,000 a year followed by twenty years without earnings. With no initial wealth, return, tax, inflation, pension or inheritance, consuming $40,000 in every year requires saving $20,000 in each working year. The resulting $800,000 pays for the twenty later years and is exhausted at their end. The stock at retirement records provision for a period without a wage. It does not by itself establish an intention to found an estate. Different lifespans, uncertain income, public pensions and medical needs alter the calculation, but they preserve the reason for making it: a snapshot of accumulated wealth can conceal obligations still to come.

This is an invented, fully specified life-cycle example. It develops the complete original Utility Lifetime alongside the stock-and-flow account in Collected Reservoir. The household calculation holds its income path and lifespan fixed; aggregate accumulation requires a separate account of overlapping households, transfers and asset values.

The judgment of thrift becomes less secure when it is inferred backward from the fortune. The same saving rate applied to unequal incomes produces unequal sums; the same investment return applied to unequal initial holdings produces unequal gains. A household may sacrifice heavily to retain a small amount, while another retains much more after satisfying every ordinary want. An enterprise may create substantial value that appears in the market price of its shares before it distributes cash. In that case a gain can reflect work and invention, although its size also depends on expectations, competition and the claims other participants hold. Neither a positive gain nor the absence of a wage settles the contribution. The moral account requires evidence about effort, opportunity, risk and the institutions that divide the result.

The balance sheet records accumulation, not a measure of virtue.

What remains of the reward of thrift is a valid account of one activity and an insufficient account of the whole distribution. Saving can build independence, carry a person through retirement and transmit something deliberately preserved. Gains and gifts can also enlarge a stock, and saved capital income can reinforce an advantage already possessed. Their relative importance has to be investigated in the place, period and population at issue. A fortune may contain the fruits of contribution without every dollar being a certificate of it; a modest reserve may embody great sacrifice without ever becoming a fortune. The closing figure tells us how much stands in the account. The question of what is deserved begins with how it came to be there. The argument remains open.
