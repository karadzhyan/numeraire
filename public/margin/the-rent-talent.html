Rn viii

The Talent

in print · The Rent · plate viii

The rare gift is cultivated by its owner and rewarded by a market whose reach the owner did not create alone. This plate follows the superstar premium through imperfect substitution, the reproduction of a performance, the cost of becoming excellent, and the audience's role in deciding whom to hear. It asks what part of the fortune exceeds the performer's alternative, and why that calculation can clarify the argument about desert without settling it.

The gift helps explain why the voice is wanted; the world around it helps decide what the wanting will pay.The argument of this plate

The singer did the work. Whatever came to her at birth, she practiced, rehearsed, performed, and gave the audience something it wanted; to describe her fortune as unearned can sound like refusing to hear the music. Yet the owner of the fertile field also points to something valuable, and the value alone never settled the landlord's claim. The difficult case is a gift that belongs inseparably to a person, takes discipline to become a skill, and commands a reward far beyond what equally demanding work brings elsewhere. The gift was not chosen. The cultivation was undertaken. The scale of the reward was arranged by a world the performer did not make. The Talent asks which of these the fortune is paying for, and what the answer can tell us about its being deserved.

The first source of the great reward lies in how buyers compare performances. Someone who wants a particular voice may find that several cheaper performances give no satisfactory substitute for hearing it once. Quality and quantity do not always trade evenly. Sherwin Rosen used this imperfect substitution to explain why rewards can rise more steeply than the differences in ability that sustain them. But ability, in his model, is an index of the qualities buyers value, known to them and fixed among potential sellers. It is not a biological test of innate merit. The model asks how a distribution of valued qualities becomes a distribution of earnings; it does not establish how much of the quality came from birth, education, opportunity, or work. Even at the beginning, the economic explanation and the moral question occupy different ground.

Rosen, “The Economics of Superstars” (1981), pp. 845–848. Talent is represented by an observable quality index; investment and uncertainty are set aside in the basic assignment model. The model does not estimate an innate component of any performer's achievement.

The second source is the reach of a single performance. A singer appearing in a room faces the room's limits; a recording can be heard by people who never enter it. Production need not rise proportionately with the number of listeners. Combined with a preference for the better performance, that possibility permits a small number of sellers to serve large audiences. The scarcity is now peculiar: the valued voice remains rare, while access to its performance can be reproduced on a great scale. A fortune can follow from multiplying a modest advantage by a very large audience. Something real is produced in this multiplication — more people can enjoy the performance — even though the singer's inherited gift has not grown in proportion to the money. The market changes the distance the gift can travel and, with it, the reward that can reach its owner.

A small premium at the level of the listener can consequently become a large rent at the level of the performer. Take an invented case in which an audience would pay twenty cents more per listening for one performance than for its closest alternative. Hold the relevant delivery costs equal and suppose enforceable rights allow the performer to collect that difference. Across a thousand listenings, the premium is $200; across a million, it is $200,000. Nothing in this arithmetic says the performer worked a thousand times harder or became a thousand times more gifted. Nor does it say the listeners received nothing for their money: each preferred what they obtained. The calculation locates the source of the magnification. Whether this entire premium can actually be collected depends on competition, contracts, copying, and the terms on which the performance reaches its public.

The twenty-cent example is invented. Rosen’s pure joint-consumption case, § III.B, pp. 852–853, distinguishes a seller with arbitrarily close competitors from an outlier with a finite quality advantage. In the latter case a small unit rent multiplied across a large market can yield a large total rent; concentration alone does not guarantee it.

That distinction prevents the superstar story from becoming a law that the best must take everything. If rivals are close substitutes, they can constrain the price; if tastes differ, different performers can have their own audiences. A surgeon cannot reproduce an hour of personal attention as a recording reproduces a song. Live performance can offer qualities that a broadcast lacks, and expanding a seller's clientele can degrade the service each receives. Rosen's own analysis contains these limits, as well as cases in which changes in demand and cost alter entry and earnings in different ways. Scale makes exceptional concentration possible. The exact result depends on what is scarce, what can be copied, what deteriorates with expansion, and what buyers are willing to substitute. The winner's income therefore cannot be read backward as a simple measurement of the distance between her gift and everyone else's.

The economic meaning of rent requires a second comparison, with the performer's own alternative. Suppose, in another invented one-period example, receipts from performing are $100,000, direct costs are $10,000, and the best available alternative would yield $40,000 after its own costs. Assume comparable hours and working conditions, with no additional advantage or burden left outside the figures. The surplus above the alternative is $50,000. That is the rent relative to this specified choice. It is not the difference between the star's pay and a colleague's pay, and it is not a conclusion that half the work was imaginary. Change the alternative, the burden of the work, or the period over which the decision is made, and the measured surplus changes. Calling income rent identifies what payment exceeds a particular opportunity cost; it does not identify a sum detached from every possible use of the person who earns it.

The receipts-and-alternative example is invented: $100,000 − $10,000 − $40,000 = $50,000. Rosen’s § II, p. 850, explicitly separates direct costs from the seller's opportunity cost in another activity. The comparison is with the person's alternative, not another person's observed wage.

The time at which the comparison is made matters especially when a gift has been cultivated. Years of training may already be sunk when a successful performer decides whether to accept tonight's engagement. They remain part of the earlier decision whether to train at all. A return unnecessary to induce one more performance can belong to a prospect of rewards that helped induce the education, the practice, and the risk of failure. Conversely, the fact that some prospective reward was necessary does not show that every dollar of the eventual fortune was required. The successful career is one outcome among those an entrant faced; the visible winner cannot stand in for all who began. The useful inquiry keeps the two moments distinct: what is needed to elicit the service now, and what expectations were needed to elicit the investment before its result was known.

The Founding Talent develops the cultivated gift and the cost of entering a tournament. The distinction here separates an ex post opportunity-cost calculation from an ex ante investment decision. Rosen's basic model deliberately leaves learning and uncertainty outside its scope; it cannot supply their empirical magnitudes.

There is another difficulty: an audience may help make the distinction that later appears to have been waiting inside the gift. In the 2006 Music Lab experiment, participants chose among unfamiliar songs, with some able to see earlier download counts. Across the experimental worlds, that social information increased both inequality and the variation in which songs succeeded. Quality, measured by popularity when choices were made without those counts, still mattered; it did not uniquely determine the result. The experiment supplies a reason to resist treating success as an independent certificate of preexisting superiority. It does not establish that famous musicians are talentless, or measure what share of any commercial fortune came from luck. Its narrower lesson is enough for this plate: when other people's choices influence ours, the audience can magnify early differences into rewards that the original performances alone would not predict.

Salganik, Dodds, and Watts, “Experimental Study of Inequality and Unpredictability in an Artificial Cultural Market” (2006), pp. 854–856, with supporting methods. The outcome was downloads of 48 unfamiliar songs in an experimental market; the authors explain the limits of applying that setting directly to commercial markets.

The gift also reaches the public through institutions that can claim part of its return. A recording requires production and distribution; a performance requires a venue or a means of access; a name requires an audience capable of recognizing it. Some intermediaries supply indispensable services, and some can use control of access to bargain for more than the cost of those services. The amount left to the performer depends on that division as well as on how much listeners value the work. A large audience can therefore coexist with modest pay for its creator, just as a lucrative contract can reflect a strong bargaining position as well as an exceptional performance. The price observed at the end belongs to a chain of claims. Treating it as the naked yield of talent removes the institutions that helped determine both its size and its recipient.

None of these mechanisms can, by itself, decide whether the fortunate may keep what they receive. One argument starts with the person's claim to her own capacities: a gift remains hers even if she did not choose it, and willing listeners have reasons of their own for paying to hear it. Another starts with the arbitrary distribution of those capacities and the social arrangements that make them valuable: birth supplies no reason why one person's opportunities should dwarf another's, and collective arrangements need not copy the lottery into every lifetime prospect. These arguments disagree about the grounds of entitlement. A calculation of opportunity cost cannot choose between them, any more than it can decide what a lawful title to land should permit. It can make the disagreement more precise by showing where scarcity, investment, and institutional power enter the payment.

The Collected edition's The Talent places the moral difficulty at the center of this treatment. Economic rent is an analytical category; whether scarce ability confers a claim to the whole surplus is a further judgment about ownership, reciprocity, incentives, and distribution.

The comparison with land is consequently illuminating without making the singer and the field interchangeable. Neither owner chose the initial endowment. Yet a person must act to supply a performance, can alter its quality, can withhold effort, and has a life whose uses cannot be reduced to an acre's alternative tenancy. The field, too, can be improved, so even there the return to what was made must be distinguished from the advantage of what was given. The comparison disciplines the easy claim that a valuable service makes every part of its price deserved; the differences discipline the equally easy claim that an unchosen gift settles the entire income as unearned. A serious account owes both the performance and its circumstances their due. It can acknowledge the work without mistaking the market's scale for the worker's moral magnitude.

The gift helps explain why the voice is wanted; the world around it helps decide what the wanting will pay.

The fortune is thus earned in more than one sense, and those senses need to be kept visible. Work was performed; a contract may have been honored; an audience received something it valued. A surplus may also have arisen from an ability that could not be reproduced, an early opportunity that others lacked, or a technology that made one performance available to millions. These facts can coexist in the same receipt. The question of good luck survives the demonstration of real achievement, just as the achievement survives the recognition of luck. What the economics of talent gives us is a way to see the mixture and to question the inference from payment to desert. The singer owns her voice. How far that ownership should carry a claim upon the fortune the whole arrangement makes possible is the question the price records without answering. The argument remains open.
