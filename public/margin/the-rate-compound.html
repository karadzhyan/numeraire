Rt xi

The Compound

in print · The Rate · plate xi

The same multiplication that builds a saver’s claim can enlarge a borrower’s obligation, but its path depends on what is paid and when. This plate distinguishes capitalization, interest-only payment and amortization, then follows the claim into default risk, income growth and the distribution of purchasing power. It asks when lending makes a productive opportunity possible and when the terms of continued dependence turn a price into something experienced as tribute.

The balance follows a formula; the burden follows a life.The argument of this plate

A debt can grow while its owner sleeps. That is the quiet force of compounding: the next period’s interest is charged on an amount that already contains the last period’s interest. For the saver, the process is accumulation. For the borrower, it can make a short interval of relief the beginning of a longer obligation. The two descriptions concern the same claim viewed from opposite sides. To understand what the multiplication transfers, however, we must first ask whether interest is being paid, added to the balance or merely promised by someone who may never pay it.

Take an imagined loan of one hundred at ten percent a year. If no payments are made and each year’s interest is added to principal, the balance becomes one hundred and ten after one year, one hundred and twenty-one after two, and about two hundred and fifty-nine after ten. Simple interest charged only on the original hundred would instead produce a total claim of two hundred after ten years. The difference is the interest charged on earlier interest. It requires no fresh advance of one hundred each year; it requires the unpaid charge to become part of the sum on which the next charge is calculated.

Fisher, The Theory of Interest (1930), chapter I §§8–11, connects dated sums through the interest rate. The figures are constructed here: 100(1.10)10 = 259.37, against 100 + 10 × 10 = 200. They assume a fixed rate, no fees, no payments and enforceable capitalization of the unpaid interest.

The payment schedule changes the story. If the borrower pays ten at each year’s end, the principal remains one hundred rather than swelling to two hundred and fifty-nine. If the payment is twenty, the first year’s interest is ten and the remaining ten reduces principal to ninety. In the second year interest is nine, leaving eleven of that payment to reduce principal to seventy-nine. The same percentage can accompany a rising, stationary or falling balance. A statement that gives only the rate has therefore omitted part of what the borrower needs to know.

Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, “How does paying down a mortgage work?” reviewed May 28, 2024, explains the division between principal and interest and how a declining balance changes that division. The annual loan here is an illustration, not the monthly schedule of a particular mortgage.

A payment can also be too small to stop the growth. Let the same borrower pay five at the end of each year. Ten of interest is due in the first year, so the balance rises to one hundred and five despite the payment. In the second year ten dollars and fifty cents is due, and the balance rises to one hundred and ten dollars and fifty cents. This is negative amortization. The borrower is paying, but not enough to cover the interest being added. The distinction matters because an apparently manageable installment can postpone rather than discharge the burden.

Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, “What is negative amortization?” reviewed September 11, 2024, identifies unpaid interest added to principal as the mechanism. Here the recurrence is Bt+1 = 1.10Bt − 5. Capitalization and payment timing are stipulated, rather than assumed to govern every loan.

For a fixed-rate loan scheduled to end after ten equal annual payments, the payment on that hundred would be about sixteen dollars and twenty-seven cents. The exact unrounded payment extinguishes the balance at the tenth payment. The first installment contains ten of interest and about six dollars and twenty-seven cents of principal; later installments contain less interest because the amount outstanding has fallen. Over the decade the borrower pays about one hundred and sixty-two dollars and seventy-five cents in total. That total cannot be compared directly with the no-payment balance as though both had been paid on the same date. Earlier payments have already returned resources to the lender. Compounding is an account of timing as well as multiplication.

CFPB, “How does paying down a mortgage work?”, describes a constant principal-and-interest installment with changing components. The hypothetical annual payment is 100 × .10/[1 − (1.10)−10] = 16.2745…; it excludes taxes, insurance, fees and rounding adjustments.

The lender’s balance is not yet the lender’s consumption. A claim can accumulate on paper while the borrower has no cash to deliver, and its sale price can fall below its contractual amount if repayment becomes doubtful. A creditor who expects half of a promised payment should not value it as though the whole were certain. The difference between a stated rate and an expected return is especially important when borrowing is expensive: some of the quoted charge can compensate for the possibility that principal as well as interest will be lost. The presence of that possibility does not establish that every charge is justified, but omitting it makes the account incomplete.

Fisher, chapter I §§7, 11 and 18, distinguishes the valuation of expected benefits from a pure interest comparison that deliberately sets default risk aside. A contractual balance, its market value and cash actually received are different objects.

When interest is paid, it enters one account and leaves another. Fisher’s double-entry argument makes the transfer visible: adding the two monetary entries does not create additional resources merely by recording the same payment twice. But this is an accounting statement, not a judgment that nothing important has happened. Ten dollars can cost the borrower more in forgone necessities than it gives the lender in additional comfort. The transfer can also change who has the means to invest, withstand a loss or refuse the next bargain. Equal money entries do not imply equal changes in well-being.

Fisher, chapter I §§12–13 and §6 note 5, provides the cancellation of payment and receipt while expressly distinguishing money amounts from their subjective value to richer and poorer people. No welfare-neutrality conclusion follows from the cancellation.

Nor does calling the payment a transfer make the loan itself sterile. A borrower might use the hundred to undertake an activity yielding one hundred and thirty after a year, where no such activity was possible without finance. After repaying one hundred and ten, the borrower retains twenty; the lender receives ten more than the sum advanced. Those figures divide the activity’s thirty-dollar surplus before other costs. If the activity yields only one hundred and five, the same contract leaves the borrower five short of full repayment. The productive result and the financial division must be assessed separately. A rate can price access to a useful advance and still allocate its risks or gains badly.

The burden also depends on the income against which the debt must be serviced. Suppose an unpaid balance grows at ten percent while the borrower’s nominal income grows at five. The debt-to-income ratio rises each year by 1.10/1.05, about 4.76 percent. If both grow at ten percent, the ratio is unchanged even though the balance becomes larger; if income grows faster, it falls. These are comparisons under stipulated growth and no payments, not forecasts. They show why a dramatic nominal total is insufficient to establish a growing real burden, and why modest-looking growth can become oppressive when the resources available to meet it stand still.

Inflation introduces another distinction. A fixed nominal payment buys less if prices rise, but that does not ensure the borrower has the income needed to make it. Wages may lag, rates may reset, or the debt may be indexed. Conversely, a falling price level can increase the purchasing power of a fixed claim while making its nominal amount harder to earn. The creditor and debtor share a contract; they do not necessarily share protection against the changes around it. The small print concerning adjustment can matter as much as the number printed most prominently on the page.

The question of tribute returns at the point where alternatives narrow. A household borrowing to exploit a profitable opportunity occupies a different position from one borrowing because an unavoidable bill precedes its next wage. Both may sign willingly in the immediate sense that refusing would be worse. That fact does not tell us how their alternatives were formed, how competitive the offer was or how clearly the future payment burden was understood. To criticize an oppressive bargain, however, one should identify the feature that makes it oppressive: the price relative to alternatives, the allocation of risk, the renewal of unpaid charges, or the absence of a practicable way out. The exponential formula alone supplies none of those findings.

Compounding multiplies a claim. Whether it multiplies opportunity or dependence depends on the life arranged around the claim.

A rate can be a price and a means through which a hierarchy is maintained. The two are not mutually exclusive descriptions, and neither follows simply from the presence of interest on interest. The inquiry must keep the advance, the payment schedule, the productive use, the risks and the alternatives in the same account. Otherwise the saver's celebration and the debtor's alarm pass each other, each treating one side of a shared promise as the whole. The multiplication continues according to its rule; the justification of that rule remains a human question. The argument remains open.
