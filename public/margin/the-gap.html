The Gap

in print · The Contract · plate i

No contract can foresee everything. The contingencies the future holds are without number, language is too coarse to name them all, and the cost of writing them down would swallow the deal — so every contract, however long, is incomplete, a sketch with gaps where the unforeseen will fall. This plate sets out the incompleteness of contracts: why the complete agreement is impossible, what the gap is made of, and why the deepest question a contract poses is what it does not say.

The best-laid schemes o' mice an' men gang aft agley. Robert Burns, ‘To a Mouse’, 1785

No contract can foresee everything. The contingencies the future might bring are without number; language is too coarse to name them all, foresight too short to imagine them, and the cost of writing down every possibility would soon exceed the value of the deal. So every contract, however long and however carefully drawn, is unfinished — a sketch with gaps where the unforeseen will one day fall. The gap is not the mark of careless drafting but the permanent condition of agreement, the place where the contract runs out and something else must take over. And because the gap can never be wholly closed, the real question a contract poses is not what its words say, but what is to happen when the world arrives at a place those words did not foresee.

To see why this is so, begin with the ideal it falls short of. Economic theory once imagined a contract that would be complete: one that named, for every possible state of the world, exactly what each party was to do — so much delivered if the harvest failed, so much if it flourished, such a price if war came and such another if peace held, a clause for every contingency the future might unfold. Kenneth Arrow and Gérard Debreu built the most elegant model in all of economics on this premise, showing that if a full set of such contracts existed — one for every good in every state — a market could allocate all the world's risk in a single grand bargain, and nothing would ever need to be revisited. In that imagined world there would be no firms, no courts, no renegotiation, no trust: every future already provided for, every obligation already fixed, the whole of time settled in advance. The complete contract is the benchmark against which every real one is measured, and every real one falls short.

The model is the Arrow-Debreu framework of general equilibrium with complete markets, set out in the 1950s: for every commodity in every state of the world there is a market and a price, so that all risk is allocated in advance and trade need never reopen. Its elegance is also its warning — the conditions it requires, a contract for every contingency, are precisely those the real world cannot supply, and much of the economics of contracts, organizations, and finance is the study of what happens when the Arrow-Debreu markets are missing.

It falls short for three reasons, which deepen as one descends. The first is cost: even where the parties can foresee a contingency and describe it plainly, writing every one of them into the text, and bargaining over the term that should govern each, would consume more than the agreement is worth — so much of the future is at once too unlikely to dwell on and too dear to provide for. The second is language: many contingencies, though imaginable, cannot be set down in words precise enough to be acted upon, for the situations that matter are often subtle, gradual, or compound, and the cruder the description the more it invites dispute. The third and deepest is the mind itself: Herbert Simon taught economics to take seriously that human reason is bounded — that the parties to a contract, however shrewd, can attend to only a handful of the futures the world holds in store, and cannot even enumerate, let alone provide for, the full range of what might come to pass. Cost limits what is worth writing; language limits what can be written; and bounded rationality limits what can even be thought.

The three sources are now standard: the cost of writing and negotiating contingent terms, the difficulty of describing contingencies precisely, and the impossibility of foreseeing them at all. Herbert Simon's bounded rationality, advanced from the 1940s against the assumption of unlimited computational power, supplies the foundation, for it is the boundedness of the contracting mind, not any laziness, that makes the complete contract unattainable. Institutions exist, in part, to cope with what unaided contracts cannot foresee — the insight Ronald Coase brought to the theory of the firm and Oliver Williamson to the study of governance.

The third reason is worth dwelling on, for it marks the difference between a contract that is merely unfinished and one that is unfinishable. A gap left for reasons of cost or language is a gap the parties could have closed had they troubled to; but some gaps could not have been closed at all, because the contingency that falls into them was one no one could have conceived when the contract was signed. The distinction is the one Frank Knight drew between risk and uncertainty: risk is a known list of outcomes whose probabilities one may weigh, but uncertainty is the condition in which the list itself is unknown — in which the future holds states no party has imagined and no language yet describes. A contract written before the railway could not allocate the risks of the railway; one written before the computer could not divide its spoils; and one written today cannot provide for the surprise that will define the decade to come. The deepest gaps in a contract are not the contingencies it failed to cover but the ones it could not have known to cover — the genuinely novel, which arrives without ever having been on anyone's list.

The distinction is Frank Knight's, from Risk, Uncertainty, and Profit (1921): risk denotes outcomes drawn from a known distribution, uncertainty the deeper condition in which the distribution — even the list of possible outcomes — is unknown. Contract theory turns the distinction to its own use, separating contingencies that are merely uncontracted, left out by choice or cost, from those that are genuinely unforeseen, absent from anyone's conception. It is the latter that make incompleteness more than a matter of drafting effort, and that no foresight, however diligent, could have closed.

There is a further obstacle, subtler than foresight, that consigns even well-understood matters to the gap: the problem of proof. A contract is enforced by a third party — a court, an arbitrator — who was not present at the dealing and knows only what can be demonstrated to it. And a great many things the parties themselves can see perfectly well cannot be proved to such a stranger. Whether a worker truly did his best, whether a supplier's effort was diligent or merely adequate, whether a delay was unavoidable or contrived — these the parties observe but cannot verify, and a term that turns on them cannot be enforced, however clearly it is written, because no court could ever determine whether it had been met. The distinction between what is observable to the parties and what is verifiable to a court is one of the pillars of the modern theory of contracts, for it means that even the foreseeable and the describable may be uncontractable, left to the gap not because no one saw the contingency coming but because no one could prove, afterward, which contingency had come. What cannot be verified cannot be written; and so it joins the silence.

The observable-but-not-verifiable distinction is central to the incomplete-contracts theory of Sanford Grossman, Oliver Hart, and John Moore. A variable is observable if the parties can see it, verifiable if a court can confirm it; only verifiable variables can be made into an enforceable term. Effort, quality, and good faith are the classic examples of the observable yet unverifiable — plain to the parties, opaque to the court. The recognition that general rules cannot foresee every particular is ancient: Aristotle made it the basis of equity, the correction of a law where its universal terms fail a case the lawgiver did not foresee.

And so the contract, which presents itself as a full and settled specification of the bargain — the terms all written, the price all fixed, the obligations all clear — is in truth a small island of the explicit in a sea of the unaddressed. A price is a verdict on a hidden contest, mistaken for a fact of nature; and the terms a contract sets down, which wear the look of a complete and final accounting, are a verdict of exactly that kind — a settlement rendered over a vast silence, taken for the whole of the agreement only because it is the part that was written. The writtenness is the source of the illusion. What is set down in black letter seems, by the mere fact of being set down, to exhaust the deal; but it exhausts only what the parties could foresee, describe, and prove, which is a small and shrinking province of all that will come to bear on them. The rest — the unforeseen, the indescribable, the unverifiable — is the gap, and the gap is not a margin at the edge of the contract but the greater part of its substance, the dark matter around the bright and visible terms.

The point is that the explicit terms are not merely incomplete but unrepresentative: they cover the foreseeable, describable, verifiable fraction of the relationship, which systematically excludes the contingencies most likely to matter — the novel, the subtle, the unprovable. To read the written terms as the agreement is to mistake the lit portion of the room for the room. The writtenness lends the terms a false air of finality, as though what is fixed in black letter must be the whole of what was agreed, when it is only the part that could be.

It will be objected that this makes too much of a manageable thing. Sophisticated contracts, the objection runs, provide for far more than the skeptic allows: financial instruments specify their payoffs across thousands of states; commercial agreements run to hundreds of pages of careful contingency; force-majeure clauses sweep up the unforeseen in a single catch-all, and broad standards — to use best efforts, to act in good faith, to behave reasonably — delegate the filling of gaps to courts and arbitrators in advance. A contract need not be complete, the argument concludes, only complete enough, and a skilled drafter can come remarkably close. And there is a sharper, more theoretical objection, pressed by Eric Maskin and Jean Tirole: that even genuinely unforeseen contingencies may not matter, because rational parties can design clever mechanisms — games of revelation played after the fact — that elicit whatever the situation reveals and implement the outcome they would have chosen had they foreseen it. If incompleteness can be engineered around, then the gap, however real, may have no consequences worth the name.

The practical objection points to real craft: catch-all clauses, broad standards, and delegation to arbitration genuinely extend a contract's reach. The theoretical objection is the Maskin-Tirole critique of 1999, which argued that if parties are sufficiently rational, the inability to foresee or describe contingencies need not reduce the outcomes they can achieve, since a suitable mechanism can implement them regardless. Grossman, Hart, and Moore replied that the critique assumes away renegotiation and the very bounds on rationality that motivate the theory — a dispute over whether incompleteness has bite that remains, in the formal literature, unsettled.

The objection mistakes the management of the gap for its closing. A force-majeure clause does not specify what happens when the unforeseen strikes; it names who shall bear the loss, or hands the question to a judge — an admission that the contingency could not be provided for, dressed as a provision. A duty of good faith, a standard of best efforts, an agreement to arbitrate: each is not a filling of the gap but a decision about who shall fill it when it opens, and on what principle they shall do so. These devices are the architecture of incompleteness, not its abolition; they presuppose the gap they are built to manage, and the more a contract leans on them the more openly it confesses how much it cannot say. As for the theoretical objection, the mechanisms it relies upon require what real contracting most conspicuously lacks — unbounded rationality to design them, perfect commitment never to renegotiate them, and a court able to verify their workings — so that the critique, ingenious on its own terms, dissolves precisely where bounded parties, tempted to renegotiate, deal before a court that cannot see inside their relationship. The gap survives every attempt to define it away, because it is not a flaw in draftsmanship but a fact about the relation between finite minds and an open future.

The distinction is between specifying an outcome and allocating a decision. A force-majeure clause or a good-faith standard does not say what shall happen; it says who shall decide, or who shall bear the loss, when the unforeseen arrives — which is gap-management, not gap-closing, and so is evidence of incompleteness rather than a refutation of it. The mechanisms of the theoretical critique, in turn, require unbounded rationality, commitment against renegotiation, and verifiability — the three things real contracting lacks — so the gap reappears the moment the idealizing assumptions are dropped.

The incompleteness of contracts, then, is not a defect that better lawyering will one day cure; it is the permanent condition of any agreement struck by parties who cannot see what is coming. A price is a verdict on a hidden contest, mistaken for a fact of nature; and because no contract can foresee everything, the terms it fixes are never the whole of it but a verdict rendered over a silence that no writing can fill. To read a contract, therefore, is to attend less to what it says than to what it cannot — to find the gap, which is where the unforeseen will arrive, the indescribable will lodge, and the unprovable will hide, and to ask what is to become of all that the words left out. The completeness of the written deal is the first illusion the study of contracts must dispel. It conceals a question the terms themselves cannot answer and the next plate must take up: if the gap is always there, and always larger than the text, then what governs it — and who decides, when the contract falls silent, what the silence shall mean?

The discipline is to read a contract by its silences. The terms tell what the parties could foresee, describe, and prove; the gaps tell where the real hazards lie, since it is into the gap that the unforeseen falls and the disputes that matter erupt. The practical lawyer knows this — that the fight is rarely over what the contract says and usually over what it failed to — and the theorist formalizes it: the design of a contract is less the specification of terms than the allocation of authority over everything the terms could not reach, the subject of the plate that follows.

No contract can foresee everything, so every contract is a sketch over a silence. To read one, study not what it says, but what it could not.

What becomes of the silence is the burden of the plate that follows. For a gap, once it opens, does not stay empty: when the unforeseen arrives and the words give out, someone must decide what is to be done, and the someone who decides holds, in that moment, the whole power of the relationship. The gap is the question; the answer is residual control, the right to fill the silence; and the answer, the next plate will argue, is not handed down by the meeting of minds the law imagines but seized by the distribution of power the contract conceals. The deal is a sketch. The gap is the rest. And the rest is where the contest lives. The argument remains open.

The first plate of Volume XV, The Contract, and the foundation on which the volume is built: that no contract can foresee everything, so that every contract is incomplete. Where classical theory imagined the complete contingent agreement — a clause for every state of the world, modeled by Arrow and Debreu — this plate sets out why no such contract can be written: the cost of providing for every contingency, the coarseness of language, and, deepest of all, the bounded rationality Herbert Simon placed at the center of economic life, which leaves the genuinely unforeseen — Knight's uncertainty, as against mere risk — beyond anyone's power to anticipate. To these is added the problem of proof, the gap between what the parties can observe and what a court can verify, which consigns even the foreseeable to the silence. The incompleteness is permanent, and the institutions that manage it — the firm Coase explained, the governance Williamson mapped, the equity by which Aristotle had courts fill the cases a lawgiver could not foresee — only confirm it.
