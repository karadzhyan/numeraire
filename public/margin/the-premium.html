The Premium

in print · № IV · 2 of 12 plates

The premium is the price of risk: the extra return that must be offered to induce anyone to bear an uncertain prospect rather than a sure one. This volume anatomizes it — the aversion that makes the cautious pay to shed risk and the bold demand to be paid for bearing it, the insurance and diversification that tame it, the equity premium that puzzles those who measure it. Its question is the oldest one about every great fortune: how much of the reward for bearing risk is the wage of courage and skill, and how much the mere luck of having survived.

The question of the volume the premium looks like the just wage of courage, the fee the fearful pay the brave — but how much of a great fortune is the reward for skill, and how much the mere luck of having borne risk and survived?
The argument

Every price the table has weighed so far has been the price of something one can point to — an hour of labor, an acre of land, a year of waiting. The price this volume weighs is stranger, for its object cannot be pointed to at all: it is the price of not knowing. Risk is the shadow across every economic act whose reward lies in the future — nearly all of them. The merchant who ships across an ocean, the farmer who sows before the weather is known, the investor who buys a share — each commits the present to a future he cannot see, and must reckon with the chance that it will disappoint. A prospect that might pay much and might pay nothing is worth less than a certainty of the same average value; and the difference is the premium, the extra return that makes a person bear uncertainty rather than flee it. This volume is the anatomy of that premium — the price of bearing what cannot be foreseen.

The premium shows two faces. Most people will pay to be rid of uncertainty, surrendering a small certain sum to escape a large uncertain loss: that is the principle of insurance, a fixed yearly payment to a company that makes the household whole if the rare disaster strikes. And most must be paid to take uncertainty on, demanding a higher return for a riskier prospect: that is why a speculative bond yields more than a government's, and why, over a long run, the bold tend to end richer than the timid. Both express one fact — that risk is a cost, a thing people will spend to avoid and must be paid to accept. The mathematics began with gamblers: Pascal and Fermat, dividing the stakes of an interrupted game, founded the calculus of probability and with it the expected value, the average of the possible outcomes each weighted by its chance. Yet people plainly do not maximize expected value — or no one would buy insurance at a price designed, by its nature, to exceed the expected loss.

The reason was supplied by Daniel Bernoulli in 1738. A sure thousand, he saw, is worth more than an even chance at two thousand, because the satisfaction money brings rises slower than the money itself: the second thousand adds less than the first, so the gain from doubling is outweighed by the equal chance of nothing. From this one insight — the diminishing marginal utility of wealth — flows the whole theory of risk-aversion. A risk-averse person values a gamble below its expected value, and the amount he marks it down is the premium he will pay to avoid it. Around that price an architecture has grown: insurers who pool thousands of independent risks until, by the law of large numbers, the average becomes nearly certain; investors who diversify so that failures cancel successes; markets in which the reward for risky shares over safe bonds has, in the record, puzzled those who measure it. Risk can be pooled, spread, and traded; but it cannot be destroyed, only moved, and at every move it carries its price.

All of this presumes the odds are known — that they can be set as a number, however rough. Frank Knight, in 1921, drew the distinction that has shadowed the subject since. Risk proper is measurable: the odds can be estimated, the danger insured, the premium calculated. But there is a deeper not-knowing — true uncertainty — where the odds themselves are unknown and unknowable, and no frequency can be assigned. The genuinely novel venture, the unprecedented crisis, the technology that has never existed: these cannot be insured, because they cannot be priced, and whoever bears them bears something no premium was set to cover. Knight made this the very source of profit — the entrepreneur's reward as the compensation for an uncertainty that could not be reduced to a calculable risk. Keynes said it more bluntly of the things that matter most: about the prospect of a war, or the rate of interest twenty years hence, we simply do not know. The premium for measurable risk can be computed; the premium for uncertainty is whatever the bold can be induced to accept, and the event, in the end, allows them to keep.

And here the volume's question opens. The premium presents itself as the most reasonable of prices, even the most admirable: it is what the cautious pay the courageous to carry a burden they would shun — the price at which fear hires nerve. But a price is a verdict on a hidden contest, mistaken for a fact of nature; and the price of risk, beneath its look of a simple fee for a simple service, settles several contests at once. The first is over who actually bears the risk, as against who collects the premium for it — very often not the same. The second is over the line, never cleanly drawn, between the skill that earns a reward and the luck that merely received one. The third is over who, in a society, is in a position to bear risk at all, and who is forced instead to pay others to bear it for them. The premium looks like the wage of courage; it may be something nearer a toll, levied by those who can afford to gamble upon those who cannot.

Consider the second contest, for it presses hardest on the title-page's question: how much of a great fortune is courage, and how much is luck? The premium is paid, in the end, to those who bore a risk and survived — and survival, in a world of chance, is not the same as wisdom. For every bold wager that founded an empire, a hundred just as bold were ruined; and the ruined write no memoirs, so we see only the survivors and mistake the selection for a verdict on their gifts. A price is a verdict on a hidden contest, mistaken for a fact of nature; and the price of risk, read off the fortunes of those who lived to enjoy it, cannot easily tell the genius who foresaw from the gambler who was spared. Nor is risk-bearing open to all alike: the rich can afford to be brave, having a cushion against the bad outcome, while the poor cannot, for a single failure ruins them — so they buy safety, and the premium they forgo goes to those whose wealth lets them outlast the variance. The price of risk flows, quietly, from those who cannot bear it to those who can.

None of this means the premium is unearned, or that risk-bearing is a fraud. Someone must carry the future's uncertainty, and carrying it is a genuine cost: the sleepless proprietor who has staked everything, the investor who watches a fortune swing, the insurer who may be ruined by a single catastrophe all bear a real weight, and a society that offered them nothing for it would find its ships unsailed and its ventures unfunded. Skill in bearing risk is real, too — the judgment to tell a sound gamble from a reckless one, the nerve to hold when others flee, are not nothing, and the steadiest fortunes are less often the luckiest than the best managed. The premium does necessary work, calling forth the risk-bearing a productive economy cannot do without. The argument of this volume is not that the premium is undeserved, but that it is a verdict — that beneath the clean price of a clean service lies the unsettled question of who bore the risk, who was skilled and who was spared, and who could afford to play at all.

The twelve plates trace the price of risk from concept to conscience. The Peril opens with the price itself; The Fortune asks the question beneath it, of skill, luck, and the survivors who cannot tell them apart. The Odds sets out the calculus of probability the gamblers bequeathed, and The Aversion the diminishing utility by which Bernoulli explained why we shun a fair bet. The Policy anatomizes the pooling that tames the average, The Hedge the diversification that cancels risk for free, The Equity the puzzle of why market risk has paid so well. The Tail weighs the rare catastrophe the everyday premium forgets, The Uncertainty the Knightian not-knowing no premium can cover, The Bearer the gap between who holds the risk and who is paid for it. The Bubble examines risk mispriced by the madness of crowds, and The Reckoning closes on the deepest question: how a just society should share the risks no one can escape and someone must bear. Other volumes border this one — the rate, whose discount on the future is risk's near twin; the utility, whose diminishing returns explain the aversion; the stock, whose cushion decides who can bear risk and who must pay to be spared it. Through them all runs one suspicion: that the price of courage and the price of luck are harder to tell apart than the world's fortunes would have us believe. The argument remains open.

Volume IV, The Premium, opens with two plates in print — The Peril and The Fortune, the price of risk and the contest over whether bearing it is rewarded for skill or for luck. The fourth element of the table, and the twenty-second of its volumes to appear; it joins The Wage, The Rent, and The Rate among the prices — the wage the price of labor, the rent the return to ownership, the rate the price of time, and now the premium the price of risk. It borders The Rate most closely, for the discount the future suffers for being uncertain is the near twin of the discount it suffers for being late; The Utility, whose diminishing marginal returns explain the aversion that sets the premium; and The Stock, the cushion of wealth that decides who can afford to bear risk and who must pay to be spared it. The thesis is that the premium, which wears the look of the just wage of courage, is in truth a verdict — on who really bears the risk and who is merely paid for it, on the skill that foresaw and the luck that was spared, and on who could afford to gamble at all.
