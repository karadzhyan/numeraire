The Single Seller

in print · The Power · plate ii

The monopolist is the figure power first calls to mind — the single seller of a thing with no substitute, free to name his price within the bounds of what buyers will bear. This plate asks where such a creature comes from, what it truly costs beyond the markup, and why the easy verdict against it will not quite hold: for monopoly is sometimes the cheapest way to supply us, sometimes the price we pay for invention, and its remedies are a menu of disappointments.

Thales hired all the olive-presses, no one bidding against him; and when the harvest came, and they were wanted all at once, he let them out at any rate he pleased. Aristotle, Politics, Book I · c. 350 bce

The monopolist is the figure the word power first calls to mind, and rightly, for he is power in its purest and least complicated form: the single seller of a thing for which there is no substitute, facing the whole demand of the market alone. He does not, as the cartoon has it, simply charge what he likes — even he is bound by what buyers will pay, and the higher his price the fewer of them will pay it. What he can do, and the price-taker cannot, is choose the point on that demand which suits him best: hold back the quantity, let the scarcity he has manufactured lift the price, and pocket the difference between that price and the cost of the last unit made. The loss to everyone else is not merely that they pay more. It is that trades which would have benefited both sides simply do not occur, because the seller finds it more profitable to leave them undone.

The monopolist, then, is the price-maker grown to his full and final size — and the first question the figure raises is where such a creature comes from, for single sellers do not spring up by chance. They are made, and made in three broad ways. Some are the gift of nature, or rather of cost: industries in which one firm can supply the whole market more cheaply than several, so that competition would be sheer waste. Some are the gift of the state, conferred on purpose: the patent that grants an inventor a monopoly for a term of years, the franchise that hands one company a city's water or its rails. And some are seized — by cornering a scarce resource, or holding the one bridge, the one mine, the one pass that all must use. The three are worth keeping apart from the start, for they are not equally to be deplored: the seized monopoly is the robber of the textbooks, but the natural and the legal monopoly are, in their different ways, designs — arrangements a sensible society might choose, and sometimes must.

The word is Aristotle's, near enough: from monos and pōlein, alone to sell. He tells, in the Politics, how Thales of Miletus, taunted that his philosophy had left him poor, quietly leased every olive-press in the district one winter, and when the harvest came and the presses were all at once in demand, let them out at whatever price he liked. The first monopolist in Western letters is a philosopher proving a point; and the point — that whoever corners the supply may name the terms — is the whole of what follows.

The natural monopoly deserves the closer look, because it holds the whole volume's difficulty in a single case. Consider the supply of water to a city. To serve it at all requires a vast and costly network of mains beneath the streets; but once the network is laid, the cost of serving one more household is slight, and falls, reckoned per household, the more households are served. A firm that serves the whole city thus delivers water more cheaply than two firms each serving half and each digging up the streets — and far more cheaply than ten. Here competition is not merely difficult but positively wasteful: the efficient number of suppliers is one. And there lies the trap the volume keeps stepping into, for the very scale that makes the single supplier the cheapest also makes him a monopolist, free to charge whatever the captive city will bear. The efficiency that justifies the monopoly and the power that condemns it are not two facts to be weighed against each other; they are one fact seen from two sides.

A natural monopoly is one the cost curve creates. Where the expense of serving a market falls the more of it a single firm serves — one set of pipes, one grid of wires, one bed of rail laid once and used by all — a single supplier is genuinely cheaper than several, and competition would only duplicate the costly works to no purpose. Here monopoly is not a crime but an efficiency; and that is exactly the difficulty, for the same scale that makes one firm the cheapest also leaves it answerable to no one.

What the single seller costs us we have partly seen, and need only name again before going past it. There is the deadweight loss, the trades killed by a price held above the cost of one more; and there is the transfer, the surplus marched out of the buyers' pockets and into the seller's. But monopoly exacts a third cost that the diagrams of the first plate do not show, and it may be the heaviest of the three. A firm that fears no rival is under no discipline to keep itself lean. Its costs are permitted to drift upward, its managers to grow comfortable, its products to age; the spur that competition applies — improve or perish — is simply absent, and a slackness sets in that wastes resources no triangle on a graph will ever catch. The loss here is not the bargain forgone but the excellence never attempted: the firm that, secure in its position, never troubles to be as good as it might.

There is a cost of monopoly the welfare triangle misses entirely. The firm that fears no rival need not strain to keep its costs low, its managers sharp, its methods current; slack creeps in, and the waste is not the trade forgone but the effort not made. Leibenstein called this X-inefficiency, the looseness of the unpushed firm. Hicks had put it more memorably a generation earlier, observing that the truest reward of monopoly is not the extra profit but the quiet life it buys.

And yet — for the volume is bound to give power its due — there is a case for the single seller, and it is among the most important in all of economics. It runs through innovation. The competitive firm, earning just enough to cover its costs, has neither the surplus to fund invention nor much reason to attempt it, since any improvement is instantly copied by its rivals and the advantage competed away. The prospect of a monopoly changes everything: the inventor who knows that success will buy him, for a time, a market of his own will brave the expense and the risk that no price-taker would. Schumpeter built his whole vision of capitalism on this insight — that the competition which counts is not the haggling over a penny but the perennial gale of creative destruction, in which each temporary monopoly is the prize that lures the next inventor to topple it. The patent system is that argument cast into law: a monopoly deliberately granted, for a fixed term, as the price a society agrees to pay for being invented for. Static efficiency says break every monopoly; dynamic progress says that some monopoly is the engine of everything. Between the two the subject has never struck a final balance.

Against the whole indictment stands Schumpeter's reversal. The competition that matters, he held, is not the textbook's haggling over price but the gale of creative destruction — the new product, the new method, that sweeps the old monopolist away — and the prospect of a monopoly, however brief, is precisely what summons the innovator to the labor and the risk. The patent is this argument made law: a monopoly granted on purpose, for a term of years, as the price a society pays to be invented for. Whether the price is worth it is the standing question of the system.

There remains the monopolist's subtlest art, the one promised at the outset, and it overturns a tidy assumption of the first plate. There we supposed the seller posts a single price, the same to all comers, and on that supposition the deadweight loss is unavoidable — the units priced out of reach simply go unsold. But suppose the seller can tell his buyers apart and charge each the most that he, particularly, will pay: the rich buyer a high price, the poor buyer a low one, each just below the point at which he would walk away. Now the seller makes the sales he formerly lost — the poor buyer, offered a low enough price, buys after all — and the deadweight loss shrinks, even vanishes, the output swelling back toward the competitive level. Discrimination is, by the efficiency test, an improvement. But every penny of the surplus those trades create is captured by the seller, for each buyer is charged exactly his limit and left with nothing. Here efficiency and exploitation, which the simple model holds apart, walk arm in arm: the more perfectly the monopolist discriminates, the more trades occur, and the more completely the buyers are stripped.

To charge every buyer the same price is to leave money on the table — surplus in the hands of those who would have paid more. The monopolist who can sort his buyers and charge each the most he will bear escapes this, and Pigou sorted the sorting into three degrees: the first, a separate price for every buyer; the second, a schedule by quantity or version; the third, a different price for each identifiable group. Perfect discrimination has a startling property — it restores the competitive quantity, every willing trade made, while handing the whole of the gain to the seller.

What was once a textbook curiosity — perfect discrimination, charging each buyer his private maximum — is fast becoming an industry. To charge each buyer his limit, one must know each buyer's limit, and for most of history no seller could; the single posted price endured in part because ignorance enforced it, and a posted price has at least the rough virtue of charging the duke and the pauper alike. The data economy dissolves the ignorance. A seller who knows what you have searched and bought, what device you carry, what you can afford and how long you wavered, can approach a price set for you alone — and the uniform ticket gives way to a quiet, individuated reckoning that no one else can see in order to compare. The power to discriminate is the power to know; and the knowing, and the watching that feeds it, return in the plate on the platform.

The first-degree discrimination economists once treated as an impossible limit, for want of knowledge, the data economy approaches in earnest. A seller who knows what you searched, what you bought, what you can afford, and how long you hesitated can come close to setting a price for you alone — the most that you, particularly, will bear. The single posted ticket, which charged the duke and the pauper alike, gives way to a price tailored in the dark; and the surveillance that tailors it is taken up again in the plate on the platform.

If the single seller is sometimes the cheapest way to supply us, he cannot simply be abolished, and the question becomes the awkward one of what to do with a monopoly one cannot do without. The textbook offers a menu, and every dish disappoints. One may regulate him — set his price by public authority — but the efficient price, equal to the cost of one more, will not cover the great fixed cost of his network, so the regulator must either subsidize the loss or permit a higher price and surrender the very efficiency he came to secure; and he must do it knowing far less of the firm's true costs than the firm itself, and at constant risk of being captured by it. One may nationalize him, and exchange the slackness of the private monopolist for the slackness of the public one. One may, with Demsetz, auction the franchise to whoever will serve cheapest, putting competition for the market in place of competition within it — though the winner, once enthroned, is a monopolist like any other. Or one may simply tolerate him, and trust that the fear of some distant rival keeps him honest. There is no clean answer, only a choice among flawed ones, because the thing itself is a knot: a power that is also an efficiency, and cannot be undone without undoing both.

None of the cures is clean. Set the regulated price at marginal cost, the efficient level, and the natural monopolist — whose average cost is still falling — cannot cover his outlay and goes broke; set it at average cost and the efficiency is lost. Hand the thing to the state and you trade private slack for public. Demsetz proposed auctioning the franchise — competition for the market in place of competition in it — but the winner, once installed, is a monopolist still. And every regulator, as a later plate records, is liable to capture by the firm he watches.

Strip away the cases and the cures, and the single seller stands as the plainest illustration the volume affords of its governing thought. His price is the price-maker's carried to the limit: chosen, not found; bounded by nothing but what the buyer will bear; owing nothing to the cost of one more or to the pressure of any rival, because he has none. It is, of all prices, the one with the least of the fact of nature in it and the most of the naked verdict — the contest at its most one-sided, the strong wholly strong and the buyer with truly nowhere else to go. And still it is posted on the shelf in the same plain numerals as the competitive price beside it, and still the buyer, reading the tag, cannot see which kind of price he pays. Monopoly does not change how a price looks. It changes only everything behind it.

The single seller is the price-maker of the first plate carried to his limit. His demand is not a slice of the market's but the whole of it; his markup is bounded by nothing but what buyers will bear; and the gap between his price and the cost of one more — the measure of power — is as wide as the case allows. If the competitive price is a fact no one chose, the monopoly price is the opposite pole: a figure almost wholly chosen, a verdict with scarcely any fact left in it, and yet posted in the selfsame numerals.

It would be the simplest thing to end there, with the single seller condemned, and much of the time the condemnation is just. But the plate has turned up too many complications for so clean a verdict. The monopoly may be the cheapest way to bring us water or power, so that breaking it would cost us more than bearing it. It may be the deliberate and time-limited reward we have promised our inventors, the price of the cures and the engines that competition alone would never have funded. Its discrimination may, even as it strips us, conjure trades a single price would have denied. The single seller is therefore not merely the villain of the piece but the sharpest form of the question the whole volume turns upon: whether power in a market is an aberration to be hunted down and broken, or a permanent feature of the economic world — sometimes wasteful, sometimes the price of progress, sometimes the only efficient way — that the law and the citizen may hope to manage but never finally abolish.

The single seller is the contest at its most one-sided — and the proof that a power worth condemning may also be a power we cannot do without.

The single seller is where the study of power begins in earnest, because he is power with nothing else mixed in — no rival to share the market, no competition to blur the picture, only one will facing the demand of all. The plates that follow reintroduce the complications the monopolist strips away: the few who must reckon with one another, the many made a little powerful by their differences, the barriers that keep the position safe, the law that assails it. But each is a departure from the pure case set out here. A price is a verdict on a hidden contest, mistaken for a fact of nature; and in the monopolist we meet the contest with only one fighter left standing, handing down the plainest verdict of all — which we may yet, on reflection, decide not wholly to overturn. The argument remains open.
