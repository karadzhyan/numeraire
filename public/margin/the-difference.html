The Difference

in print · The Rent · plate i

Why does the owner of a field receive a payment for land he did nothing to make? Ricardo's answer founded the modern theory of rent: that rent is a child of difference — the surplus the better land yields over the worst land in use, bid away to the owner by competing farmers. This plate sets out that theory in full, and the inversion at its heart: that rent does not raise the price of corn, but the price of corn raises the rent.

Rent is that portion of the produce of the earth which is paid to the landlord for the use of the original and indestructible powers of the soil. David Ricardo, On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation · 1817

There is a question that sounds simple and is not: why does the owner of a field, who may never have set foot on it, receive a payment for the use of land he did nothing to make? The classical economists worried at it for a century, and it was David Ricardo who gave the answer that has shaped every discussion since. Rent, he saw, is a child of difference. Were all land equally fertile and equally placed, and were there enough of it, none would command a price at all; it is because the good land is scarce, and the poorer land must be pressed into use to feed a growing population, that the better land yields a surplus over the worst — and that surplus, bid for by those who would farm it, becomes the rent the owner pockets for having got there first.

That the question was hard is plain from how long it resisted answer. The earliest systematic economists, the physiocrats of France, had made rent the hinge of their whole system: land, they held, was the one truly productive thing, the sole source of a surplus over costs, and its rent the net product on which kings and merchants and all the rest ultimately fed. Adam Smith, a generation later, was less reverent. He saw in rent a kind of monopoly price, the charge a landlord could exact simply because the land was his and others needed it, and remarked drily that proprietors, like the rest of mankind, are fond of reaping where they have not sown. But Smith never quite got the mechanism straight, wavering — treating rent in one breath as a consequence of price and in the next as a cause of it, a cost that drove the price of produce up. The confusion sat at the heart of the subject, and clearing it would make Ricardo's name.

Rent was the first puzzle the new science set itself. The physiocrats of eighteenth-century France made it the center of everything, holding that land alone was truly productive and its rent the net product from which all wealth flowed. Adam Smith was warier: he saw rent as a kind of monopoly price, and remarked that landlords, like other men, love to reap where they never sowed — yet he muddled the matter by treating rent at times as a cause of high prices rather than a result of them. It was the muddle Ricardo set out to clear.

Ricardo's insight was that rent is not a payment the land exacts but a difference the land reveals. Imagine, he said, a newly settled country with land of every grade and few people to work it. Only the best land is farmed, for there is more than enough; and because it is not scarce, no one pays to use it — a man who asked rent for his field would be left with it idle, while his neighbor tilled land just as good for nothing. Now let the population grow. The best land no longer suffices, inferior land must be broken to feed the increase, and at once the best land reveals its advantage, yielding more corn for the same labor than the poorer ground now at the margin. Farmers, preferring the better land, bid against one another for the right to it, and their bidding rises until it has skimmed off the whole of its advantage. That skimmed-off difference — between the good land and the worst land in use — is rent, and it falls to the owner not for anything he has done but for what he happens to hold.

Picture lands of descending quality, and a people that grows. While the best land suffices, none is scarce and none pays rent. But as mouths multiply and second-rate, then third-rate ground must be tilled, the best land comes to yield, for the same labor and outlay, a markedly larger crop than the worst land still worth working. That margin of advantage is what the better land's would-be tenants compete away in their bids for it; and the winning bid, the surplus over the poorest land in use, is handed to the owner as rent. Rent is the measure of the difference.

The same principle, Ricardo showed, works not only across fields but within one. A farmer with a good acre does not till it once over; he applies labor and capital in successive doses, and each dose, by the nature of the soil, returns a little less than the last. He keeps adding until he reaches a dose that barely repays itself — yielding only its own costs and the ordinary profit, nothing beyond; to go further would be to lose. That last dose, which just breaks even, is the intensive margin, and like the worst land in use it pays no rent. Everything the earlier, more productive doses yield above it is surplus, and that surplus is rent. By this Ricardo could define the thing exactly: rent is the payment made to the landlord for the use of the original and indestructible powers of the soil — the land's inherent capacity to yield more than the margin, a capacity the owner neither created nor can destroy.

The same logic works downward into a single field. A farmer adds labor and capital to his acre dose by dose, each yielding a little less than the one before, until the last is barely worth applying — returning only its costs and the ordinary profit, and no more. That final dose pays no rent; everything the earlier, more productive doses yield above it does. So whether one spreads cultivation outward to poorer land or drives it deeper into better, rent is always the surplus over the dose that just breaks even — the return, as Ricardo put it, to the original and indestructible powers of the soil.

From this followed the conclusion that overturned the common understanding, and it is the analytical heart of the theory. Ask an ordinary man why bread is dear, and he may say it is because the farmer must pay so much rent for his land and passes the charge along. Ricardo saw that this puts the matter backward. The price of corn is not set by the rent of the good land; it is set at the margin, by what it costs to raise corn on the worst land in cultivation — the land that pays no rent, and whose grain must fetch enough to cover only its labor and profit. That marginal price is the price all corn commands, the good land's included; and it is precisely that price, high enough to make the poor land worth tilling, that lets the good land yield its surplus — which becomes the rent. Rent is not a cause of the high price but a consequence of it. Corn is not dear because a rent is paid; a rent is paid because corn is dear.

Here lies the reversal that made Ricardo's name. Common sense says the farmer must charge more for his corn because he must pay rent for his field — rent as a cost, passed on in the price. Ricardo saw it backward. The price of corn is fixed at the margin, by the cost of growing it on the worst land, which pays no rent at all; the high price is what then permits the better land to throw off a surplus, and that surplus becomes the rent. Rent does not raise the price of corn; the price of corn, by calling poor land into use, raises the rent.

Ricardo made this the engine of a whole grim account of where an economy is tending. As a people grows and cultivation creeps down to ever-poorer soils, the gap between the best land and the marginal land widens without cease, and rent climbs with it. Wages are held near the cost of subsistence. Profit is left to absorb the squeeze from both sides, falling as rent rises, until the day it is too thin to tempt further accumulation and the economy grinds into what Ricardo called the stationary state. In this long decline one party alone grows steadily richer — the landlord, whose rents swell with every fresh acre of bad land broken to feed the crowd. His interest, Ricardo wrote in a phrase that scandalized the propertied, is always opposed to the interest of every other class in the community. Nor was this academic: it was a charge laid against the Corn Laws, whose tariffs walled out foreign grain, kept its price high, and so fattened the rents of English landlords at the expense of all who ate.

From this Ricardo drew a grim arithmetic of growth. As population presses on the land and cultivation crawls down to ever-poorer soils, the differential widens, and rent swells; wages are pinned near subsistence; and profit, caught between the two, is slowly squeezed toward the vanishing point where accumulation halts — the stationary state. The landlord alone gains from the whole sad progress, his interest, Ricardo wrote, forever opposed to that of every other class. It was no idle theorem: it was a weapon against the Corn Laws, whose tariffs propped up the price of grain and the rents that fed upon it.

The cornfield was only the clearest case of a principle reaching far past it. Wherever a wanted thing comes in grades — a mineral lode richer than the next, a shop better placed than its rivals, a singer the public prefers to a hundred others — the superior specimen earns, over the marginal one barely worth employing, exactly the differential surplus Ricardo had found in land; later economists would treat the rent of land as merely the plainest instance of a phenomenon running through the whole economy, and Marshall gave its short-lived form a name, the quasi-rent. The theory has weak joints. The no-rent margin, so tidy in the model, is hard to find in an old and crowded country, where no spare land goes begging and every acre fetches some price. And the powers of the soil are not always so original as the phrase implied, for drainage and marling and long manuring can make poor land good — so that some of what is collected as rent is really the return on capital sunk into the ground, a wage of past investment masquerading as the gift of nature.

The theory outgrew the cornfield. Any factor that comes in grades of scarcity — a richer seam, a busier corner, a rarer gift — earns the same differential surplus over the marginal specimen of its kind, and Marshall would later treat land rent as merely the clearest case of a general thing, christening its temporary cousin the quasi-rent. The doctrine has its softnesses: the no-rent margin is a fiction in any old and crowded country, where every acre fetches something; and the soil's powers are not always original, since drainage and dunging improve them, so that part of what looks like rent is really the return on a buried investment.

Beneath the mechanism lies the matter this table everywhere pursues, and in rent it shows with unusual nakedness. Consider what the landlord has actually done to earn his rent. He has not made the good land fertile; its powers are, by Ricardo's own account, original and indestructible, there before him and owing nothing to him. He has not placed it by the road or the river. He has not summoned the growing population whose hunger bids up its worth. He has done one thing only: he holds the title. His rent is therefore not the price of any service he renders but a toll levied on a scarcity he commands — the surplus that difference and crowding throw off, collected by whoever owns the advantaged ground. And the market presents this toll in the bland disguise all such tolls wear: as the going rate for the use of land, a plain fact of supply meeting demand, no more to be questioned than the weather.

Strip the mechanism to its moral and the rent stands revealed. The owner of the good land did nothing to make it good; he neither raised its fertility nor placed it by the river nor brought the hungry population that bid up its worth. His payment is not a wage for any service rendered but a toll on a scarcity he happens to command — the surplus that difference and crowding throw off, collected by whoever holds the title. The market reports it as the going price for the use of land, a plain fact of supply and demand. It is nothing of the kind.

But it is far from a plain fact, and the whole force of Ricardo's demonstration is to show why. A price is a verdict on a hidden contest, mistaken for a fact of nature; and the rent of land is the verdict on the oldest contest there is — over who shall own the scarce good ground that everyone needs and no one made. The owner's rent is the measure of his victory in that contest, dressed up as the natural yield of his property. The man who got there first, or whose ancestors did, or who bought the title from one who did, collects in perpetuity a share of what the land brings forth — not because he adds to the produce but because he can withhold the land until he is paid. Ricardo's theorem, for all its cool geometry of margins and differences, is at bottom an account of just this: of how the mere ownership of the scarce and the superior converts itself, silently and lawfully, into a permanent claim on the wealth that other men's labor produces.

The power that earns the rent is the power to withhold. The landlord need not lift a tool or improve an acre; he need only be able to keep others off the land until they pay him for entry. That is what ownership of a scarce and needed thing confers — not a capacity to produce but a capacity to exclude — and the rent is simply the price of being let past the gate. Ricardo's margins and differences are the geometry of it; the substance is older and simpler, the toll of the gatekeeper on ground he did not make.

What Ricardo achieved was subtler than a defense of rent or an attack upon it: he explained it. He showed, with a rigor the subject had never attained, exactly how the surplus arises, why it grows, and into whose hands it falls — and he did so without pronouncing one word on whether those hands deserve to hold it. The theory is a piece of mechanism, splendid and complete, and like all mechanism it is silent on questions of desert. It tells us that the landlord's rent is a differential surplus born of scarcity and difference; it does not tell us whether a surplus so born is his by right, the community's, or no one's in particular. That is not a flaw in it. It is the boundary of it — the exact point where analysis ends and judgment must begin.

Ricardo explained the rent without justifying it — and an explanation that withholds its blessing is the most unsettling kind.

And judgment, once the mechanism is understood, becomes very hard to avoid. For if the rent of land is a surplus the owner did nothing to create — if it is, in the plainest sense, unearned — then the question the theory so carefully declines to answer presses all the harder to be asked: by what right is it kept, and might it not as justly be reclaimed by those whose presence and labor conjured it? Ricardo, a landowner himself, left the question hanging. Others would not be so delicate. The price of corn is a verdict on the cost of the worst land in use; the rent of the best is a verdict on who owns it; and a verdict, unlike a fact of nature, can always be appealed. The next plate hears the appeal. The argument remains open.
