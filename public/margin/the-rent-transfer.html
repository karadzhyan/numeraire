The Transfer

in print · The Rent · plate iv

The modern definition draws a line through an income: what is necessary to keep the factor in its present use, and what it receives above that amount. But the line depends on the alternative, the market being considered, and the time allowed for a change. This plate follows the comparison through a worker’s options, a supply schedule, a specialized investment and a purchased title. The result is a way to question the necessity of a payment while keeping that question distinct from the value of the service and the recipient’s claim to deserve it.

Every claim that a payment was necessary carries an account of what would happen without it.The argument of this plate, after Marshall

An income arrives as one figure, although it may answer two very different questions. How much must be paid to keep a person or a resource doing what it does? How much can it command because others compete for its services? The difference between the answers is economic rent. The first is the payment necessary to hold the factor in its present use, its transfer earnings; the second is the payment it actually receives. This plate draws the line between them through a wage, a site, and an investment, and finds that the line moves when the alternative moves. It is a precise distinction with a demanding premise: before declaring how much of an income is surplus, one must say what its recipient could do instead.

The alternative is part of the definition and must be stated before the rent can be measured. Imagine a restorer who earns £100,000 a year at one museum and can earn £90,000, on otherwise identical terms, at another. Relative to that employer, £90,000 is required to retain her and £10,000 is rent. Suppose that if she left restoration altogether, the best alternative available to her were a different occupation paying £60,000, with all other attractions and burdens again held equal. Relative to remaining in the profession, the rent is £40,000. The two calculations answer different questions. A policy affecting one museum leaves the other restoration job in view; a change affecting the whole profession may alter it. The same paycheck contains different measured rents because the boundaries of the use have changed.

The restorer and all amounts are invented. The distinction applies the alternative-use reasoning in Marshall, Principles of Economics, eighth edition (1920), V.IX, §§1–3: resources differ in their ability to move between employments, and a local change differs from one covering the whole trade. The term transfer earnings is used here as the modern analytical definition; no claim is made that Marshall coined it.

A fixed supply to the economy can still have a substantial transfer price to the individual who wants to use it. A shopkeeper may have to pay £50,000 for a site because another business can offer that sum; the fact that nobody made the underlying ground does not make the site free to this shopkeeper. If the land could earn £20,000 in its next-best permitted use, that alternative belongs in a calculation of the surplus from its present use. If the inquiry is about the existence of the whole stock of bare land, the calculation has a different boundary again. The principle reaches behind the invoice to the forgone alternative. It does not authorize the tenant to ignore the invoice, or the analyst to count the same opportunity twice.

The generalization frees rent from the landlord without turning every recipient into one. A wage can include a surplus above what would induce the worker to stay; a payment for a machine can exceed what it could earn elsewhere; a firm can receive more than is necessary to retain the resources committed to its activity. These possibilities arise within ordinary productive work. The restorer’s skill and care may be indispensable, and her payment may still exceed the amount required to keep those same services at that museum. To identify that excess is to ask a conditional question about supply. It does not establish that the restorer created no value, that her work lacks merit, or that the employer has a superior claim to the difference. The line passes through an income before anyone tries to use it to divide people.

Marshall, V.IX, §4, describes an element of rent in wages and an element of earnings in the income popularly called rent. His distinction between the components of mixed incomes supports the analytical separation; the judgment of what a person deserves is a further question.

The supply curve can display the distinction, provided its meaning is kept in view. Take an invented group of five people, each offering one identical unit of work. Their minimum acceptable payments for that unit are £20, £30, £40, £50, and £60, and each accepts an offer equal to the minimum. At a uniform payment of £60, all five work and receive £300 in total. Their transfer earnings sum to £200. The remaining £100 is rent: £40 for the first person, then £30, £20, £10, and nothing for the last. In a smooth version of the same case, the area beneath the supply curve measures the payments needed to obtain the units; the area between that curve and the uniform price measures their surplus. The picture is an accounting of specified alternatives, not a photograph of anyone’s mind.

Invented calculation: 5 × £60 = £300; £20 + £30 + £40 + £50 + £60 = £200; rent totals £100. Each reservation payment includes all relevant alternatives and burdens in this example. The generalization follows the earlier Collected Scarcity; this numerical example and its assumptions are new.

The existence of that £100 does not mean that any method of collecting £100 leaves the work unchanged. Cut the uniform payment from £60 to £50 and the fifth person leaves; the others still accept, but only four units are supplied. The fifth person had no rent from which the cut could come. By contrast, if a collector knew every minimum and took half of each person’s rent, the five would retain £40, £45, £50, £55, and £60. Each still receives at least the amount required to supply the unit. These are different interventions in the same example. One crosses a participation threshold; the other has been constructed to avoid every threshold. The theoretical possibility of taking a surplus without changing supply depends on identifying the surplus and on the way the payment is altered.

Outside an invented schedule, a transfer earning includes more than the next wage offer. Hours, uncertainty, travel, danger, autonomy, and the attractions of the work all affect what would keep a person in it. The actor who says he would perform for nothing may mean that he enjoys acting; he has not thereby offered to accept every rehearsal schedule, every insecurity, or every loss of alternative employment without compensation. A machine’s other use may require transport, conversion, or repair. A site’s other use may depend on a permission that cannot presently be obtained. The comparison must preserve what is being purchased and count what must be given up to supply it. Otherwise an apparent rent can be manufactured by setting an unusually complete actual income beside an artificially impoverished alternative.

The five-person example holds work quality and conditions fixed. The broader requirement is opportunity-cost accounting: compare feasible alternatives on like terms. Marshall’s V.VIII, §§1–3 stresses interdependent demands and the net product after additional expenses and incidental savings; V.IX, §1 distinguishes resources that can readily move to another use from those tied to the trade.

Observed choices can reveal something about the alternative without revealing its exact value. A credible competing offer establishes an available possibility; a worker’s refusal to leave at the current wage may help place a bound on what she would require. Neither fact by itself supplies a complete reservation schedule. A worker may stay after a cut because her other offers disappeared at the same time, because moving is costly, or because she expects the cut to be temporary. The relevant evidence concerns those circumstances as well as her action. A measure of rent improves when its counterfactual is explicit enough to be checked against them. Calling the alternative unobserved is a reason to investigate it carefully, and an invitation to state what the comparison holds fixed.

Time changes the alternative as surely as a boundary drawn around a profession does. A press made for one particular kind of printing may earn little outside that trade. Once it has been installed, its owner can rationally accept a payment that covers its current avoidable expenses while leaving much of its construction cost unrecovered. Before it was ordered, the money could have been invested elsewhere. An income measured against the press’s poor present alternative can therefore be a large quasi-rent, while its whole lifetime return scarcely repays the earlier commitment. Marshall’s distinction between capital still free to move and capital already sunk into a particular appliance keeps these questions apart. What holds the old press in service this week is a different amount from what would make building another press worthwhile.

Marshall, V.VIII, §6, and V.IX, §3 and note 87. Past construction costs do not govern the same decision as avoidable current expenses, but anticipated future earnings matter to new investment. No claim is made that every payment above present operating cost is excess profit over the asset’s life.

The same change of viewpoint matters when a person has spent years acquiring a skill. The prospect of a professional income may have helped induce the training, even if the trained person would now remain in the profession at a lower income. That does not convert every subsequent premium into a necessary inducement, any more than the existence of an earlier outlay guarantees that it was worthwhile. It identifies the relevant question. A change confined to the receipts of people already trained and a lasting change understood by those considering training have different possible effects. The former concerns choices after a commitment; the latter concerns choices before it. A serious account of rent must specify which choice it means to leave undisturbed, and for how long.

A payment can also look ordinary to its present recipient because a previous owner sold the expected rent in advance. Suppose a title is expected with certainty to yield £100 a year forever, with no expenses, and the discount rate is five per cent. Its price is £2,000. A buyer paying that price receives an entirely ordinary five-per-cent return. Yet the purchase has not supplied the annual £100 with a new productive explanation: the price of the title was calculated from the income it was expected to confer. Whether that income comes from a natural advantage, an improvement, or an exclusion remains to be examined. A normal return on the purchase price of an existing claim can coexist with a rent in the activity from which the claim draws its income.

The perpetuity is invented: £100 ÷ 0.05 = £2,000, and £100 ÷ £2,000 = 5%. Marshall’s V.IX, §2 and note 84 warns against using a return on the capitalized value of an existing claim as a circular explanation of the income being capitalized. The illustration assumes certainty, a constant discount rate, no costs and perpetual payment.

The alternative may itself bear the mark of an unequal contest. A person with few opportunities can receive a small rent above a very poor fallback and still live badly; another can have a large necessary payment because many employers are competing to hire her. The first person’s willingness to remain does not show that the wage provides a decent life. The second person’s ability to leave does not establish that every institution producing her alternatives is desirable. Transfer earnings take the feasible alternatives as the starting point for a particular comparison. A political inquiry can then ask how those alternatives were made—by education, wealth, exclusion, bargaining rules, or an absence of anywhere else to go. The measured rent and the fairness of the circumstances that measure it are separate questions about the same income.

The line between transfer earnings and rent can be drawn where the alternative is specified, and tested where evidence bears on it. It becomes unreliable when the analyst silently changes the market, the period, or the service being compared, or treats an accounting residual as a verdict on a life. The productive contribution of a person, the amount needed to secure that contribution, and the amount the person deserves are distinct propositions. An artist can create great value while needing a smaller payment to continue; an owner can collect a payment necessary to win a site away from another bidder while the underlying land remains something nobody created. The useful discipline is to hold the proposition steady while following the money. Each further claim then has to show what it adds.

The earlier Collected Boundary supplies the inquiry into the chosen alternative and the distinction between measuring a surplus and judging desert. This plate develops those questions with explicit comparisons; it does not treat a conditional definition as an estimate of the rent contained in any real person’s income.

The payment tells us what someone received. The alternative tells us how much of it was needed to keep the service there.

Rent is the excess, but an excess always requires a comparison. Once the comparison is stated, the concept can disclose a surplus within the most respectable-looking wage or the most ordinary-looking return on an asset. It can also expose how much of an apparent windfall is carrying a cost that the first glance omitted. That is the force of the idea: to make the recipient’s necessity a question rather than accept the whole payment as its own justification. Whether anyone may keep the surplus remains a judgment about rights, institutions, and desert. Whether there is a surplus at all is an inquiry into what would happen if the payment changed. The line can be drawn; its conditions must remain visible. The argument remains open.
