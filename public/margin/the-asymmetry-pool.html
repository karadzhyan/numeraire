As viii

The Pool

in print · The Asymmetry · plate viii

A common premium must cover the people who choose to pay it, and those choices can change the cost of the promise. Following two kinds of applicant through a common offer, separate prices and compulsory membership distinguishes protection against a loss from transfers between risk classes. The pool’s solvency then leaves a further question: which misfortunes should be shared, and who should finance that decision?

A common price depends on who remains to pay it.The argument of this plate, after Rothschild, Stiglitz, Einav and Finkelstein

To insure is to pool risk: the many who pay premiums cover the losses of those whom the event reaches, and each exchanges an uncertain bill for a more predictable obligation. The arrangement depends on the composition of the company assembled beneath it. A premium calculated for a whole population may be inadequate for the people who actually choose to pay. Those people have reasons for coming forward. They know something about their health, their driving, their property or the venture they want protected. The insurer sees an application. The applicant may see a symptom. When that difference affects the decision to buy, the price of the promise helps determine the cost of keeping it.

The eagerness to buy can carry information about the loss the buyer expects. Hold preferences and resources fixed, and a person more likely to suffer a covered loss will ordinarily value the policy more. A common premium can then attract a pool with greater expected claims than the population from which it formed. Raising the premium to cover those claims may drive out the less costly remaining members; their departure raises the average again. This is the logic of adverse selection. Its force comes from the connection between demand and cost: the customer who declines the price also takes a particular risk out of the pool. A sufficiently severe version can destroy an offer that appeared sustainable before anyone had decided whether to accept it.

Liran Einav and Amy Finkelstein, “Selection in Insurance Markets: Theory and Empirics in Pictures” (2011), pp. 116–120. Their initial framework fixes the insurance contract and lets firms compete over its price. A sequence of premium increases is one possible adjustment process; the existence and extent of undercoverage need not depend on that particular sequence.

A small pool makes the selection visible without requiring anyone to behave foolishly. Take the hypothetical applicants of the separate Utility treatment: each begins with wealth of a hundred, and a loss would remove sixty-four. Half face a one-in-ten chance of loss; half face a one-in-two chance. Each evaluates wealth by its square root, so that a certain sum can be preferable to a gamble with the same expected wealth. The low-risk applicant's expected utility without insurance is 9.6, the high-risk applicant's eight. Full insurance leaves wealth equal to a hundred less the premium in either state. The largest premiums the two applicants would pay are therefore 7.84 and thirty-six. These amounts include the value of relief from uncertainty, as well as expected claims.

The common price that would cover both groups fails to recruit both groups. Expected claims are 6.4 for the low-risk applicant and thirty-two for the high-risk applicant. With equal numbers, the average is 19.2. A full-coverage offer at that premium is attractive to the high-risk group and unattractive to the low-risk group. Once only the former buys, its expected cost is thirty-two rather than 19.2. A premium of thirty-two can support that remaining group under the example's assumptions. The low-risk group stays outside even though its members would willingly pay more than their own expected claims to be insured. The loss lies in a mutually valuable protection that the common offer cannot deliver to them.

The numerical applicants are adapted from the later Utility Applicant. With utility √wealth, the uninsured certainty equivalents are 9.6² = 92.16 and 8² = 64; subtracting these from a hundred gives maximum premiums of 7.84 and thirty-six. Loss probabilities are fixed, claims verifiable and insurer administration costless. Only the stated full-coverage offer is available. This is not the full contract-menu equilibrium of Rothschild and Stiglitz (1976).

Perfect classification would restore insurance in this example while removing the transfer between risk classes. At premiums of 6.4 and thirty-two, each group could buy full coverage at its own expected cost. The low-risk person would have 93.6 for certain instead of facing wealth of a hundred or thirty-six; the high-risk person would have sixty-eight for certain instead of facing the same two outcomes with a much greater chance of the loss. Both prefer that protection. Knowing the chance of an accident does not reveal which person will have one. The insurer can still spread realized losses across policyholders even when it prices each class separately. What disappears is the subsidy from the lower expected cost to the higher one.

The distinction matters because insurance and solidarity can ask a pool to do different work. Protection against an event moves resources toward the state in which a loss occurs. A common price across known risk classes also moves expected resources toward the people more likely to enter that state. A society may have compelling reasons to want both. Someone born with a costly condition may face a premium perfectly accurate as a forecast and impossible as a bill. The accuracy of the forecast does not decide whether that person should bear it alone. Conversely, a payment from a lower-risk member does not cease to be a transfer because it appears on an insurance invoice. Naming both functions makes the dispute about who pays more intelligible.

The cost of the next member can explain why a stable pool remains too small. If the people willing to pay the most are also the costliest to insure, lowering the premium brings in progressively less costly members. The average cost of those already inside remains above the cost of the next entrant. A competitive insurer must cover the pool's average, while an efficiency comparison asks whether the entrant values protection more than the additional expected claims and resources required to provide it. Those tests can part company. Someone may be worth insuring at the margin yet decline the premium needed to support the existing membership. The failure can therefore take the quiet form of persistent exclusion, without the dramatic disappearance of every policy.

Einav and Finkelstein (2011), pp. 117–123, distinguish average-cost equilibrium pricing from the marginal-cost welfare comparison and show both full coverage and complete unraveling as possible cases. Their monetary surplus interpretation requires its stated assumptions; differences in income and welfare weights raise a separate distributive question.

A menu can make applicants reveal risk through the protection they choose, at a cost in the protection they receive. Rothschild and Stiglitz allowed firms to compete over both the premium and the amount insured. Under their assumptions, a common contract can be vulnerable to a rival offer that attracts the lower risks alone. In the possible separating equilibrium, the high risks receive full insurance and the low risks accept incomplete insurance that makes their contract less attractive to the high risks. The deductible helps distinguish people by making them bear something they would otherwise prefer to shed. Even that candidate equilibrium need not survive every profitable competing offer. The result identifies a difficulty in a specified competitive arrangement; it does not establish that every actual insurance institution must collapse.

Michael Rothschild and Joseph Stiglitz, “Equilibrium in Competitive Insurance Markets” (1976), §I.3–I.7, pp. 632–638, and conclusion, p. 648. The basic model has privately known risk types, identical risk-averse preferences, risk-neutral insurers, free entry and one contract per customer. Its separating allocation and possible nonexistence concern that equilibrium definition, rather than a universal prediction about insurance markets.

A requirement to participate can preserve the broad membership, but it cannot settle the distributional question by arithmetic alone. In the two-group example, requiring both groups to pay 19.2 keeps the expected account balanced. Relative to risk-specific premiums, each low-risk member contributes an extra 12.8 and each high-risk member receives an equal expected subsidy. The low-risk member is also compelled to buy at more than the stated maximum willingness to pay. The pool has become financially sustainable through a rule that changes who may leave, while that rule creates a real conflict between members' interests. A judgment in favor of the arrangement must explain why its protection and redistribution justify those terms. The balanced account supplies no welfare weights of its own.

A subsidy offers another means of keeping the price within reach, and moves part of the obligation outside the pool. If public funds meet the gap between a member's contribution and the insurer's receipt, the offer may recruit people who would otherwise remain uninsured. Someone must finance that gap. The relevant comparison includes the resources needed to raise and administer the funds, the coverage gained and the distribution of the payments. A rule that limits risk classification faces the same institutional task: it must address the selection pressure created by the common price, instead of assuming that a prohibition on classification also produces the money needed to sustain it. The benefit can be defensible without the financing becoming invisible.

The empirical direction of selection must remain an open question. A cautious person may both take greater care and value insurance more highly. If differences in aversion to risk are large enough, the most eager customers can be the least costly. Administration also consumes resources: processing a policy or verifying a claim can cost more than the additional protection is worth to a particular buyer. Einav and Finkelstein show why these departures change the policy comparison. A mandate that improves the allocation in the simple textbook case need not improve every richer one. The question is which people enter, what their entry costs and what protection is worth to them—not whether the word insurance by itself establishes the sign of the result.

Einav and Finkelstein (2011), “Departures from the Textbook Environment,” pp. 122–126, including notes 3–7. Administrative loads can make universal coverage inefficient under their criterion; sufficiently strong preference heterogeneity can generate advantageous selection. The paper is a dated theoretical and empirical synthesis, not a current estimate for any named insurance market.

A durable pool consequently requires a decision about membership as well as a calculation of price. A group assembled for some other purpose, a publicly financed program and a menu of private contracts each alter who arrives and who can withdraw. They also distribute the power to classify, to exclude and to contest a decision differently. A person classified as costly may have little control over the characteristic that makes the forecast accurate; a person asked to subsidize others may reasonably want to know the terms and limits of the obligation. The institutional problem is to make these claims answerable while keeping the promise solvent. Neither a more exact prediction nor a more generous aspiration can discharge it alone.

A pool must decide which misfortunes it shares before it can decide what each member owes.

The original question survives with its two parts distinct. Hidden risk can prevent a voluntary common offer from holding together, and several arrangements can overcome that obstacle under different conditions. Who should bear the cost reaches beyond the equilibrium calculation to the purpose of the protection and the grounds of membership. Knowledge can help an insurer price each person more accurately while making access harder for those whose circumstances it reveals. Shared protection can defend those people while requiring a contribution from others. The next plate asks what a disclosure requirement can accomplish: whether making one party reveal what it knows gives the other party the knowledge needed to choose. The argument remains open.
