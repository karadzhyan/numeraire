The Sky

in print · The Commons · plate x

The atmosphere connects a shared physical constraint to governments with separate jurisdictions and unequal capacities. Distinguishing the benefit of restraint from the allocation of permission reveals why an emissions account must be joined to a political bargain. Cumulative use, treaty obligations, finance and coordination show what such a bargain must accomplish while preserving the claims of people who will inherit its consequences.

A shared limit needs a common account.The argument of this plate

The pasture has an edge. Beyond the last fence another village’s rules begin, and a person who has taken too much can be asked to leave. The sky offers no such exit. Carbon dioxide released in one country contributes to a change whose consequences extend across borders and generations; the benefits of restraint cannot be reserved for the people who paid for it. Yet the power to govern a furnace, a forest or a road remains largely in institutions with bounded jurisdictions. The atmospheric commons therefore joins a shared physical constraint to divided political authority. The question is how the users of one system can make their separate decisions answer to what those decisions do together.

The scarce thing is more precise than a quantity of air. For a chosen temperature limit, cumulative carbon dioxide emissions are constrained, with the size of the remaining budget depending on the probability of meeting the limit, the treatment of other warming influences and the response of the climate system. The IPCC’s 2023 synthesis connects limiting human-caused warming to limiting cumulative carbon dioxide emissions and reaching net zero carbon dioxide, alongside reductions in other greenhouse gases. A budget expressed in this way is a conditional account of the physical problem. It does not arrive with a distribution of claims among the people who must live within it. Science can clarify the consequence of an emissions path while leaving the terms on which that path is divided to political judgment.

IPCC, AR6 Synthesis Report, Summary for Policymakers (2023), §§B.3–B.5, pp. 18–20, especially B.5. The assessment’s dated budget estimates are not a current allowance. The argument here uses the cumulative-emissions relationship and its stated conditions.

Two accounts of the commons meet in that constraint. Avoided warming has benefits that a contributor cannot confine to its own territory. A claim to emit within a finite collective budget, however, uses room that cannot also be assigned to someone else. The first feature creates the temptation to leave the work to others; the second makes the allocation of permission contentious even among people willing to cooperate. A promise that everyone will do a fair share cannot settle either problem until the meaning of fair has acquired terms. Equal present allocations, responsibility for past use, capacity to pay and the needs of people who still lack basic energy services can point toward different burdens. The dispute is part of governing the resource, rather than a distraction from a merely technical task.

A cumulative limit also makes today’s allocation a claim on tomorrow. Imagine a wholly abstract allowance of sixty units for three periods, with no replenishment or removal. Taking thirty in the first period and twenty in the second leaves ten for the third. Taking fifteen and twenty leaves twenty-five. Both histories respect the same cumulative limit at the moment the third period begins, but they leave very different room for the people who arrive then. An annual promise is therefore incomplete unless its place in the cumulative account can be seen. The illustration supplies no climate estimate and no rule for assigning generations their shares. It makes visible the transfer accomplished when an earlier user spends more of a fixed allowance.

Constructed bookkeeping: 60 − 30 − 20 = 10; 60 − 15 − 20 = 25. The allowance has arbitrary units, a stipulated total and no replenishment. It is not a calculation of the Earth’s remaining carbon budget. The intergenerational structure develops the earlier Collected Climate.

The incentive to wait is real, but it need not exhaust a government’s reasons to act. A change in energy use may also improve local air, reduce exposure to a volatile fuel supply or create a service that citizens want. These accompanying benefits can make some measures worthwhile within a jurisdiction even before all the global benefits are counted. Other measures remain costly to the acting country while benefiting others substantially. A workable agreement must recognize the difference. It can build on action that already has a local constituency while arranging contributions, finance or reciprocal commitments for action whose benefits travel farther than its political rewards.

The Paris Agreement gives this cooperation an institutional form without creating a world sovereign. Parties must prepare, communicate and maintain successive nationally determined contributions, and pursue domestic mitigation measures with the aim of achieving their objectives. Common reporting and review make their efforts more visible, while a recurring global stocktake asks what the collective effort amounts to. These provisions create obligations and occasions for scrutiny. They do not make every announced outcome an unconditional guarantee, or place national energy systems under a single international administrator. Their force depends partly on the domestic institutions that turn a contribution into measures and partly on what parties and their publics do with the information the agreement brings into view.

Paris Agreement (2015), arts. 4, 13–15. Article 4.2 distinguishes the duties concerning contributions from the aim of domestic mitigation measures; article 15 establishes a facilitative, non-adversarial and non-punitive compliance mechanism.

The account needs rules about what may be counted. A city’s reduction may already be included in its country’s inventory; adding the two reported quantities could then count one change twice. A project’s claimed saving depends on the path against which it is compared. A shift in production may reduce emissions recorded in one place while increasing them elsewhere. Each difficulty concerns the relation between a reported achievement and the shared physical outcome. Transparent boundaries, methods and treatment of overlaps give others a way to examine that relation. An impressive collection of certificates cannot substitute for a defensible account of what happened.

Capacity changes what a promise can accomplish. A country may be willing to replace an emitting technology while lacking affordable finance, reliable equipment or the institutions needed to operate its replacement. Assistance can alter the feasible bargain as well as its distribution. The Paris Agreement accordingly gives developed-country parties a financial obligation to assist developing-country parties and encourages support from other parties. The amount, timing and use of that assistance remain consequential: a promise too uncertain to finance a project cannot serve as the project’s machinery. Cooperation requires terms that the recipient can use and that contributors and affected communities can examine.

Paris Agreement, art. 9, especially paragraphs 1–3 and 7, supplies the finance distinction and reporting provisions. The project’s feasibility depends on the use to which the promised resources can actually be put.

A uniform rule for the final account can coexist with different means of reducing its total. Electricity, buildings, transport and land use involve different decision makers, assets and opportunities. Some choices belong to households, some to cities, some to national institutions, and some to agreements among states. Their work can reinforce one another: infrastructure can make a cleaner private choice possible, and a common standard can make local results comparable. Their work can also conflict, as one policy rewards an activity another is trying to reduce. The need for coordination arises from these connections. It does not require that every decision be made in the same room.

The atmospheric commons is especially unforgiving of a rule that mistakes delay for neutrality. Long-lived effects and the risk of irreversible change mean that waiting can alter the situation in which a later agreement must operate. That is a reason to build institutions capable of learning while acting: measures that can be assessed, accounts that can be corrected and commitments that can be strengthened as understanding changes. It is also a reason to keep the burdens visible. A durable agreement must answer people asked to surrender livelihoods or opportunities, and people who bear harm despite having little power over its causes. The unity of the atmosphere does not erase the inequality of those living beneath it.

IPCC, §§B.3–B.4, addresses unavoidable or irreversible changes and adaptation limits. Elinor Ostrom, “A Polycentric Approach for Coping with Climate Change” (2009), pp. 29–39, examines coordination, leakage, monitoring and learning across multiple levels. The institutional conclusion here draws these distinct physical and governance arguments together.

The sky is shared; the work of restraint must be assigned.

The absence of a planetary warden leaves an institutional task of unusual scale. The users must make the common limit legible, divide its demands, equip one another to meet them and maintain an account against which promises can be judged. None of those acts alone supplies the others. A budget without a bargain can be ignored; a bargain without an account can conceal its failure; an account without the power to revise conduct can become an elaborate record of loss. The atmosphere asks whether divided authorities can govern a shared consequence. The people who will inherit that consequence must somehow enter the terms of their answer. The argument remains open.
