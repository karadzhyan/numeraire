Rt xii

The Future

in print · The Rate · plate xii

A few percentage points can change the present value of a distant loss by orders of magnitude. This capstone distinguishes the private price of a dated promise from the public judgment about future welfare, then examines abundance, opportunity cost, unequal exposure and uncertainty about the rate itself. Ramsey’s separation of money from enjoyment and Weitzman’s weighting of distant scenarios keep the calculation useful while exposing the choices it cannot make for us.

Distance in time is a fact; the weight we give it is a judgment.The argument of this plate

A person born a century from now cannot buy a place in today’s bond market. She cannot bid against an action that will damage her water, offer us a payment to preserve a forest or refuse the terms on which we leave her an atmosphere. Yet we must decide now about things that will affect her. The rate enters that decision as a device for comparing dates. It can look like a small technical input while determining how much of her future appears in our present account.

The force is visible in an imagined loss of one trillion dollars, expressed in constant purchasing power, two hundred years from now. At a constant annual discount rate of five percent, its present value is about fifty-seven million eight hundred thousand dollars. At one and a half percent it is about fifty billion nine hundred million. The lower-rate valuation is roughly eight hundred and eighty times the higher one. If preventing the loss costs one hundred million today and has no other effects, the two rates reverse the decision. Nothing about the stipulated future loss has changed. The rate has changed the weight assigned to it.

Weitzman, “Why the Far-Distant Future Should Be Discounted at Its Lowest Possible Rate” (1998), §1, pp. 201–202, poses the distant-horizon problem. The figures here are hypothetical and use annual compounding: 1012/1.05200 = 57,828,268.13; 1012/1.015200 = 50,908,646,439.09. The monetary loss is an assumed valuation, not an estimate of a particular catastrophe or a price assigned to a life.

The earlier inquiry into discounting explained a private comparison. A person able to exchange money across dates at a specified rate can ask what present sum is equivalent to a later payment. The public question is larger. Which consumption does the project sacrifice today, whose benefit does it produce later, and what alternative investment could carry resources forward? A market rate can provide evidence about opportunities available under existing arrangements. It cannot by itself establish how people excluded from those arrangements should count. Using the rate from a traded promise silently imports the circumstances of that promise into a decision about people who never traded it.

Fisher, The Theory of Interest (1930), chapter I §§7–11 and 18, develops the capitalization of dated benefits and specifies a safe-payment comparison. Ramsey, “A Mathematical Theory of Saving” (1928), p. 553, expressly separates discounting money from discounting utility.

Ramsey began his 1928 saving problem by refusing to discount later enjoyment merely because it came later. The ethical position was explicit, and so were the model’s exclusions: a continuing community, simplified production, no distributional differences within it and no disasters destroying the accumulated stock. His rejection of pure time preference did not say that every additional unit of future consumption must be valued equally with an additional unit now. If people will be better provided for, another unit can matter less to them. Equality between persons and equality between quantities are different commitments.

Ramsey (1928), §I, pp. 543–549, especially pp. 543–546, states the ethical premise, assumptions and marginal-utility condition. Section II(c), pp. 553–555, introduces pure utility discounting separately. The present application does not adopt his simplifying assumptions as facts about the future.

A small illustration makes the distinction concrete. Give present and future welfare equal weight, and let the value of consumption have a marginal utility of one divided by consumption. At consumption of one hundred, an additional unit has marginal value .01; at one hundred and two, it has value about .009804. The future unit then carries 100/102 of the present unit’s marginal weight. That is the same factor as discounting consumption at two percent for one period, although neither person’s welfare has been discounted for arriving later. If consumption is equal, this particular reason for discounting vanishes. If future consumption is lower, it points in the other direction.

The assumption of greater future abundance is therefore part of the argument. It cannot do its work merely by appearing in a spreadsheet. Nor does an increase in average consumption show that those exposed to a particular loss will be richer. A benefit protecting poor households from destruction can have a different welfare value from an equal monetary payment to already secure owners, whether the recipients live now or later. Ramsey set those distributional differences aside to solve his stated problem. A public decision that needs them must put them back, rather than allowing the convenience of an average to decide whose deprivation is tolerable.

Ramsey, pp. 543–544 and 553–555, identifies the aggregation assumption and the distinction between utility and money discounting. The one-period calculation and its equal-consumption control are constructed here. They illustrate marginal weights under a specified utility function, not a numerical recommendation for social policy.

Opportunity cost nonetheless remains. Resources devoted to protecting the future cannot simultaneously provide housing, care or other investments today. Some present investments may also make future people better able to withstand harm. Equal moral standing does not tell us that every distant benefit outweighs every immediate need, or that the most expensive protective measure must be chosen. It requires the comparison to preserve the claims on both sides. A low discount rate can expose a future cost that a higher rate nearly erases; it does not dispense with the need to establish the cost, the effectiveness of prevention and the alternatives forgone.

Uncertainty makes the arithmetic less accommodating than simply choosing a middle number. Imagine a small project delivering one hundred dollars of constant purchasing power in a century under either of two equally likely scenarios. In one, the applicable annual rate is one percent throughout; in the other, it is five percent throughout. Averaging the two present values gives about eighteen dollars and eighty-seven cents. Averaging the rates first and discounting at three percent gives only about five dollars and twenty cents. The operations are different. Lower-rate scenarios preserve more of a distant payment, so they carry more weight in its expected present value.

Weitzman (1998), §§2–4, pp. 202–207, including note 5, averages discount factors across scenarios. The illustrative annual-compounding calculation is 50/1.01100 + 50/1.05100 = 18.87, compared with 100/1.03100 = 5.20. The payoff is identical in both scenarios and the project is assumed small; no state-dependent damage adjustment is hidden in the example.

Weitzman’s result gives this observation a precise distant limit. In his model, rate paths converge, and scenarios with lower limiting rates increasingly dominate the surviving present-value weight. This does not instruct us to select the smallest number we can imagine for every project today. The scenario support and its probabilities are part of the setup; the time before the distant regime still discounts what follows. And the project must be treated differently if its payoff depends on the same conditions that determine the rate. A measure valuable precisely when future society is poor or damaged cannot be assessed by changing the discount rate while pretending its benefits are unrelated to those states.

Weitzman, §§2–4, especially p. 204 note 5 and pp. 206–207. The paper’s small, uncorrelated-project interpretation, convergent scenario paths and retained near-period discount factor qualify its limiting result. It does not establish a universal zero rate or a complete rule for state-correlated catastrophic losses.

The object being valued also resists compression. A monetary measure of future damage already contains judgments about what is lost, who bears it and what substitutes are possible. Applying a rate does not cure an incomplete measure. Nor can the possibility of a large investment return guarantee that a particular destroyed resource will be replaceable when replacement is needed. These are questions about production, exposure and institutions as well as preference. The discipline of discounting is useful when it makes the assumptions inspectable. It becomes a concealment when the resulting present value is presented as though it had arrived without them.

The future’s absence from the transaction is consequently a problem of representation. We can state the pure time preference separately from the expected consumption path; show who bears the cost and who receives the benefit; compare plausible rates and states; and identify the decisions whose ranking changes. None of these acts supplies a mechanical ethical answer. Together they prevent a single unexplained percentage from posing as one. A disagreement about posterity should appear where the disagreement lies, rather than being buried in the convention used to convert a date into a number.

A discount rate can measure an opportunity. It cannot, by itself, decide how much a person matters.

The volume began with the price of moving resources through time. It ends where some of the people affected cannot participate in setting that price. We owe them neither an effortless promise that every future loss will be prevented nor the convenience of treating distance as disappearance. We owe an account of the tradeoffs, with the assumptions exposed and their standing preserved inside the calculation. The rate helps make that account; it does not relieve us of making the judgment. The argument remains open.
