The Surplus

in print · № VII · 2 of 12 plates

Buy a thing for less than it was worth to you, and you pocket the difference — a gain no till records. So does the seller who sells above his cost. Every voluntary exchange creates a surplus, the value brought into being by the meeting of a willing buyer and a willing seller; and the price is the line that divides it between them. This volume anatomizes that surplus — what it is, how the price splits it, and the unending three-way contest, among buyer, seller, and state, over who walks away with the gains.

The question of the volume every exchange creates a gain that neither party could make alone — so whose is it, and what decides the share?
The argument

Every good bargain leaves something over. You would have paid five dollars for the book and you paid three; the two you kept are real, though no receipt records them, and they are yours by the simple grace of having wanted the thing less dearly than its price allowed you to have it. The seller, who would have let it go for one, keeps two of his own. Between you, the sale has conjured four units of advantage out of nothing — four that existed nowhere before the two of you agreed to deal. This is the surplus, and it is the quiet reason exchange exists at all: not a transfer, in which one gains what the other loses, but a small act of creation, in which both walk away richer than they came. This volume is about that created value — where it comes from, how the price divides it, and the long contest over who keeps it.

Economists give the two halves of it their names. The buyer's share — the gap between what a thing is worth to him and what he pays — is the consumer's surplus; the seller's share — the gap between his price and his cost — is the producer's surplus; and the sum of the two, across all the units that change hands, is the gains from trade, the total value an exchange brings into being above the cost of bringing it about. The previous volume, on the margin, supplies the instrument for seeing it. Each unit traded adds its own thin sliver of gain — the distance between what the marginal buyer would pay and what the marginal seller would accept — and the surplus is simply the sum of all those slivers, the area caught between the falling curve of demand and the rising curve of supply, out to the point where the two cross. It was an engineer, Jules Dupuit, asking how much a bridge was worth to those who would cross it, who first drew the figure; Marshall who named the consumer's surplus and made it a tool. The gain is mutual, and that is its wonder: unlike a robbery or a tax, in which one pocket fills as another empties, an exchange leaves both parties fuller than before, the buyer and the seller each carrying off a piece of a value that the trade itself has called into being. Where the margin showed how a price is fixed, the surplus shows what that fixing creates.

And here is the heart of the matter. Every exchange creates a surplus, but the price decides how it is split. Sell the book at one dollar and the buyer keeps nearly the whole gain; sell it at five and the seller takes it all; sell it at three and they share. The price is the line drawn through the surplus, assigning so much to the one and so much to the other — and where that line falls is not dictated by the nature of the book. It is the outcome of a contest: of supply against demand in an open market, of bargaining where the parties are few, of sheer power where one side can set the terms and the other must take them. So the price, which wears the look of a plain fact about the thing — the book costs three dollars, as though that were a property of it like its weight — is in truth a verdict on a hidden struggle over the spoils of cooperation. A price is a verdict on a hidden contest, mistaken for a fact of nature; and the surplus is precisely what the contest is over, and what the verdict divides.

The surplus is more than a private gain to be split; it is the ruler economics reaches for when it would judge whether an arrangement is good. An outcome is called efficient when it makes the total surplus as large as it can be — when no rearrangement could enlarge the gains from trade, no worthwhile trade left unmade. And there is a celebrated theorem, the first of welfare economics, which holds that a competitive market does exactly this, left to itself: it drives out every last unexploited gain, leaving no surplus on the table — Adam Smith's invisible hand, recast as a proof. A companion theorem, the second, adds a consoling promise: that any efficient outcome one likes, however the gains are shared out, can be reached through a competitive market, provided only the starting wealth be arranged aright — so that efficiency and fairness might be pursued apart, the market left to enlarge the pie while the state, by some prior redistribution, saw to its cutting. It is a promise the later plates will test and find wanting. The most powerful uses of the idea, though, concern surplus not created but destroyed. When a monopolist throttles his output to lift his price, he does not merely pull some of the buyers' surplus across to his own side of the ledger; he annihilates a part of it outright — the trades that were worth making and now go unmade — and that vanished gain, which falls to no one, is the deadweight loss, the truest measure of what the distortion costs. A tax does the same; so does a binding price control; so does the uncounted cost of the spillover. Much of what the volumes on power and the externality were doing, beneath their own vocabulary, was an accounting of surplus quietly destroyed.

The surplus, then, is fought over on more than the two sides of the counter. The buyer and seller divide it through the price; the firm with power claws a larger share toward itself; the state, the silent third party to every deal, taxes a slice away, and burns a little more as deadweight loss in the taking. But the gravest question the surplus raises is not how it is divided but whether its sum is the right thing to maximize at all. For the gain is measured in willingness to pay, and willingness to pay is fenced by ability to pay: the rich man's idle whim, backed by his fortune, registers as more surplus than the poor man's pressing need, backed by little — so that to maximize the total, weighting every dollar of gain the same whoever holds it, is silently to weight each person by his wealth. The efficient outcome may thus be the one that gratifies the moneyed, and the cool word efficient may carry a verdict on distribution that it loudly disclaims. There is value, too, that the surplus never sees — the unpriced, the priceless, the goods the poor cannot afford even to bid for. Economics has an answer of sorts — that a change is good if its winners could compensate its losers and remain ahead — but the compensation is a hypothesis the world seldom honors, and a test of what could be paid slides easily into a license for what is not. The most measured of standards has a politics folded quietly inside it.

So this volume returns, from a new direction, to the conviction that runs through the whole table. A price is a verdict on a hidden contest, mistaken for a fact of nature — and the surplus reveals what that contest is for, and what the verdict apportions. Every trade brings into being a pool of gain that exists only because two parties troubled to meet; the price is the line that divides the pool between them; and we, reading the price as a measurement of worth, forget that it is rather the settlement of a struggle over the spoils of cooperation — a boundary whose place is fixed by power, by information, and by law, and could have been fixed elsewhere. The surplus is the prize; the price is the verdict that awards it; and to mistake that verdict for a fact of nature is to forget that the gains had to be divided by someone, somehow. The twelve plates trace the surplus from the gain itself and the price that divides it, through the triangle that measures it, the loss that destroys it, and the theorem that maximizes it, to the prize that power seizes, the toll the state exacts, the bias by which the calculus favors the willing rich, the compensation forever promised and seldom paid, the value left unpriced, the older quarrel over where the surplus truly comes from, and the last doubt whether its greatest sum is the good. Where the volume on the margin showed how a price is set, this one shows what a price divides. The argument remains open.
