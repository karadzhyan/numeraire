The Rider

in print · The Commons · plate iii

The interest shared by a crowd does not by itself explain why anyone joins the work. A repair, a repeated encounter and a selective benefit show how contribution can become worthwhile—and why neither a large group nor a small one has its fate settled by its numbers. Cooperation needs an arrangement that connects the individual act to the common result.

The common gain still needs a private reason to act.The argument of this plate

The man who will not mend the channel may want the water as much as anyone. He may know exactly what the neglected bank will cost, approve the proposed repair, and wish his neighbors every success in making it. His refusal need contain no disagreement about the common good. It may follow instead from a smaller calculation: the repair will serve his field whether or not he gives a day to it, while the day he gives is lost to him alone. The community’s interest is real. So is his interest in leaving its expense to the community. The free rider lives in the distance between those two reckonings, and a crowd does not close that distance simply by agreeing that the work is worth doing.

The benefit of the undertaking and the benefit of joining it are different things. Mancur Olson made that distinction the center of collective action. An individual choosing whether to contribute compares what changes because he contributes with what his contribution costs him. He does not ordinarily compare the whole good with the whole cost of providing it, because neither lies wholly within his choice. A clean channel may be worth far more than its repair. Yet if each person expects to receive the water on the same terms whether he works or stays away, the magnitude of the common advantage cannot by itself pay the private bill. What is missing is a connection between the contribution and the contributor’s own position.

Mancur Olson, The Logic of Collective Action (argument first published 1965), Introduction, pp. 1–3, and Chapter I, §F, pp. 43–52. Olson’s taxonomy distinguishes privileged, intermediate and latent groups; the common interest alone does not determine which situation obtains.

A small invented commons shows the arithmetic. Four households can each undertake a repair costing three units. Each completed repair benefits every household by one unit, whether that household worked or not. If all four contribute, each receives four and pays three: everyone gains one. But hold the others’ decisions fixed. A household’s own repair adds only one to its own benefit and costs it three. Declining the work improves its position by two, whatever the others do. All four therefore decline in the single encounter, if they care only about these stated payoffs. The joint gain is available; it is the choices leading to it that will not hold together.

This is the trap described in the earlier Many, now turned from taking too much into maintaining too little. The logic does not require the commoners to misunderstand one another, to hate their neighbors, or to forget next year’s need. They may see the failure approaching with perfect clarity. Nor does it follow that every shared undertaking has this structure. A repair essential to one household may be worth doing even if that household must bear the entire expense. A contribution that completes a threshold may make the difference between receiving the whole benefit and receiving none. Before calling an outcome a dilemma, one must establish the alternatives that make it one.

The four-household numbers are an original illustration of the structure developed in the Collected Many. Olson, Chapter I, §F, pp. 45–51 and notes 66, 68 and 70, qualifies the role of numbers, recognizes a member’s incentive to provide the good alone, and discusses bargaining failures even where provision is individually worthwhile. A threshold can change incentives without guaranteeing successful coordination.

Number changes the difficulty without furnishing a verdict. A contribution can disappear into a large undertaking; an absence that would be obvious around a small table can pass unnoticed among thousands. Organization itself also consumes effort: finding the beneficiaries, obtaining their assent, collecting their shares, and checking performance. Olson’s large group must clear this additional obstacle before it can produce the good it wants. Yet the headcount is not a sufficient description. A large group with one very large beneficiary can secure some provision from that beneficiary; a small group of equally determined holdouts can secure none. What matters is the distribution of gains, the visibility of action, and the cost of making a response possible.

The most direct response is to join the collective benefit to something that can be withheld. An association might supply a service to its members as well as campaigning for a change that benefits outsiders. Membership then purchases something the nonmember does not receive. A levy can make payment a condition of belonging to the jurisdiction that supplies the good. These are different arrangements, but each makes an individual consequence accompany the shared result. Their existence explains how a large organization can operate without proving that the organization serves everyone well. A selective benefit can be expensive to provide; an enforced contribution can support a wasteful project. Solving the problem of collecting money does not settle the wisdom of spending it.

Olson, Chapter I, §F, pp. 50–52, especially note 72, distinguishes positive selective incentives from coercive ones and specifies their force relative to the individual’s contribution. The membership service and levy here illustrate those mechanisms; they are not claims about the measured performance of a particular organization.

A continuing relationship supplies a different connection. Return to the four households, and suppose the same choice repeats indefinitely, each household can observe the others’ actions, and all agree to contribute while everyone has done so before. After a refusal, all stop. Cooperation brings each household one every period. A single refusal while the other three work brings three now, followed by zero. If next period’s payoff is valued at four-fifths of this period’s, continued cooperation is worth five today and the betrayal only three. Under these assumptions the threat can sustain the repair. At a discount factor of one-half, cooperation is worth two and the betrayal three; the same promise has lost its force.

This repeated version is an original calculation, not an estimated community behavior. Cooperation yields 1/(1 − δ); a one-period deviation followed by permanent noncontribution yields 3. The proposed strategy can deter the deviation when δ ≥ 2/3. Perfect observation, the continuing opportunity and the specified responses are assumptions. The punishment is credible within this game because noncontribution is individually preferable when the others do not contribute.

The calculation reveals what familiarity can accomplish and what it cannot. To have met before is not enough; a person must expect something worth preserving afterward. To know that someone failed to contribute is not enough; the failure must be distinguishable from illness, bad luck or a duty already discharged elsewhere. And a threat that answers every mistake with permanent collapse can destroy the very value it was supposed to defend. A community therefore needs more than a repeated encounter. It needs a way to make obligations intelligible, evidence answerable, and corrections proportionate. The future becomes an incentive through rules that people can actually use.

The earlier plate’s decisive qualification survives: a dilemma is a diagnosis, not a prophecy. Ostrom’s experimental work examined settings in which communication, agreements and the possibility of sanctions changed conduct. The ability to punish was not an unqualified benefit, because punishment also consumed resources; allowing participants to choose arrangements mattered to what followed. Such findings give substance to the space between the one-shot model and the working commons. They do not remove incentives from the explanation. They show people participating in the making of those incentives, with trust and reciprocity among the motives through which an arrangement acquires force.

Elinor Ostrom, “Beyond Markets and States: Polycentric Governance of Complex Economic Systems”, Nobel lecture (2009), §5, pp. 423–426. Her account distinguishes baseline, communication and sanctioning conditions, including the costs of sanctions and participants’ choice of rules. It does not warrant predicting cooperation in every community.

A common interest supplies a reason for the work. An institution must give people a reason to bear their part of it.

There is a further danger in making the free rider a moral category before making it an economic one. The same charge may be light for a wealthy member and impossible for a poor one. The same unpaid hour may cost one household leisure and another its livelihood. A contributor may already supply knowledge, care or work that the collector’s chosen measure fails to recognize. None of these possibilities makes the financing problem vanish. They determine what a fair and workable division of the cost would be. A rule that counts only the most convenient contribution can mistake incapacity for opportunism and make the settlement harder to sustain.

The crowd moves when the arrangement changes what joining means: a benefit becomes conditional, an obligation enforceable, a promise credible, or a contribution part of a relationship people value. There is no single device that can be carried unchanged from four households to a nation of strangers. But there is a question that can travel: what connects the individual act to the shared result, and who bears the cost of that connection? The next plate carries it out to sea, where one lamp can guide many vessels and the light refuses to distinguish the ship that paid from the ship that did not.
