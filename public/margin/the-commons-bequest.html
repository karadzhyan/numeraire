The Bequest

in print · The Commons · plate xi

The future users of a commons cannot enter the bargain that determines what they will receive. Distinguishing the asset from its yield and a valuation from its ethical assumptions gives substance to the idea of trusteeship. The inquiry asks how records, representation, public reasons and revisable commitments can protect a worthwhile inheritance while taking the serious needs of present people into account.

The inheritance is shaped before its heirs arrive.The argument of this plate

The people who will inherit the pasture are absent when its present users decide how hard to graze it. They cannot vote against the extra animal, contribute to a repair or promise a reciprocal favor in exchange for restraint. They will receive the field after those decisions have become its condition. A community governing a commons across time must therefore find a way to represent an interest that cannot yet represent itself. Calling the living trustees expresses the demand. Giving that demand force requires an account of what is entrusted, what may be used and who can question the spending before the future arrives to discover the result.

The inheritance has a stock as well as a flow. Suppose an imagined resource begins with one hundred units, receives two units of renewal in each year and loses four to withdrawal. With these rates fixed, no other losses and withdrawals continuing for twenty-five years, the remaining stock is fifty. The annual withdrawal may have supported worthwhile lives and useful work. The arithmetic nevertheless shows that half of the opening stock has also been spent. A statement of current income that records only what the commoners received can conceal this second account. Stewardship begins by making the distinction visible, so that the community can decide whether it is living from the resource’s yield or drawing down the thing that produces it.

The resource is constructed: 100 + 25 × 2 − 25 × 4 = 50. Renewal is stipulated as constant rather than modeled as a biological response to the stock. The example develops the intergenerational concern in The Inheritance; it is not a sustainable-yield estimate for a real ecosystem.

A duty to transmit a worthwhile inheritance does not answer every question by requiring the physical world to remain unchanged. A community may use one asset to build another: timber may become shelter, income may become education, a reservoir may replace a less reliable supply. The question is what these substitutions preserve and what they lose. Money in a fund can purchase some future services; it cannot guarantee a replacement for every species, place or ecological function. Conversely, preserving a resource while denying present people the means to live can impose a grave burden of its own. The trusteeship argument must identify the interest being protected and explain the terms on which a substitution is acceptable. The word capital cannot perform that judgment by grouping everything under one name.

Discounting gives a sharp form to the difficulty without settling it. Imagine a project that costs twenty units now and provides a certain benefit of one hundred units fifty years later, measured in the same real consumption units. At a constant annual discount rate of one percent, the future benefit has a present value of about 60.80 and exceeds the cost. At five percent it has a present value of about 8.72 and falls short. A change in the rate reverses the decision while leaving the promised physical outcome unchanged. The calculation is a comparison conditional on the rate, the certainty and the units. To make it a judgment about the bequest, the community must explain why those conditions fit the people and consequences under consideration.

Constructed values: 100 ÷ 1.0150 ≈ 60.8039; 100 ÷ 1.0550 ≈ 8.7204. Kenneth Arrow and coauthors, “Determining Benefits and Costs for Future Generations” (2013), pp. 349–350, distinguish approaches based on consumption and opportunity cost and examine uncertainty about future rates.

Several different reasons can enter a discount rate. Resources used now may have an alternative investment return; additional consumption may matter less to people who are better off; the treatment of later people’s welfare may itself carry an ethical weight. These reasons cannot be exchanged without explanation. A future generation’s expected wealth does not tell us whether the people harmed by a particular loss will be wealthy, or whether they can buy a substitute for what has disappeared. A return available on one investment does not establish that every ecological loss can be repaired with its proceeds. The accounting can clarify a choice when its assumptions are exposed. When the rate silently carries all these judgments at once, the apparent precision conceals what the community is choosing.

Uncertainty can make preserving room to choose valuable in its own right. A reversible use leaves a later community able to reconsider; an irreversible conversion may close that possibility before the need for it is understood. This does not make every delay wise or every preservation project worthwhile. Waiting can also forgo benefits and leave present harms in place. It gives the decision a further dimension: the value of information that may arrive, the cost of keeping an option open and the consequence of discovering too late that the chosen substitute does not work. A prudent trustee compares those possibilities rather than treating an uncertain future as a reason to omit it from the account.

The language of responsibility across generations has received an explicit international expression. UNESCO’s 1997 declaration asks present generations to protect future interests while preserving future generations’ freedom of choice. Its environmental provisions address the conditions of life and the consequences of major projects; its account of development also calls attention to poverty and inequality among people now living. These claims form an ethical declaration, rather than a universal judicial trust that automatically supplies a claimant, a remedy and a court. Their importance for the commons lies in the breadth of the demand: the inheritance concerns the ability to lead lives and make choices, and its protection must be considered alongside the serious needs of the present.

UNESCO, Declaration on the Responsibilities of the Present Generations Towards Future Generations, adopted November 12, 1997, preamble and arts. 1–5, 10 and 12. The preamble frames moral obligations and guidelines; article 2 protects future freedom of choice, and article 10 connects sustainable development with poverty and deprivation.

The trust can also acquire a concrete foothold in law. In Illinois Central Railroad v. Illinois, decided in 1892, the United States Supreme Court upheld Illinois’s resumption of control over submerged lands in Chicago’s harbor that an earlier legislature had purported to grant to a railroad. The state held the lands under navigable water for public uses including navigation, commerce and fishing, and could not surrender its general control of the harbor through the grant. The Court nevertheless allowed room for grants of particular parcels that promoted the public interest or did not substantially impair it in the remaining lands and waters. The doctrine protected a continuing public responsibility without requiring every part of the resource to remain untouched. Its force came from a defined trustee, resource and public use. For the intergenerational question, the instructive feature is that a present legislature could not finally sell away the public authority its successors would need to discharge that responsibility.

Illinois Central Railroad Co. v. Illinois, 146 U.S. 387, 452–456, 459–464 (1892). The majority distinguished permissible parcel grants from abdication of control and sustained the 1873 repeal of the 1869 grant. Its holding concerned the specified submerged lands and continuing public uses, with separate directions concerning particular piers.

Representation can begin before a future person exists. A community can require an account of the resource’s condition, disclose the long consequences of a proposed use and appoint someone to examine the interests missing from the present bargain. It can distinguish ordinary maintenance from a decision that irreversibly disposes of the asset. It can reserve funds for restoration, place limits on a drawdown or require public reasons before those limits change. These are possible institutional choices, with costs and weaknesses to be judged in their setting. Their shared purpose is to make the future interest part of the decision while the decision can still be altered.

The representative needs an obligation to explain as well as an opportunity to object. No present official can know every preference of people yet to be born, and a claim to speak for them can protect an incumbent’s own attachment to the past. An examination of the bequest should therefore identify the affected resource, the range of plausible consequences, the people bearing present costs and the alternatives considered. Contestable reasons are more useful than a ceremonial invocation of descendants. They let others distinguish preservation of future options from preservation of present power, and let later evidence correct a decision made under earlier uncertainty.

Commitment and revision must coexist in the institution. A rule that every new majority can suspend at no cost may provide little protection against successive small withdrawals. A rule that no generation can revise may prevent adaptation to conditions its authors never imagined. Durable procedures can place a greater burden of explanation on irreversible choices, require records that survive officeholders and make resource conditions visible to successive users. They can also specify how new knowledge enters a revision. The aim is continuity of responsibility through changing membership: an obligation carried forward, with reasons that can be examined rather than merely inherited.

Dietz, Ostrom and Stern, “The Struggle to Govern the Commons” (2003), pp. 1908–1910, develop adaptive governance and institutions prepared for change. The proposed intergenerational procedures extend that inquiry to the continuity of responsibility as the community’s membership changes.

An inheritance is governed before its heirs can ask for an account.

What binds the living is ultimately something they must give institutional form: a judgment that possession for a season does not exhaust the interests in the thing possessed. The judgment becomes effective through inventories, limits, representation, public reasons and the means of correcting a failure of care. It must defend a future worth inheriting without making the present expendable, and preserve choices without pretending to know which choices descendants will make. The commoners cannot obtain the consent of everyone their conduct will affect. They can make the absence of that consent a reason for greater care in what they leave behind. The argument remains open.
