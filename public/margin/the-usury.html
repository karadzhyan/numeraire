The Usury

in print · The Rate · plate ii

For most of history, lending at interest was a sin: Aristotle called money barren, the Church forbade usury, and the prohibition rested on the sense that to charge a man for the time he takes to repay is to feed on his need. The defenders answered that time is valuable and the bargain free, and they largely won. This plate gives the thesis on which the volume turns: that beneath the quarrel lies a contest theory cannot settle — between the creditor who can wait and the debtor who cannot — and that the rate the moderns call the natural price of time is the verdict in it, fixed by power and law, and merely relabeled when usury became interest.

The usurer breaketh the first law that was made for mankind after the fall, which was, In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat thy bread; not, In the sweat of another's face. Francis Bacon, "Of Usury," 1625

For most of history, to lend at interest was a sin. Aristotle called money barren and its breeding against nature; the medieval Church forbade usury outright; and the prohibition, however widely evaded, rested on an intuition that has never quite died — that to charge a man for the time he takes to repay is to profit from his need rather than from any labor of one's own. The defenders answered that time is genuinely valuable, that the lender forgoes its use, that interest is the just price of waiting. But beneath the long quarrel lay a question neither side could settle by theory alone: who decides what an hour of waiting is worth — and is the rate the natural reward of patience, or the measure of one party's power over another's necessity?

The condemnation was no passing scruple but the settled judgment of the ancient and medieval worlds, and it rested on an argument. Aristotle gave it its enduring form. Money, he held in the Politics, was devised as a barren token of exchange; to lend it at interest is to make the token breed, and the Greek for interest, tokos, betrays the unnaturalness, for it means offspring. Of all the modes of getting wealth, he wrote, usury is the most hated and the most against nature. The Scholastics refined the charge. Thomas Aquinas argued that the usurer sells what does not exist: in lending money one cannot charge separately for its use, as a landlord charges for the use of a house, because money is consumed in the spending, its use inseparable from the thing; and to charge by the length of the loan is to sell time, which belongs to all men in common and is no one's to sell. On these grounds, and not on mere prejudice, the canon law forbade the taking of interest for more than a thousand years.

Aristotle's condemnation in the Politics turned on a metaphor: money, a barren token of exchange, ought not to breed, and tokos, the Greek for interest, means offspring. Aquinas sharpened it: to charge for the use of money lent is to sell what does not exist, since money is consumed in use; and to charge by duration is to sell time, which is no man's to sell.

The prohibition gave way, as prohibitions against profitable things do, less by refutation than by erosion. Distinctions multiplied to let commerce through. A lender might not take usura, gain from the loan as such, but he might claim interesse, compensation for a loss the loan had cost him — and as trade grew, the forgone gain grew with it, until the exception had all but swallowed the rule. Calvin, in the sixteenth century, permitted interest on loans for production while still condemning the man who ground the face of the poor. And in time the defense turned from apology to confidence. Time, it said, is genuinely valuable: the lender surrenders the present use of his money and deserves payment for the surrender, as the previous plate set out; the borrower, far from robbed, is served, gaining resources he lacks. And a bargain struck freely between consenting adults over the price of money is the business of neither priest nor prince to forbid. Jeremy Bentham carried this to its limit in his Defence of Usury, insisting that usury laws injure the very borrowers they profess to shield — driving the needy to illegal lenders at ruinous rates, and choking off the credit that might have rescued them. The case for interest as a lawful, even kindly, price was made, and for the modern world largely won.

The prohibition eroded through distinctions: interesse, compensation for the lender's loss, was allowed where usura, gain from the loan itself, was not, and the gap widened as commerce grew. Calvin permitted interest on productive loans while condemning the exploitation of the poor; and Bentham's Defence of Usury (1787) argued that usury laws harm the borrowers they pretend to protect, driving the needy to illegal lenders at worse terms.

Yet the victory of the defense did not dispose of the ancient question; it merely moved it out of sight. For the defenders had answered an argument the ancients only half meant. The talk of money's "barrenness" was indeed feeble: money lent to build a mill is not barren but among the most fruitful things there is, and the lender who parts with its present use has given something real. But beneath the bad biology lay a true perception, and it was never about fertility — it was about need. There is all the difference between the merchant who borrows to seize an opportunity and the laborer who borrows to eat. The first bargains from strength: if the terms displease him he declines and keeps his place. The second has no such liberty, and takes whatever is demanded, the alternative being not a better loan but hunger. The "free bargain" on which the modern defense rests presumes two parties who can each walk away; and the essence of the usurious case, the one the ancients had in view, is exactly that one party cannot. Strip the old condemnation of its faulty metaphor, and a sound distinction remains: between the price of time and the price of desperation — and a rate of interest may be either.

The defenders rightly saw that money's "barrenness" is a poor argument: money lent to build a mill is fruitful, and the lender forgoes something real. But the ancients' deeper charge was about need, not fertility — the difference between the merchant who borrows to seize a chance and can walk away, and the laborer who borrows to eat and cannot. The "free bargain" the defense invokes presumes parties who can decline; the usurious case is the one in which a party cannot.

Once it is granted, as the previous plate showed, that the rate is no single figure dictated by nature but a magnitude that must be set somewhere within a wide range, the question the usurers' judges were really pressing becomes unavoidable: who sets it, and how? The answer, in every age, has been power, hedged about by law. The terms on which a loan is struck are fixed by the relative strength of the parties — the lender, who has many borrowers and can afford to wait, against the borrower, who has few lenders and cannot. And the law has never been a bystander; it has always reached in to shape the contest. The usury ceiling caps what the strong may charge the weak; bankruptcy releases the debtor and limits the creditor's claim; the ancient jubilee canceled debts outright, that no generation be born into the bondage of the last; the laws of collateral and foreclosure decide what a man forfeits when he fails. None of these deviates from a natural rate the law would otherwise leave alone, for there is no such rate to leave alone. There is only the rate a given balance of power, under a given law, brings forth — and to raise the ceiling or strike it down, to enforce the bond or soften it, are the decisions that fix what an hour of waiting shall be worth.

Because the rate is set within a wide band, the terms of a loan turn on the relative strength of the parties — the lender's many alternatives against the borrower's few — and on the law that frames them. That law has never stood outside the contest: the usury ceiling, the bankruptcy that releases the debtor, the jubilee that canceled debts, the rules of collateral and foreclosure. There is no natural rate the law merely permits, only the rate a given balance of power produces.

And so the rate, the price of time, stands revealed as the recorded outcome of this contest, and the usury question as one never answered but only renamed. A price is a verdict on a hidden contest, mistaken for a fact of nature; and the interest rate, which the modern theories array in the robes of nature — the meeting of impatience with productivity, the reward of abstinence, the price of liquidity — is the visible settlement of the oldest economic struggle there is, between the creditor who commands resources and the debtor who needs them, between those who can wait and those who must pay for not waiting. What an earlier age called usury and damned, ours calls interest and prices; but the thing beneath the two names is one — the gain the lender takes from the borrower's necessity and from the time the debt runs — and the question of its justice, which the medievals at least had the honesty to ask aloud, is the same question still. The theories that call the rate natural are not false; they describe, with precision, the machinery by which the settlement is reached. They are silent only on the struggle the machinery settles, and on the plain fact that the figure it yields measures who, in that struggle, has prevailed.

What the medievals called usury and forbade, the moderns call interest and price; but the thing itself — the gain the lender draws from the borrower's need and from the time the loan runs — is unchanged, and so is the question of its justice. The modern theories explain why some return is owed; they do not fix its level, and the level was always the point. The rate is the figure in which the creditor-debtor contest records its result.

The modern defense is far stronger than its medieval opponents, and must be set out at full power. It makes three claims, each formidable. The first: interest is exchange, not extortion. Capital is a genuine factor of production, as real as land and labor; the man who supplies it gives something of value — the present command over resources — and is paid for it, his return no more a theft than the worker's wage or the landlord's rent. The second: the prohibition of interest does not shelter the poor but destroys them. Bentham's point has been confirmed by every experiment in suppression — forbid lending and you drive it into the dark, to the loan shark at rates the open market would never have borne, while the honest projects of the needy go unfunded for want of any lawful credit. The third: a positive rate does indispensable work, rationing the scarce supply of present resources among the uses competing for them, steering capital toward the projects that promise most. Abolish the rate and you do not free investment but blind it, for nothing would tell which venture deserved the world's limited savings. On this account the rate is no verdict on a contest but the solution to a problem every society must solve, and the lender is the borrower's benefactor, not his predator.

The defense makes three strong points. Interest is exchange, not theft: capital is a genuine factor, its return as earned as wage or rent. Prohibition does not protect the poor but strips them of credit, as Bentham argued. And a positive rate rations scarce resources among competing uses. On this account the rate solves a problem of coordination, and the lender is the borrower's benefactor.

Each of the three claims is true, and the thesis survives all three. Grant that capital is a genuine factor and its return earned: that establishes that some return is owed, not what — and the question was never whether the lender might be paid, but how much, and on what terms, the line between a fair reward for capital and the stripping of a borrower who cannot refuse. Grant that prohibition harms the poor: it follows only that prohibition is a clumsy instrument, not that whatever rate emerges is just; the choice was never between a free rate and no credit, but among the legal orders that decide what the borrower bears, and those are choices, not gifts of nature. And grant that the rate rations scarce capital: it rations by willingness to pay, steering capital toward those who can pay most because they need it least, and away from those who would use it most urgently and are charged the most — the flaw that mars every price that reads the purse in place of need. The defense proves that interest is not simply theft and that forbidding it is folly; it does not prove the rate natural, or just, or anything more than the terms on which a contest has been settled, usually against the weaker party. Keynes saw the residue of the old suspicion when he looked to the euthanasia of the rentier — the extinction, by abundant capital and low rates, of the income accruing to the mere owner of scarce funds, which he judged to reward no sacrifice but only a scarcity its possessor was lucky to hold. Marx put it more harshly, calling interest-bearing capital the purest fetish of the system: money that appears to breed money of itself.

Each point is sound and none decisive. That capital earns a return settles that something is owed, not what. That prohibition harms the poor shows prohibition the wrong instrument, not that any rate is just — the choice is among legal orders that shape what the borrower pays. And rationing by willingness to pay steers capital to the rich, who need it least, and away from the poor, who are charged most. Keynes looked to the euthanasia of the rentier; Marx called such gain capital's purest fetish.

The oldest question in the economics of the rate is also its deepest, and it has never once been retired. A price is a verdict on a hidden contest, mistaken for a fact of nature; and the price of time is the verdict in the longest-running contest of all — between the one who has, and can therefore wait, and the one who has not, and therefore cannot. The ancients condemned interest with a feeble argument, that money is barren, wrapped around a true perception, that the lender feeds upon the borrower's need. The moderns vindicated it with a powerful argument, that capital is productive and the bargain free, which quietly changed the subject, from the justice of the terms to the bare existence of a return. Between the weak truth of the one and the strong evasion of the other stands the question neither faced squarely: not whether the lender may be paid, but how much he may take, on what conditions, and with what force of law behind him — and that question is decided, in every age, by a balance of power wearing the mask of a natural law. To name the rate "the price of time" makes it sound like a reading taken from the clock. It is, in truth, a reading of who — in the immemorial struggle between creditor and debtor — the market and the law have permitted to win.

The ancients condemned interest with a weak argument around a true perception; the moderns vindicated it with a strong argument that changed the subject, from the justice of the terms to the existence of a return. The real question stands unanswered: not whether the lender may be paid, but how much, on what terms, and enforced by what law — settled, in every age, by a balance of power dressed as a fact of nature. To call the rate "the price of time" makes it sound like a reading of the clock; it reads, instead, who has been let prevail.

The medievals called it usury and forbade it; we call it interest and price it. The borrower's need, and the lender's gain from it, are unchanged.

Beneath the modern theory of the rate — the abstinence and the impatience, the productivity and the liquidity, the lever a central bank now holds — lies a contest as old as lending, over whether the price of time is the just reward of patience or the measure of one party's power over another's need; and the contest is settled not by any theory but by the law and the balance of force that fix the terms. What the volume's remaining plates take up are the workings of that settlement: the natural rate by which it is justified and the lever by which it is moved, the bond and the compound by which it is enforced, and the future toward which the whole apparatus of discounting finally points. The rate is the price of time, and the price of time is a verdict; who renders it, and in whose favor, is the question this volume has held open from its first page. The argument remains open.
