The Enclosure

in print · The Commons · plate v

The fence can strengthen the reason to improve a resource while taking an established use from someone else. Rights, investment and the English enclosure evidence show why productivity and distribution belong in the same inquiry. The gain from changing an arrangement does not settle who should receive the title, how existing claims should be recognized, or which consequences remain outside the boundary.

The larger harvest leaves the claims to be decided.The argument of this plate

The fence does two things at once. It can give someone a better reason to tend what lies within it while telling someone else that he may no longer enter. The first act is the familiar economic promise of enclosure: the person who bears the expense of improvement can expect to receive its return. The second is a change in the distribution of claims. A field may yield more grain after its boundaries are redrawn while a household that once used it loses its means of living. There is no contradiction. The gain in production and the loss of access belong to different accounts, and the fence cannot answer its own case by displaying only the first.

Enclosure does not always introduce rights where none existed. Common use can rest upon an established membership, seasons, limits and obligations. A person may have a right to graze animals without a right to sell the field, or a right to collect a product without a right to decide who else may enter. Schlager and Ostrom separated access, withdrawal, management, exclusion and alienation because the single word ownership conceals these differences. A reform may redistribute one of these powers, several of them, or all of them. The inquiry begins by identifying what the users already possess. Otherwise an apparent creation of property can conceal the destruction of another form of it.

Edella Schlager and Elinor Ostrom, “Property-Rights Regimes and Natural Resources” (1992), pp. 249–253. Their framework distinguishes operational rights from powers to make rules and transfer rights. Common property and unregulated open access are not interchangeable descriptions.

The economic promise is nevertheless real. Where one person pays for an improvement and others can take much of its yield, an investment worth making collectively may not be worth making alone. Retain the small example from the earlier Enclosure: four households share equally in an improvement worth twelve, but someone must first spend eight. A household paying the whole expense receives three and loses five. If all share the expense, each pays two, receives three and gains one. Giving one person the whole return would also make the investment attractive. The example establishes a problem of appropriating the return; it leaves open more than one way of solving it.

The explicitly hypothetical eight-for-twelve example is retained from the later Utility Enclosure. It assumes four equal shares, no enforcement or bargaining costs, and an improvement that actually produces the stated benefit. A unilateral investor’s payoff is 12/4 − 8 = −5; equal finance gives each 12/4 − 8/4 = 1.

Demsetz described the emergence of property rights as a response to circumstances in which bringing costs and benefits within a decision becomes worth the expense of doing so. A higher value of the resource, a new use for it, or a cheaper way of defining and enforcing a boundary can alter that reckoning. The argument directs attention to the price of the institution itself: surveying, bargaining, watching, adjudicating and defending. It does not establish that every boundary people succeed in drawing is efficient, or that a change advantageous to those who control the decision is advantageous to everyone it affects. An explanation of the incentive to establish a right is not yet a justification for awarding it.

Harold Demsetz, “Toward a Theory of Property Rights” (1967), the sections on the concept, role and emergence of rights, pp. 347–353. The proposition about changed benefits and costs is his theoretical account of institutional change.

The English experience cannot be reduced to a transfer from no owner to an owner. Parliamentary enclosure rearranged a complicated landscape of holdings and common rights. In their January 2023 study of English enclosures between 1750 and 1830, Heldring, Robinson and Vollmer examine a process involving consolidation, the settlement of claims and the implementation of new arrangements, rather than a single abstract switch called privatization. Their analysis finds higher agricultural yields alongside greater inequality in landholding. Those results matter together. They permit an account in which the land becomes more productive and its gains become less equally held, rather than requiring one finding to cancel the other.

The study also makes the comparison more demanding than putting enclosed parishes beside unenclosed ones. The places choosing Parliamentary enclosure could differ in what they stood to gain; some other places had already changed piecemeal. The authors use variation in the success of nearby enclosure petitions to construct an instrumental-variable comparison. Its causal interpretation depends on the proposed source of variation affecting the outcomes through enclosure rather than through other channels. Their principal estimates concern parishes whose enclosure decision responds to that variation. They are evidence about a particular historical process under stated assumptions, not a universal return promised to every fence.

Leander Heldring, James Robinson and Sebastian Vollmer, “The Economic Effects of the English Parliamentary Enclosures”, NBER Working Paper 29772, January 2023 revision, §1, §§2.1–2.4, §§4–5 and §8. Section 4 explains selection, the leave-one-out petition-success instrument and its exclusion restriction; §5 reports yield and land-inequality results. The identification argument is not a randomized allocation of enclosure, and the finding is not an estimate for all forms or periods of common-land reform.

The distributional question survives even if the productive gain is beyond dispute. Imagine four households whose established claims each yield twenty. A new arrangement can yield one hundred in total after all its resource costs. If one household receives the entire title and keeps the entire return, total value rises from eighty to one hundred while three households lose their twenty. There is room in this simple money-valued example to preserve their previous positions: paying each of the three twenty leaves the new owner forty, twice his earlier income. But the room for payment is not the payment. Without a term that delivers it, the claim that everyone could benefit describes an unrealized bargain.

This compensation example retains and develops the later Utility plate’s four claims of twenty and new total of one hundred. After payments of sixty to the three displaced households, the new owner retains forty. The figures assume comparable money values, recognized claims, a feasible transfer and no omitted costs. They do not price every possible loss or establish that consent has been obtained.

Some claims are also harder to carry into the new ledger than others. A surveyed holding can be recorded more easily than an informal use whose value appears in a household’s ability to survive a bad season. The cost of obtaining a new allotment, meeting an assessment or fencing it may arrive before its improved yield. A poor holder may therefore be unable to retain an asset that would have been profitable to keep. The question is not merely whether the final resource is worth more. It is whether the procedure recognizes the relevant claims, whether people can meet the transition’s demands, and whether the announced compensation can arrive when it is needed.

Nor does exclusive title make every consequence exclusive. A landowner can prevent another person from taking his crop while allowing runoff to damage a neighboring stream. A fishery right defined by a catch share may fail to protect the habitat on which future catches depend. Even the future value retained by the owner can be sacrificed if immediate liquidation is more attractive to him. Schlager and Ostrom emphasize that a secure right can encourage investment without guaranteeing sustainable use, and that the power to sell a resource is not a necessary condition for every successful investment in it. The useful question is which consequences a particular bundle brings into view, and which it leaves outside.

Schlager and Ostrom, pp. 253–257 and 259–260, distinguish the incentives of different rights holders and qualify any inference from ownership or alienability to investment and conservation. The crop, runoff and habitat cases here are analytical illustrations; no empirical conservation outcome is inferred from the label on the title.

A larger harvest can justify changing an arrangement. It cannot, by itself, decide who is entitled to the harvest.

These distinctions change what counts as an alternative. The choice need not be between a pasture open without limit and a single private proprietor. A group can exclude outsiders while dividing use among its members; it can retain common management while assigning individual withdrawals; it can make a long lease secure without surrendering every power over the land. Such arrangements have costs and can fail. So can individual ownership, which still requires boundaries, enforcement and rules for effects crossing into someone else’s domain. Comparative judgment has to set workable institutions beside one another, carrying each institution’s costs into the account.

By what right is the fence raised? A claim about higher output offers one reason to consider a change. The rights already held, the authority to alter them, the people allowed to participate, and the terms on which losses are borne supply the rest of the inquiry. Enclosure can secure care that a previous arrangement discouraged; it can also concentrate a gain that others helped make possible. Its defense must explain both acts. The next plate keeps the resource under a public rule instead, and asks whether the authority that can command restraint can learn enough to command it well.
