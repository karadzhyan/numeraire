As ix

The Mandate

in print · The Asymmetry · plate ix

A command to reveal information begins a chain that ends, if it succeeds, in a decision the buyer can make. The required fact, its unit, its timing and its reader each matter. Following a dealer's warranty disclosure and a historical mortgage experiment separates the existence of an account from its comprehension, while asking what intermediaries and enforcement can accomplish when the buyer never reads it.

The required account needs a reader who can act.The argument of this plate

The law can order a seller to speak, but the sentence must still reach the person for whom it was required. Between a fact in the seller's possession and an informed purchase stand several obstacles: concealment, unfamiliar terms, an inconvenient comparison, the hour at which the information arrives. A disclosure mandate addresses the first by command and the others only through its design. It can make the lender name a charge or the dealer describe a warranty while leaving the customer free to choose. That modest appearance gives disclosure much of its appeal. The harder question is whether the required telling changes what the buyer can understand and do—and what protection remains necessary when a complete account would exceed the buyer's time or knowledge.

Voluntary disclosure has a powerful logic when silence is itself informative. A seller able to prove superior quality has a reason to distinguish the product from the average. Once that seller speaks, the best of the remaining sellers has the same reason; an unfavorable inference can work its way down through those still silent. The argument depends on the proof being available and credible, the cost of producing it being low enough, and purchasers understanding what silence means. A seller who has never tested the product may look just like a seller hiding a bad result. A costly test may be worth obtaining for a large producer and prohibitive for a small one. Where those possibilities remain, a missing statement cannot simply be read as the worst possible statement.

Dranove and Jin, “Quality Disclosure and Certification: Theory and Practice”, NBER Working Paper 15644 (January 2010), §II.1, pp. 12–16, sets out the unraveling argument and departures involving verification costs, information, market structure and consumers' inferences. The passage uses the retained working-paper version and treats disclosure as conditional behavior.

A mandate can make comparable information available even when no individual seller has enough reason to supply it. It can choose the fact to be reported, prescribe a common unit, specify when the buyer receives it, and make an omitted answer visible as an omission. Each decision matters. The useful unit for a household choosing a loan may differ from the unit convenient for the lender's accounts; a fact supplied after the borrower has paid substantial costs may be less useful than the same fact supplied while alternatives remain open. Standardization also lets another person compare offers on the buyer's behalf. The law is organizing a means of inquiry, with a producer, an audience and a moment when the answer can still affect the bargain.

The federal Used Car Rule shows how specific that organization can be. For transactions and dealers within its scope, the rule requires a Buyers Guide on the vehicle before it is offered for sale to a consumer. The guide identifies the dealer's warranty position and, when a dealer warranty is offered, describes such matters as covered systems, duration and the share of repair costs the dealer will pay. The final guide becomes part of the sales contract and overrides contrary contract provisions. This is a useful answer to a particular uncertainty: what responsibility the dealer has undertaken. It does not purport to be a complete mechanical history of the vehicle. The buyer's need to learn about condition survives the disclosure of the terms on which defects will be borne.

16 C.F.R. part 455, especially §§455.1–455.4, in the eCFR text current through October 16, 2026. The part defines the covered vehicles, dealers and transactions; §455.2(b)(1)(ii) preserves state limits on “as is” sales, and §455.6 provides for state exemptions. The guide's contract effect comes from §455.3(b). A disclosure of warranty responsibility should not be confused with a certification of the vehicle's condition.

Comprehension requires a further translation from the disclosed term to its consequence. Imagine two invented loans, each placing nine hundred and fifty dollars in the borrower's hands today, with a single payment exactly one year later. The first calls itself a thousand-dollar loan, withholds fifty as a fee and requires eleven hundred at the end. The second advances nine hundred and fifty without a fee and requires one thousand and fifty. The first costs the borrower one hundred and fifty for the use of nine hundred and fifty; the second costs one hundred. A ten-percent interest figure on the first loan's stated principal leaves the withheld charge outside that simple comparison. On the cash actually received, its one-year cost is about 15.8 percent. The numbers become useful when their denominators and payment dates are visible together.

The loans are constructed for this plate, with no interim payments or other charges: (1,100 − 950) ÷ 950 ≈ 15.79 percent, and (1,050 − 950) ÷ 950 ≈ 10.53 percent. This is a one-period cash-flow comparison, not a statement of the legal annual-percentage-rate calculation for an actual loan. The fixed term and equal net advance make the fifty-dollar difference unambiguous.

Changing the form can change what people understand without first changing their intelligence. In a study published in 2007, FTC economists James Lacko and Janis Pappalardo tested then-current mortgage disclosures and a prototype with 819 recent mortgage customers at twelve sites. Respondents examined hypothetical fixed-rate loans and answered questions about their costs. The groups using the then-current forms answered roughly sixty-one percent of the relevant questions correctly; the prototype groups answered roughly eighty percent. The prototype reorganized the account, combined information and changed what was included, so the result cannot be assigned to larger type alone. It establishes a concrete possibility: a disclosure that exists on paper can convey substantially more when its content and organization are designed for the decision being made.

Lacko and Pappalardo, Improving Consumer Mortgage Disclosures (FTC Bureau of Economics staff report, June 2007), ch. 4, pp. 41–58; §6.1, pp. 69–76; ch. 7, pp. 121–128. Testing occurred in 2005. The sample was not nationally representative; the comparison used hypothetical loans in a controlled setting. These are historical forms, and improved recognition of costs is distinct from demonstrated improvements in actual borrowing outcomes.

The same experiment also leaves room for the difficulty the mandate was meant to overcome. Important misunderstandings survived in the prototype groups. A borrower at an actual closing may face time pressure, many other papers and a need for funds that makes refusal costly; the experimental respondent also lacks the full personal stakes of an actual loan. Those differences can pull in different directions. A successful comprehension test is evidence that information can be conveyed more effectively. The next inquiry is whether people use it at the relevant moment, whether suitable alternatives exist, and whether the resulting choices serve the interests the disclosure was intended to protect. A rule should be evaluated through those steps rather than credited with the last because it achieved the first.

An unread disclosure can still have consequences if someone else reads it and can act. A comparison service may translate the figures, a rival may expose an unfavorable term, or an enforcement body may discover that the seller's actual conduct differs from the filed account. Anticipation of that scrutiny can affect the offer before the customer sees it. The useful question therefore reaches beyond the proportion of buyers who read every page. It asks who uses the information, how their incentives relate to the buyer's interests, and whether the record enables a consequential challenge. A dormant file with no reader and no practical route to correction offers much less than a public statement that another institution can test.

Required disclosure also changes what suppliers have reason to measure and improve. A score based on an observable outcome can reward genuine improvement, yet it can also reward selecting easier cases or concentrating effort on the part of quality that receives a number. An honest report can therefore coexist with conduct that defeats the purpose of the reporting rule. The danger depends on the measure and the opportunities it creates. A useful response can include showing the population behind an average, preserving dimensions that a single rank suppresses, or testing whether the underlying service changes alongside the reported score. Publishing more figures helps only when those figures bear on what the buyer needs to know.

The measurement and response problem is developed in Dranove and Jin, §II.1, including the distinctions between disclosure costs, information acquisition and changes in measured dimensions. The passage makes a conditional institutional argument; it does not infer that an observed high score, or a particular provider's conduct, is fraudulent.

The justification for compulsion should be as definite as the fact compelled. A seller's control of information material to the proposed exchange gives a reason to require an accurate, timely account, particularly where buyers cannot feasibly obtain it themselves. The rule still has to consider the cost of producing that account, the privacy of other people whose records it would expose, and the usefulness of the information to its intended reader. A request for the essential terms of a bargain and a demand to publish every fact in a firm's possession impose different burdens. More demanding protections may be justified where the purchaser cannot interpret the relevant evidence at any practical cost, or where an error causes harm that a later remedy cannot repair. Disclosure and substantive protection can then work together.

A fact becomes protective when someone can use it.

The telling, unread, may preserve evidence or discipline the teller; its sufficiency depends on the institution that makes those effects real. For the purchaser himself, the promise of disclosure is fulfilled when the concealed term becomes an intelligible choice while he can still choose. That requires a limited, usable account and an answer when the account is false. The next plate moves to the harder case in which even a faithful description leaves the buyer dependent on expert judgment: the service whose necessity he cannot readily evaluate after receiving it. There the law must decide how to make a trusted adviser answerable as well as how to make the adviser speak. The argument remains open.
