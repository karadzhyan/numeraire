The Tail

in print · The Premium · plate viii

Quiet years can conceal an obligation that has yet to fall due. This plate distinguishes the probability of a rare loss from its severity, its dependence on other losses and the capacity of the promised bearer to pay. The passage from an apparently safe record through a thin balance sheet to forced sales and public guarantees asks which part of a premium rewards resources truly committed, and which part leaves the danger elsewhere.

The uneventful account records the receipts; the promise records what may still be owed.The argument of this plate

A premium can arrive every year while the event that earns it remains absent. The account then shows income with little disturbance, and the person collecting it acquires the appearance of a steady producer of value. Yet the transaction may have sold a promise whose cost has simply not fallen due. A rare loss can consume years of receipts, exhaust the capital behind the promise and leave someone else to meet the remainder. This is the problem of the tail: observation is richest where events are ordinary, while the survival of the arrangement may depend on events scarcely represented in the record. To understand the return, we must follow the possible loss all the way through the promise to the resources that would actually absorb it.

A distribution gives the range of outcomes and the probabilities attached to them. Its tails concern outcomes far from the familiar center. When large deviations are more frequent than a normal distribution would suggest, a model built around that bell curve can understate the chance of a severe loss. Rama Cont’s survey of financial returns describes heavy tails among the recurring empirical features of those series, alongside volatility clustering and other forms of dependence. This finding does not establish one universal law for every asset, horizon or hazard. A heavy tail need not imply an infinite variance, and an apparent power law in a finite sample need not settle the behavior beyond it. The useful lesson is exacting: the shape imposed on the unobserved part of a distribution can determine a result that the observed center scarcely tests.

Rama Cont, “Empirical Properties of Asset Returns: Stylized Facts and Statistical Issues” (2001), §§1–4, pp. 223–229. Cont distinguishes marginal distributions, tail estimation and dependence, and emphasizes uncertainty about tail parameters. The survey does not make every financial return series an exact power law or imply that every relevant variance is infinite.

Rarity alone can produce a deceptively uneventful history. Suppose, by invention, that a particular disaster has a one-percent chance each year, independent across years and unchanged over time. The chance of twenty years without it is about eighty-two percent. Even a century without it has a probability of about thirty-seven percent. A hundred-year label under those assumptions would describe an annual probability, not an appointment that becomes overdue as the calendar advances. In practice, the annual probability itself may be uncertain or changing, and independence may be a poor description. Those difficulties deepen the inference problem. The invented calculation already shows why a quiet twenty-year account cannot, by its quietness alone, refute a risk large enough to matter.

An invented constant-hazard illustration. The probabilities of no event are 0.99²⁰ ≈ 0.8179 and 0.99¹⁰⁰ ≈ 0.3660. Independence and a stable one-percent annual probability are stipulated, not estimated from a particular flood, market or catastrophe record.

Nor can a single threshold tell us how costly the breach will be. Compare two invented loss distributions. In each, there is a ninety-nine-percent chance of no loss. The remaining one percent brings a loss of a hundred in the first and a thousand in the second. Define the ninety-nine-percent value-at-risk as the smallest loss at which cumulative probability reaches ninety-nine percent: it is zero in both. Yet the average loss in the worst one percent is a hundred in one case and a thousand in the other; the unconditional expected losses are one and ten. These are bounded distributions, so no claim about an infinite tail is involved. The example separates a quantile from the severity beyond it. A number answering how often a boundary is crossed cannot, unaided, answer what lies on its other side.

Invented distributions with probability masses 0.99 at zero and 0.01 at the stated loss. Under the specified quantile convention, both 99% value-at-risk measures equal zero. The average of the worst 1% equals the nonzero loss. A different confidence level would ask a different question; no threshold is a maximum-loss guarantee.

The loss also has a date and a setting. A catastrophe that destroys many incomes at once makes resources scarce just when claims on those resources multiply. The premium required to bear it can consequently exceed the expected monetary loss: an additional dollar is especially valuable in the bad state. Barro’s rare-disaster framework develops this relation through a specified process for aggregate consumption. The argument needs both the probability distribution and the pricing of consumption across its states. It cannot be reduced to adding an arbitrary fear surcharge. Two promises with equal expected losses can command different prices if one fails when the rest of the holder’s means have also failed. A diversified collection of names can remain exposed to that common condition.

Robert J. Barro, “Rare Events and the Equity Premium” (2005), NBER Working Paper 11310, §§I.A and I.C, pp. 2–6 and 11–13. The May 2005 model specifies the rare contraction and the consumption preferences that give losses in that state their price. A large catastrophe premium is a conditional result of those ingredients.

But the price of a promise and the capacity to perform it are separate matters. Consider an invented protection seller with twenty dollars of owners’ capital, a two-dollar premium collected immediately, and no other assets, costs or investment return. It promises a hundred dollars if a disaster occurs, with a stipulated probability of one percent. The expected contractual loss is only one dollar. Nevertheless, if the disaster arrives, the seller has twenty-two dollars available and falls seventy-eight short. A premium above expected loss has not made the promise secure. The buyer has purchased a claim whose performance depends on a balance sheet, and the balance sheet does not contain the resources the claim may require.

The same example explains how the private reward can exceed the reward for bearing the full loss. Assume limited liability, no outside recovery and no rescue. In the ordinary state the owners receive twenty-two, a gain of two on their initial capital. In the disaster state they receive nothing, losing twenty. Their expected gain is therefore 1.78. If the promise were fully performed from owners’ resources, its expected profit before any charge for the capital committed would be one: the premium of two less the expected claim of one. The difference, 0.78, is exactly the expected unpaid claim, one percent of the missing seventy-eight. The arithmetical advantage comes from loss left with the buyer. It describes this stipulated contract and capitalization, not an equilibrium in which an informed buyer must willingly pay the same premium for weak and strong protection.

An invented one-period balance sheet. With limited liability, expected owners’ gain is 0.99 × 2 − 0.01 × 20 = 1.78. Expected unpaid claims are 0.01 × 78 = 0.78; their sum with the 0.22 expected payment equals the full expected claim of 1. The comparison excludes expenses, investment returns, a capital charge and rescue, and does not determine a fair market premium.

Protection can of course be organized with resources capable of meeting the promise. Capital, collateral, reinsurance and public guarantees can change who pays and how much is available. Each must be followed to its own source. A reinsurance contract transfers a claim to another institution; the institution must itself be able to perform when the common event arrives. Collateral provides a resource, whose value and availability at that moment matter. A public guarantee moves a contingent obligation onto public finances. None of these arrangements restores destroyed goods merely by renaming the liability. They can distribute the financial burden, sustain spending when private resources are depleted and make a promise more reliable. Their cost and their capacity are part of the service for which the premium is paid.

A loss can also alter the behavior of those required to bear it. Take an invented investor holding assets worth two hundred, financed by debt of a hundred and eighty and equity of twenty. A five-percent fall in asset value reduces the assets to a hundred and ninety and halves the equity to ten. To restore the original ratio of ten dollars of assets for each dollar of equity, the investor must sell ninety of assets and use the proceeds to repay debt, leaving a hundred of assets and ninety of debt. This calculation assumes the sales themselves do not move prices. Where many constrained holders sell into a market with limited buying capacity, that assumption can fail, and further falls can produce further pressure to sell.

An invented scaling of the leverage mechanism: 200 − 180 = 20 initially; after a 5% asset loss, 190 − 180 = 10. Restoring asset/equity leverage of ten requires assets of 100 and debt of 90, hence sales and repayment of 90. See Markus K. Brunnermeier (2009), pp. 91–94, for the loss and margin spirals and their dependence on funding and market liquidity.

The tail of a portfolio can therefore depend on institutions as well as on the separate hazards of the investments. Margin requirements, short-term funding, common creditors and interconnected promises can transmit a disturbance. Falling prices can damage the collateral supporting obligations elsewhere; a need for immediate cash can force sales of holdings whose underlying operations have suffered little. Brunnermeier’s account of the crisis distinguishes these channels instead of treating panic as a sufficient explanation. Their force depends on leverage, contractual demands, market liquidity and the resources of potential buyers. Diversification can still help against independent losses. It cannot be presumed to cancel a funding condition that makes several holders act together precisely in the state being insured against.

Brunnermeier, “Deciphering the Liquidity and Credit Crunch 2007–2008” (2009), “Amplifying Mechanisms and Recurring Themes,” pp. 91–98. Funding liquidity, market liquidity, runs and counterparty connections are distinct mechanisms. Amplification requires the relevant balance-sheet and market conditions; no identity forces all asset correlations to become one.

Who finally bears the loss is consequently a question about a chain of claims. Owners may absorb it through diminished equity, creditors through unpaid debts, insured households through claims that cannot be met, or a public institution through a guarantee it honors. The wider effects can reach workers and suppliers if damaged finance interrupts production. These consequences need not all occur, and their relative importance cannot be read from the name of the catastrophe. They depend on the contracts, the capital and the response. A payment between two parties reallocates purchasing power; destroyed productive resources and disrupted output concern the economy’s capacity to provide goods. Confusing the transfer with the underlying loss can make both the cost of rescue and the benefit of insurance unintelligible.

A placid return history leaves these questions unanswered, but it does not make them unanswerable. The promised payments can be inspected, the resources behind them identified, and their behavior under a common adverse condition considered. Such an account remains conditional on the event and dependence it has imagined. It is nevertheless more informative about the bearer than the observation that no one has yet demanded payment. Steady returns can represent a useful service honestly supplied; they can also contain compensation for a rare obligation, or gains enlarged by leaving part of that obligation elsewhere. Distinguishing these possibilities requires following the transaction. The smooth line of receipts is evidence of what has happened, and only part of the evidence of what has been sold.

The premium can be collected before the bearer of the loss has been found.

The tail brings the justification of the premium to its hardest accounting. Probability, severity, dependence and capacity must all be specified before the income can be understood as a reward for absorbing danger. Where capital truly stands behind the promise, the uneventful years can be years of service: resources have been committed against a loss that mercifully did not arrive. Where the promise exceeds those resources, some of the apparent reward may be purchased with another person’s exposure. The Bearer follows that incidence, while The Uncertainty asks what remains when even the map of possible events resists specification. The tail is the part of the known map that calm weather makes easiest to forget. The argument remains open.
