Cm vi

The Warden

in print · The Commons · plate vi

The authority that can impose a limit still has to learn what the limit should mean in practice. A catch allocation, an enforcement calculation and the distinction between scientific and local knowledge reveal the work hidden inside a rule. Public power can protect cooperation across boundaries while leaving defined decisions with those who know the resource.

The power to command does not confer the knowledge to choose.The argument of this plate

The warden can close the gate. That is his advantage over the commoner who asks his neighbors to restrain themselves while strangers continue to enter. He can make a limit binding, require a license, reserve a place from use, and give a rule a reach beyond the people willing to accept it voluntarily. But the ability to close the gate does not tell him which gate to close, when to reopen it, or what the restriction will do to the living thing behind it. Authority supplies a means of acting together. Knowledge must still supply the act with its direction.

The commons asks public power to solve more than the users’ immediate disagreement. A community may be able to agree among its present members and still be unable to restrain outsiders, protect downstream users or bind a future arrival. Its chosen boundary may leave much of the resource beyond its reach. A wider authority can establish a limit within which local arrangements operate, recognize rights against outsiders and provide a forum for disputes between groups. These are reasons for public involvement that do not depend on supposing the users incapable of cooperation. Cooperation inside one boundary can remain harmful outside it.

Elinor Ostrom, “Beyond Markets and States: Polycentric Governance of Complex Economic Systems”, Nobel lecture (2009), §§2A, 6 and 8. The polycentric argument concerns connected centers of decision and the fit between institutions and problems; it is not a claim that every resource can be governed by an isolated local group.

Hayek’s objection begins where the order’s general purpose leaves off. Much of the knowledge needed for action concerns particular circumstances: a changing condition, an unused capacity, a practicable adjustment, a cost that appears only at this place and time. It is not all waiting in a single ledger to be transmitted upward. Some of it can be articulated only through participation in the work; some loses its value before the report arrives. A central authority that knows the desired aggregate result may consequently lack the information required to choose each means of reaching it. The warden can know that the river must be protected without knowing every use through which that protection can be obtained.

That difficulty does not make the person nearest the river an oracle. A user may know his own reach intimately and know little about the aquifer feeding it, the cumulative effect of other withdrawals or the organism whose decline has not yet become visible. Scientific knowledge and local knowledge can answer different parts of the same question. Hayek distinguished those kinds of knowledge; he did not make expertise dispensable. The institutional task is to bring them into a decision without pretending either that all useful information can be centralized or that all useful information is already present locally.

F. A. Hayek, “The Use of Knowledge in Society” (1945), §§I–VII, pp. 519–530, especially §§III–V on scientific knowledge, knowledge of particular circumstances, and the need to communicate changes. Applying that distinction to resource management is the argument of this plate, not a conservation result established in Hayek’s paper.

A uniform rule can satisfy a total while wasting the means of meeting it. The earlier Leviathan offered an invented fishery with a total catch ceiling of six. One user’s net benefit from catch q is ten times q minus half its square; the other’s is six times q minus half its square. Give each a quota of three and their combined benefit is thirty-nine. Allocate five to the first and one to the second, and the combined benefit becomes forty-three. The same total catch produces a larger return because the last unit is now equally valuable to each user. An authority that knows only the ceiling cannot infer that allocation from the ceiling alone.

The hypothetical model is retained from the later Utility Leviathan. At (3, 3), benefits are 25.5 + 13.5 = 39; at (5, 1), they are 37.5 + 5.5 = 43. Along q₁ + q₂ = 6, the strictly concave objective is maximized when 10 − q₁ = 6 − q₂. The model assumes equally damaging units and contains no independent proof that six is a sustainable ceiling.

The qualification about equal damage matters as much as the arithmetic. Two units counted as the same catch may have different effects if one is taken during spawning, in a nursery, or with equipment that damages habitat. A transferable quota can allow users to discover a less costly division of the permitted total while failing to control these other margins. A uniform restriction can be easy to inspect while fitting the biology poorly. The response need not be a separate command for every conceivable circumstance. It is to decide which differences are consequential enough to enter the rule, which can be left to users, and what evidence will trigger a revision.

Information also has a supplier with an interest. A user reporting the cost of restraint may face a lower obligation if the cost appears high. An official reporting the condition of a resource may prefer evidence that makes an earlier decision look successful. Giving either report unquestioned authority is therefore a poor substitute for local knowledge. A workable system needs ways to compare claims with observations, let affected parties challenge errors, and make a revision possible before the damage becomes irreversible. The difficulty of governing from afar is partly a difficulty of distance and partly a difficulty of eliciting an account that the decision maker can trust.

The problem is not solved merely by announcing a severe penalty. Suppose, in another invented example, an illegal withdrawal gives its taker a net gain of twelve, detection occurs with probability one-fifth, and the only additional consequence is a fine of forty. A risk-neutral user then faces an expected fine of eight and still gains four from the violation. Raising detection to two-fifths makes the expected fine sixteen. The stated prohibition has not changed; its practical force has. This small calculation does not recommend a particular fine or surveillance system. It identifies monitoring, collection and the cost of obtaining reliable evidence as part of the rule itself.

The enforcement figures are original and deliberately narrow. The expected private gain is 12 − pF: 4 at p = 0.2 and F = 40, and −4 at p = 0.4. The break-even detection probability is 0.3. Risk attitudes, reputational effects, mistaken detection, collection limits, legal procedure and monitoring costs are absent from the arithmetic and would matter to an actual design.

Rules made with users can improve what the authority knows and what users are prepared to enforce. Ostrom’s analysis of resource institutions emphasizes fit, monitoring and the capacity to revise arrangements. Schlager and Ostrom further show why a right to withdraw a resource differs from a right to participate in making the rules for withdrawal. To consult users after the operative decisions have already been fixed is not equivalent to giving them a defined part in those decisions. Yet local authority also needs limits: a group capable of policing its members may exclude another group’s legitimate claim or conceal harm beyond its borders.

Ostrom, Nobel lecture, §6, pp. 426–429; Schlager and Ostrom, “Property-Rights Regimes and Natural Resources” (1992), pp. 249–257 and 259–260. The distinction between operational rights and collective-choice rights supports this allocation of responsibility; local participation alone is not a sufficient condition for good outcomes.

The warden need not know everything the users know. His rule must leave a place for what they know, and a means of testing what they say.

One possible arrangement therefore divides the work without pretending to divide the resource. A wider authority establishes an ecological ceiling and protects claims that a local group cannot fairly judge by itself. Users help specify workable practices within that ceiling. Independent observation checks both the condition of the resource and the reports made about it. A forum hears disputes, while a stated revision process allows the rule to change with evidence. This is an institutional possibility, not a universal blueprint. Each division creates its own need for coordination, and multiplying offices can produce delay or evasion as readily as correction. The purpose is to place decisions where relevant knowledge and answerability can meet.

The original question can thus be answered without imagining either an omniscient sovereign or a commons saved by the sovereign’s absence. A public rule can fit a resource when the power to impose it is joined to arrangements for learning, participation, scrutiny and change. Its reach can protect local cooperation rather than replace it; its authority can settle conflicts that local cooperation leaves unresolved. The test is what the rule enables people to know and do, not the distance of the desk from the water. The next plate enters the daily machinery through which an agreed limit becomes a lived one: watching, warning, correction and sanction.
