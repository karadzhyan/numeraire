Ct vii

The Holdup

in print · The Contract · plate vii

A commitment valuable chiefly within one relationship can leave its maker exposed when the terms are reopened. The gap between the investment worth making and the return its maker can keep explains a loss that may occur before any dispute. Competing accounts of Fisher Body and General Motors then lead to the costs of safeguards, ownership and continued dependence.

The investment can be lost before it is made.The argument of this plate, after Klein, Crawford, Alchian, Coase, Grossman and Hart

The machine was worth building because there was a buyer. Now that it has been built, the buyer knows how little it is worth to anyone else. A tool shaped for one component, a pipeline running to one refinery, a skill acquired for one employer: each joins productive usefulness to a dependence that was weaker before the investment. The price agreed while the investor could still walk away may become difficult to defend when walking away means abandoning the work. The holdup problem begins in that change of position. Its deepest cost can arrive before anyone demands a revision, when the prospect of having the terms reopened makes a useful investment too dangerous to undertake.

An investment is specific to a relationship when its value there exceeds what it can obtain in its best alternative use. That difference gives the parties something to preserve and something to dispute. Klein, Crawford, and Alchian distinguished the quasi-rent of an installed asset from the part another party can appropriate because substitutes are poor. A sunk outlay by itself establishes neither dependence nor exploitation. A machine with many competing users may command its value elsewhere. The dangerous combination is an investment already committed, an alternative that yields much less, and a promise or division of authority that leaves the resulting surplus exposed.

Benjamin Klein, Robert G. Crawford, and Armen A. Alchian, “Vertical Integration, Appropriable Rents, and the Competitive Contracting Process,” Journal of Law and Economics 21 (1978), introduction and §I, pp. 297–302, distinguish quasi-rents from their appropriable portion. Its note 4 expressly retains contracting problems within an integrated organization.

Consider a supplier who can spend seventy to make a specialized tool. With the tool, a buyer can obtain a product worth a hundred; operating costs are zero in this illustration, and the tool can fetch only twenty elsewhere. A binding price of eighty-five would leave fifteen for each party after the investment has been paid for. The total gain is thirty. Suppose instead that the price remains open and, once the tool is made, the parties divide equally the gain from trading rather than taking their outside options. The supplier can obtain twenty elsewhere and the buyer can obtain nothing. Their gain from agreement is eighty, so each receives forty above those alternatives. The price becomes sixty. Production still makes sense after the tool exists, but the supplier receives ten less than the seventy spent to create it.

Anticipating that outcome, the supplier declines to invest. A bargain that could have created thirty never appears. The difference between eighty-five and sixty would have been a transfer between the parties if the investment were already fixed; the disappearance of the investment is a loss to them together. This is why the absence of a visible dispute can be misleading. A quiet market may contain projects that were never attempted because the prospective investor expected to lose the return. Nor does the example condemn all bargaining after investment. If the tool could fetch eighty elsewhere, the same equal division would produce a price of ninety, leaving twenty after the outlay. A better alternative changes the incentive even though the bargaining rule remains unchanged.

These are this plate’s illustrations of the investment problem analyzed by Klein, Crawford, and Alchian, §I. With buyer value 100 and supplier alternative s, the assumed price is s + (100 − s)/2. The outlay of 70 precedes bargaining; parties know these values, and no enforceable earlier price fixes the division. The example illustrates a possible source of underinvestment, not an unavoidable outcome of specificity.

The safeguard has to operate early enough to change that decision. A long-term purchase commitment, a rule for adjusting prices, a payment made before the specialized work, or an asset pledged against nonperformance may make a promised return credible. Each requires attention to what can be observed and enforced. A fixed price can protect the supplier while exposing the buyer to inferior quality; a cost-based price can accommodate an uncertain expense while weakening the reason to economize. The contract must preserve enough room to respond to events without turning that room into a standing invitation to reopen the bargain. The problem is to secure the investment and still govern the performance it makes possible.

Fisher Body and General Motors became the famous historical illustration of this difficulty. In their 1978 account, Klein, Crawford, and Alchian described a long-term agreement for automobile bodies whose cost-plus provisions became troublesome as demand changed. They argued that Fisher could benefit from arrangements costly to GM and presented complete integration as a response to this exposure. That account helped make the case an emblem of asset specificity. It was also disputed. Coase’s 2000 reexamination challenged both the interpretation of plant locations and the evidence for opportunistic production choices. He emphasized existing cooperation, financing arrangements, and the interests of Fisher’s other customers. The acquisition is a historical event; the explanation of it is an argument among authors.

Klein, Crawford, and Alchian, §III.A, pp. 308–310; R. H. Coase, “The Acquisition of Fisher Body by General Motors,” Journal of Law and Economics 43 (2000), §I, pp. 15–16, and §VI, pp. 27–30. Coase notes that GM already owned 60 percent before acquiring the remaining interest in 1926. The two accounts are identified here as competing interpretations; their historical evidence is not replaced by the numerical model.

The controversy improves the question if it makes the institutional comparison more exact. A refusal to finance a new plant, for example, can concern who should bear a commitment that serves one customer while limiting service to others. To call it holdup requires an account of the obligations, alternatives, and gains being exploited. Coase placed greater weight on long-term contracts and the restraint supplied by future business. Those devices work when there is something valuable to lose through a damaged relationship and when the relevant behavior can be recognized. A reputation that cannot follow an actor, or a future that neither party expects to share, provides less security. The alternative to integration must be described as an institution with its own conditions, just as integration must be.

Common ownership changes the rights over the physical assets and can thereby change the terms of future bargaining. A supplier who owns a critical machine can withhold its use; a buyer who acquires it changes that outside option. Yet the people who supply skill, effort, and judgment remain people with decisions to make. Grossman and Hart made the resulting tradeoff central: acquiring control can strengthen one party’s investment incentive while weakening the other’s. The earlier inquiry into residual control therefore returns inside the proposed cure. Who can decide after the contract runs out, and whose preparation becomes less rewarding under that allocation of power?

Sanford J. Grossman and Oliver D. Hart, “The Costs and Benefits of Ownership: A Theory of Vertical and Lateral Integration,” Journal of Political Economy 94 (1986), §I.A, pp. 691–693, develop the costs as well as the benefits of allocating residual rights. Ownership of physical assets changes incentives; it does not acquire another person’s future choices outright.

A protection must also be worth its price. Return to the project that would create thirty. If an enforceable arrangement costs five in real resources and permits the investment to occur, twenty-five remains. If the arrangement consumes thirty-five, it has spent more than the project creates. These comparisons count the resources used to write, monitor, and operate the safeguard. A payment from one party to the other is a different item: it changes their shares and may alter their willingness to proceed, but it is not by itself an additional resource cost to the pair. The same distinction applies to a purchase of the supplier. The acquisition price and the cost of running the combined organization answer different questions.

The worker’s predicament follows the same dependence without making the employment relation identical to the ownership of a tool. Learning a firm’s particular systems can raise what the worker produces there while adding little to what another employer will pay. Training financed by the firm can likewise leave the firm exposed if the worker departs. A career structure, a deferred reward, and a continuing relation may help distribute and protect the gains; they can also make leaving more costly. The safeguard for one side can become a constraint on the other. A convincing account of security must therefore ask whose investment it protects and whose exit it burdens, as well as whether the joint product becomes larger.

The worker-and-plant comparison develops the earlier Collected Holdup and Utility Holdup. The comparison concerns relationship-specific returns and bargaining incentives; it does not turn a worker into an asset that an employer can own. The investment and safeguard arithmetic in this plate is illustrative.

The loss can be the investment that fear of a later bargain prevents.

The holdup problem asks how people can commit themselves without surrendering the reason to commit. A longer document, a common owner, a valuable reputation, and a stronger outside option can each change the answer, at different costs and for different participants. None can be assessed solely by the dispute it appears to settle; the investments it encourages and the choices it suppresses belong in the same account. Where the parties have left their protection unwritten, another author may already have supplied terms.
