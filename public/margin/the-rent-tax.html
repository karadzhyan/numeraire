The Tax

in print · The Rent · plate xi

A levy on a fixed site can leave production unchanged while changing both the owner’s income and the price of the title. Ricardo’s separation of land from improvements provides the test; George’s proposal supplies the larger ambition. Capitalization, valuation and the use of the revenue then explain why an efficient instrument still needs a carefully specified transition.

A tax can leave the productive margin intact and still change the value of a life’s savings.The argument of this plate, after Ricardo, Mill and George

The attractive thing about a tax on land is what the owner cannot do in response: make the earth cease to exist. A tax on an additional building may change the decision to build it; a charge on a site that is due whether the owner builds or waits need not do so. This is the economist’s reason for returning to rent. The state needs revenue, and much of the revenue it raises alters the terms on which people work, invest and exchange. A claim on an advantage already there seems to offer something rarer: a way to change who receives the surplus without reducing the activity that produces it. The promise is real. The word without needs care.

Ricardo began with the clean case. A tax confined to the differential rent of land would leave the productive differences between sites unchanged. The marginal land in his example paid no rent and no tax; the better land would still yield its advantage, with the state now taking part of the landlord’s receipt. But his next paragraph immediately complicated the bill called rent. It commonly paid for buildings, fixtures and other expenditure as well as the land. A tax on that whole payment could discourage investment. The distinction the theorem requires is therefore one an administration must actually make. Labeling the bill a rent tax does not perform the separation.

Ricardo, Principles, chapter X, “Taxes on Rent” (1821 third edition) presents the pure-rent case and immediately distinguishes the landlord’s capital in buildings and improvements. Both parts of the chapter are essential to the argument.

Imagine a fixed set of sites, all already available for use, with competitive bidding that leaves one site an annual pure rent of one hundred. A new charge takes twenty a year from its owner, whatever the permitted use chosen. Keep demand, the supply of sites and the return on improvements unchanged. The tenant’s reason to pay has not increased; the site’s advantage is the same. The owner receives eighty after tax, while the public receives twenty. Under those conditions the levy falls on the landholder. A landlord’s desire to recover a new expense is not an additional source of demand.

Ricardo, chapter X supplies the incidence mechanism. The hundred and twenty are hypothetical, and the comparison assumes competitive allocation, a fixed available stock and a charge that does not vary with the owner’s production or improvements.

The change also reaches backward into the price of the title. At a constant five-percent discount rate, a certain perpetual rent of one hundred is worth two thousand. After the annual charge of twenty, it is worth sixteen hundred. The owner at the announcement loses four hundred of asset value; a later buyer pays the lower price and receives eighty, still a five-percent yield. This is why the tax can be efficient at the margin and painful in transition. The new buyer’s normal return does not show that the old owner escaped a loss. Nor does the old owner’s loss show that the supply of land has shrunk.

Schwerhoff, Edenhofer and Fleurbaey, IMF WP/22/263 (2022), §§2.1–2.2, pp. 4–5 explain capitalization. Here the tax is a fixed annual amount: (100 − 20) ÷ .05 = 1,600. The example assumes certainty, perpetual income, no other tax and an unchanged discount rate.

An annual percentage of the site’s market value is a different expression of the same problem. The value being taxed already reflects the tax. With rent of one hundred, a five-percent discount rate and an annual value tax of one percent, the price is one hundred divided by six percent, about sixteen hundred and sixty-seven. The tax then raises about sixteen and two-thirds each year. Charging one percent of the old, untaxed valuation and calling it one percent of the new value would mix two bases. This small piece of arithmetic matters because an announcement about a rate tells us too little until we know which value it is applied to.

Schwerhoff and coauthors, §2.1, p. 4, equations (1)–(3) derive V = R/(r + τ) for constant annual rent and an annual tax on the resulting market value. At the stipulated rates, .01 × (100 ÷ .06) = 16⅔. This differs from a fixed twenty-dollar charge.

The incentive test is equally concrete. Suppose an improvement would add thirty a year and costs twenty-five a year to provide and maintain. Without the improvement, the site yields one hundred; with it, the net total is one hundred and five. A fixed site charge of twenty leaves eighty or eighty-five, so the additional act still earns five. A charge of ten that arises only when the improvement is made instead turns its five-dollar gain into a five-dollar loss. The first tax changes the owner’s income; the second changes this production decision. A pure site tax also need not force an already rational owner to develop sooner: if the same charge attaches to every alternative, it cancels from that comparison too.

Ricardo, chapter X distinguishes the tax bases. These annualized figures hold prices, finance and the site assessment fixed. They illustrate why neutrality alone does not prove an additional acceleration of development; finance constraints or a change in another tax would require a further account.

George proposed to make this distinction the center of the fiscal system. He would leave titles, sale and inheritance in place while taking the rental value for public use and removing other taxes. The moral argument was that the community should receive the value of opportunities no individual had produced. The economic argument was that it could do so without confiscating improvements. His claim that this would suffice for public expenditure is a further claim about the size of the base, not a consequence of the fixed-supply theorem. A levy can be a good part of a revenue system without being large enough to replace the whole of it.

George, Progress and Poverty, VIII.2 sets out the proposed substitution of land-value taxation for other taxes; VIII.3, sections I–IV gives the production, collection and equality arguments. These are George’s proposals, not contemporary estimates of the revenue available.

Assessment is where the conceptual separation becomes work. A property commonly sells with its buildings, access rights, restrictions and expectations attached. The value of the site without the owner’s improvements is a counterfactual, even when comparable vacant sales and other evidence make it estimable. An assessment that attributes a new building’s return to the bare land can reintroduce the very penalty the design meant to avoid. An assessment that ignores a change in permitted use can miss a large advantage. The site cannot move abroad, but its assessed value can still be contested. Immovable is not the same as self-valuing.

Schwerhoff and coauthors, §8, pp. 22–23 identify valuation as a practical objection and treat it as addressable rather than nonexistent. Ricardo’s distinction explains why the separation from improvements matters to incentives, not merely to the presentation of the bill.

The distribution also depends on the rest of the reform. An owner of valuable land with little current cash faces a different problem from an investor with a diversified portfolio. A levy may replace another tax, finance a service or fund a payment; gross liabilities alone do not establish who gains overall. Deferral or a phased transition can change when the bill is paid, but a credible future charge can still lower an asset’s value now. These are reasons to design the change carefully, not reasons to confuse an existing rent with a necessary incentive to create the land. Efficiency, ability to pay and reliance are distinct questions even when one policy must answer all three.

Schwerhoff and coauthors, §1, pp. 2–3, §7.5, pp. 21–22, and §8, pp. 22–23 distinguish supply efficiency from distribution and revenue recycling. Mill, V.II §§5–6 separately considers expectations already purchased and charges attached to a title before purchase.

The politics becomes easier to understand once the capital loss is visible. A new annual bill arrives at an identifiable address; the advantage from lower taxes elsewhere may be dispersed among people who do not yet know its amount. Owners need not imagine a fall in the price of their land to object to one. Nor is every owner wealthy in the same way. The original question points to an important conflict over who holds a scarce base, but it would be too easy to attribute every obstacle to one class defending one interest. Valuation, liquidity, the treatment of buildings and the credibility of the promised return of revenue also determine what people think they are being asked to accept.

Schwerhoff and coauthors, §7, pp. 20–22 discuss financial, political, transition and hardship obstacles. The passage develops those mechanisms without claiming that one explanation accounts for the tax choices of every jurisdiction.

The case for taxing rent is consequently more durable than the promise of a painless tax. It rests on finding a receipt that can be reduced without making a socially useful additional act less worthwhile. Land offers an unusually clear candidate, provided the base excludes produced improvements and the surrounding assumptions are respected. That is a strong reason to use the instrument. It leaves open how much to raise, what to replace, and how to treat the people whose balance sheets were built under the former rules.

A tax can leave the productive margin intact and still change the value of a life’s savings.

The state already helps define the title, secure the possession and provide much of the setting in which the site becomes valuable. Asking it to recover some of that value is not an excursion beyond the economy; it is a change within the arrangement the economy already uses. The serious answer to “why not?” must address the strongest version of that change. Equally, the serious proposal must describe its losses as faithfully as its efficiencies. A rent can be available for taxation without its present recipient being indifferent to losing it. The argument remains open.
