The Boundary

in print · The Firm · plate ii

If the firm exists to spare the costs of the market, why does it not grow until it has swallowed the market whole? Because command, too, has its costs, and they rise with size. This plate traces the firm's edge — the make-or-buy line — and the force that draws activities across it: the asset built for one partner and worthless to any other, and the holdup it invites. Where the market grows too dangerous, the firm takes over; where the office grows too costly, it stops.

The economies of production are of two kinds: those that depend on the general development of the industry, and those that depend on the resources of the individual house of business. Alfred Marshall, Principles of Economics (1890)

If the firm exists because directing resources by command can be cheaper than buying them in the market, an awkward question follows at once. Why not, then, direct everything by command? Why not gather the whole economy into a single great firm and dispense with the market altogether — the very proposal that, under the name of central planning, the twentieth century tried and abandoned at terrible cost? The answer must be that command has costs of its own, costs that grow with the size of the thing commanded; and the firm's true size is fixed not by how much the market wastes but by where the waste of the market and the waste of the office come, at the last bale and the last memorandum, to exactly balance.

The shape of the answer was already in Coase, and it is the shape of every answer in this work's sixth volume: a margin. A firm should bring an activity in-house, Coase reasoned, just so long as doing so costs less than buying the same activity in the market; and it should stop — leave the next activity to be bought — at the point where the two costs meet. The boundary of the firm is that meeting point, the make-or-buy line traced out transaction by transaction. It is an elegant principle and, for a long time, a frustrating one, because it named the criterion without supplying it. To say that a firm integrates whatever is cheaper to organize within tells us nothing until we can say what makes organizing within cheaper — and lacking that, the theory could only point at any boundary already drawn and pronounce it efficient, having no way to predict where the line would fall before it fell. The puzzle of the boundary was not why it existed but what governed it; and that took another economist, and another generation.

Coase had given the rule in 1937: the firm grows until the cost of arranging one more transaction inside it equals the cost of buying that transaction in the market, or having another firm arrange it. As a principle it is exactly right, and as a guide nearly empty — for it does not say which transactions are cheaper to organize within and which without, and so can be made, after the fact, to fit any boundary one happens to observe. Whatever lies inside, one says, must have been cheaper inside. The rule explains everything, and therefore predicts nothing.

The economist was Oliver Williamson, and the answer he gave turned on a single, fertile idea: that what pulls an activity inside the firm is not its cost or its complexity but a particular kind of vulnerability. Most things a firm needs it can buy from anyone — bolts, electricity, haulage, the standard services of a competitive market, where if one supplier disappoints, another stands ready. But some things require an investment shaped to one partner and almost worthless away from him: a factory built beside a single buyer's works and tooled to his specifications; a component designed for one model and no other; the deep, particular knowledge a worker accumulates of one firm's machines and ways. Williamson called the property asset specificity, and saw that it changes everything about the safety of dealing in the market. So long as what you buy is generic, the market protects you, for you can always turn elsewhere. The moment what you need is specific — built for this relationship and stranded outside it — the protection falls away, and a hazard opens that the market cannot close.

Williamson's term for the crucial property was asset specificity. A general-purpose asset — a delivery van, an all-purpose lathe — can be turned to many uses and sold to many buyers, and its owner is beholden to none. A specific asset is worth much in one relationship and little outside it: a press tooled to stamp one carmaker's panels, a pipeline laid between two particular plants, a lifetime's skill in one firm's idiosyncratic ways. The more specific the asset, the more its value is hostage to a single partner — and the more dangerous the open market becomes as a place to keep it.

The hazard is the holdup, and it is worth seeing exactly. Imagine a parts-maker who, to win a carmaker's business, must build a plant useful only for that carmaker's panels. Before he builds, he is one bidder among many, and the carmaker must court him; the instant he builds, the courtship ends, for his plant is now worth a fortune to this one buyer and scrap to all others. He is, in Williamson's phrase, fundamentally transformed — from a party with options into a hostage — and the carmaker, knowing it, may press for a lower price, daring him to refuse and eat his sunk cost. The parts-maker foresees all this, and so may decline to build the efficient plant at all, or build it only behind a thicket of costly contractual armor. The clean solution is to dissolve the two parties' separateness: let the carmaker own the plant. Under one roof there is no one to be held up and no one to do the holding, and the quarrel the market would have settled by extortion is settled instead by an order from above. Where assets are specific, the firm swallows the market — and that is what vertical integration is.

Williamson called the trap the fundamental transformation. Before the specific asset is built, many suppliers compete for the work, and the buyer has the whip hand; once it is built and the money sunk, the two are wed, and either may squeeze the other — the supplier threatening to withhold, the buyer to walk — each gambling the other has more to lose. The mere prospect of such a squeeze deters the investment, or loads it with costly safeguards. Integration is the escape: bring both assets under one owner, and the holdup vanishes, for a firm does not hold itself up; its disputes are settled by command.

This explains why firms reach inward, but not why they ever stop — and the stopping is the boundary's other half, and the answer to the question this plate began with. If integration cures the holdup, why does the carmaker not also make its own steel, grow its own rubber, drill its own oil, until one firm has absorbed the world? Because authority has rising costs of its own, and they catch up. The manager who would direct everything cannot know everything; his bounded attention, stretched too far, governs each part worse than a focused rival would. Incentives within the firm are duller than the market's — the salaried division has less reason to strain than the independent supplier whose survival rides on each sale. And the great organization breeds its own waste, the empire-building and budget-fighting that consume effort better spent on the work. Marshall had marked the two faces of this long before, the economies a firm wins from its own organization set against those it draws from the industry around it; the boundary is the seam between them, and falls where the office's mounting costs overtake the market's.

Why, then, not integrate everything into one firm? Because authority, too, decays with scale. The manager's mind, bounded like any other, cannot hold a whole economy; incentives blunt as the worker's reward parts from his effort — the high-powered spur of the market giving way to the low-powered one of the salary; and the firm fills with the politicking and budget-fighting large organizations breed. Williamson put the deepest version sharply: a great firm cannot simply mimic the market and intervene only when it helps, because the promise not to meddle is never believed. So the office, too, wastes, and the more so the larger it grows.

Two things follow, and the first is that the boundary is not a fixed feature of an industry but a standing decision, taken and retaken. Make or buy is a question a firm answers afresh for every component and every service, and its answer changes as the conditions change. When the costs of transacting are high — communication slow, suppliers few and far, contracts hard to enforce — the safe course is to make, and firms grow great and self-contained, doing everything within. When those costs fall — when networks are cheap, supplier markets deep and competitive, contracts easily written and policed — the safe course shifts toward buy, and firms slim to what they do best and purchase the rest. The long movement toward outsourcing in our own time, the breaking of the vast integrated firm into a core ringed by contractors and suppliers, is not some change in the nature of business but the boundary sliding outward as the cost of using the market has fallen. The line is alive, and it moves.

Because it is a balance of costs, the boundary moves whenever the costs move. When communication is dear and outside suppliers few, much must be made within, and firms grow long and integrated; when communication is cheap and supplier markets thick, much can be safely bought, and firms shed all but their core, contracting the rest to the market. The wave of outsourcing late in the twentieth century was not a change in human nature but a fall in the cost of transacting — the boundary sliding outward, the same calculation giving a new answer.

Honesty requires the theory's rivals and soft spots be set down too. Williamson's account, for all its reach, is not the last word. A powerful alternative, the property-rights theory of Grossman, Hart, and Moore, accepts that incomplete contracts are the root of the matter but denies that integration is a costless cure: to absorb a partner is to strip him of the residual right to decide, weakening his incentive to invest even as it strengthens one's own. Ownership, on this view, is two-edged, to be granted to whichever party's effort matters more, and the boundary balances incentives on both sides rather than merely fleeing the holdup — a refinement the plate on the asset takes up. Others, recalling the previous plate's quarrel, doubt there is any sharp line between firm and market at all, seeing instead a continuous spectrum, from the one-off purchase through the long contract and the franchise to full ownership, with no natural break. And the very episodes most often told to illustrate the holdup — the carmaker and its body-supplier above all — have been picked apart by historians until it is no longer clear they happened as the parable says. The mechanism is real; its dominion is contested.

Williamson's is not the only theory of the line, nor an unchallenged one. The property-rights account of Grossman, Hart, and Moore objects that integration is no free remedy: to bring a partner's assets under one's own roof is to take the residual say from him, dulling his incentive to invest even as it sharpens one's own — so ownership should fall to whoever's effort matters most, and the boundary balances two-sided, not one. Others doubt the boundary is sharp at all. And the very cases most cited for the holdup turn out, on closer history, to be far murkier than the parable allows.

Set these disputes aside and the boundary still teaches the lesson this work has pursued from the start. A price, we have held, is a verdict on a hidden contest, mistaken for a fact of nature; and the firm's boundary is the line that decides where such verdicts are handed down by the market and where, instead, they are reserved to the quiet authority within. To buy a thing is to submit it to the market's pricing; to make it is to withdraw it from that pricing and settle its fate by command. Which of the two shall govern any given exchange is not dictated by the nature of the thing but chosen, on a reckoning of costs and hazards — of who could hold up whom, of where the open deal is too dangerous to risk. The boundary of the firm is thus the frontier of the priced world, the limit of the market's writ; and like every frontier it was drawn by someone, in the face of some contest, and could have been drawn elsewhere.

Here the boundary bends the table's thesis a further turn. A price is a verdict on a hidden contest, dressed as a fact of nature; the boundary of the firm decides where such verdicts are permitted to be rendered at all — which exchanges are thrown open to the market's pricing, and which are withdrawn behind the firm's wall to be settled by command instead. And that decision is itself a contest: over who might hold up whom, over where authority is safer than the open deal. To see a thing priced in the market is already to know that someone judged the market the safer house for it.

And this should change how we read an industry's very shape. We are inclined to take the arrangement of an industry — which firms, of which sizes, making what and buying what — as a kind of natural fact, the form the business was bound to assume. It is nothing of the kind. Every boundary in it is the residue of a choice that might have gone otherwise: the carmaker that buys its bodies might have built them, the studio that owns its lots might have rented them, the grocer that runs its own delivery might have hired it out. What looks like the anatomy of an industry is the frozen accumulation of countless make-or-buy decisions, each one a small judgment about how far the price system should be let in. The price of the finished thing — the car, the film, the delivered parcel — carries the whole of that hidden architecture within it: not the verdict of a single open market but the sum of a hundred prior verdicts about where markets should be allowed to reach, and where command should rule instead.

We look at an industry — these firms, these sizes, this made in-house and that bought outside — and read its shape as given, the way the business simply is. But every line in it was drawn by a choice that could have gone the other way: the studio might employ its actors or hire them by the picture; the carmaker might forge its own steel or buy it. The structure is not nature's but the frozen sum of ten thousand make-or-buy decisions, each a small verdict on how far the price system's writ should run.

To buy a thing is to trust it to the market; to make it is to withdraw it from the market and rule it by command — and which shall govern is chosen, never given.

So the two opening plates of this volume close a single arc. The first asked why the islands of planning exist at all, and found the answer in the cost of using the market; this one asked how far each island spreads, and found the answer in the cost of leaving it — in the asset too specific to trust to a stranger, and the office too large to govern well. Between them they dissolve the comfortable picture of an economy that is simply, naturally, a market. A price is a verdict on a hidden contest, mistaken for a fact of nature — and the firm shows that even whether a thing is to be priced at all, or made within the silent precincts of an organization, is itself such a verdict, rendered at the boundary, revisable, and never written in nature. The line between the market and the firm is the line between the priced and the planned; it runs through every industry; and it was drawn, as all such lines are drawn, by choice contending with cost. The argument remains open.
