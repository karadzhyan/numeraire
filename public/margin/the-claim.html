The Claim

in print · The Stock · plate ii

A modest fortune is plainly the stored reward of work — wages saved, thrift made visible. A great one is something else: too large to have been earned, sustained by return and inheritance, and yielding an income drawn from what others produce. This plate asks where the one becomes the other — where the reward of labor turns into a standing claim upon it, and why the language of earning hides the turn.

The rich ruleth over the poor, and the borrower is servant to the lender. Proverbs 22:7

Past a certain magnitude, a fortune ceases to be the stored reward of work and becomes a standing claim. The savings a person lays by from a lifetime's earning are a stock built visibly out of a flow — wages not spent, accumulated against age and accident, plainly the fruit of the labor that earned them. But a great fortune is no longer chiefly the residue of its owner's exertions. It is a claim: upon the income that the rest of society produces, drawn as rent and interest and profit, maintained without effort, enlarged by the mere passage of time, and handed on at death to heirs who did nothing to make it. The question this plate asks is where, and how, the one becomes the other — at what point a sum of money stops being the wage of work stored up, and starts being a permanent claim upon the labor of others.

The two ends of the spectrum are clear enough; it is the passage between them that wants examining. At one end stands the modest competence — the savings of a working life, a house owned outright, a pension, a little put by — and here the stock is transparently a flow stored up, wages earned and not spent, set against the years when earning stops. No one looks at such a fortune and sees anything but the visible fruit of work and thrift; it is a wage that has been saved, and its character as earned is written plainly across it. At the other end stands the great fortune, the dynastic estate, the nine- or ten-figure holding that no wage however large could have accumulated and no thrift however severe could have saved — a sum so far beyond what a lifetime of labor could lay by that the idea of its being saved earnings collapses under inspection. Between these poles the character of wealth changes, not at a sharp line but along a gradient, from the stored reward of one's own work toward something that is no longer that at all; and the plate's work is to say what the something is.

There is no precise dollar figure at which a fortune changes its nature, and the plate claims none; the transition is one of character, not a fixed threshold. What changes along the gradient is the source and upkeep of the wealth — whether it is sustained by the owner's labor and saving, or by returns and inheritance that owe nothing to either. A surgeon's savings and a dynastic billion differ not in size alone but in kind, even where no exact boundary can be drawn.

What marks the great fortune off is that it has become a source of income in its own right, independent of anything its owner does. The wage stops when the work stops; but the rent of land, the interest on a bond, the dividend on a share, the profit of an enterprise one need not manage, arrive whether the owner labors or sleeps, for it is the property that earns and not the person. This is the oldest distinction in the moral accounting of wealth — between the income of labor and the income of property, the wage and the return — and the classical economists drew it sharply. Adam Smith remarked that the landlord loves to reap where he never sowed, demanding a rent even for the natural produce of the earth; Ricardo built his system on rent as the payment for the original and indestructible powers of the soil, a return to mere ownership of a scarce thing; and John Stuart Mill, pressing the thought further, observed that landlords grow richer in their sleep, the unearned increment of their holdings swelling with the growth of a society to which they contribute nothing. The income of the great fortune is income of this kind: not the reward of effort but the yield of ownership.

The distinction between earned and unearned income runs from the Physiocrats through the classical school into the tax codes of the present. Smith treats the landlord's rent as a deduction from the produce of labor; Ricardo makes rent the return to the advantage of superior land; Mill's "unearned increment" grounded George's proposals to tax away what ownership reaps from social progress. The Unearned, in the volume on rent, treats the lineage at length.

Call this income by its right name, and the nature of the great fortune comes clear: it is a claim. The flows it yields — rent, interest, profit, dividend — are not conjured from the asset by private magic but drawn from the stream of what society produces, directed to the owner by the fact of his ownership. When the bondholder draws his interest, it comes from the earnings of the enterprise that borrowed; the landlord's rent, from the product of those who use the land; the shareholder's dividend, from what the firm's workers and customers between them generate. A great fortune is thus a standing, legally enforced claim upon a share of the social product — a right, protected by the whole apparatus of property and contract, to receive a slice of what others produce, in perpetuity, without obligation to produce anything in return. The fortune is not a hoard of things sitting inertly in a vault; it is an entitlement, continuously honored, to a cut of the common stream.

That the claim is a social and legal relation, not a fact of nature, bears emphasis: an asset yields an income only because a system of property rights, enforced by law, directs a share of production to its owner rather than another. Change the rules — abolish a form of property, alter its terms, tax its yield — and the claim changes too. The income of wealth is no more a brute datum than the wealth itself; both are creatures of an institutional order that elsewhere has been arranged otherwise.

And the claim, unlike the labor it is so often confused with, does not die with the worker or even tire. A wage is bounded by the life and strength of the one who earns it; a claim is bounded by nothing of the kind. It passes, at its holder's death, to heirs who did nothing to create it and need do nothing to keep it, so that a fortune assembled in one generation becomes the unearned birthright of the next — the patrimonial pattern in which one's place in the order of wealth is fixed less by what one does than by what one stands to inherit. Worse, the claim tends to grow against those who must earn: where the return on accumulated wealth runs ahead of the growth of the economy as a whole — the condition Thomas Piketty has argued obtains in most times and places, and which he wrote as r greater than g — the claims of the past gain steadily on the incomes of the present, old fortunes outpacing new effort, wealth concentrating into ever fewer and more durable hands. The claim is not only unearned in its continuance; it is, across generations, self-enlarging, the rich growing richer by the arithmetic of compounding alone.

Thomas Piketty's Capital in the Twenty-First Century (2013) argued that when the return on capital, r, exceeds the growth rate of the economy, g, accumulated and inherited wealth grows faster than earned income, so that the past dominates the present and wealth concentrates. The forecasts are contested; but the mechanism — wealth compounding above the growth rate outpaces the wages it is measured against — is arithmetic, and its result is the matter of The Patrimony and The Concentration.

Yet the income of all this — the rent and interest and dividend that the claim yields — does not present itself as a claim at all, but as a reward. The return to capital wears, in the ordinary telling, the look of the most natural thing in the world: the yield saved-up resources earn by their productivity, the just recompense of thrift and foresight and risk, as plainly deserved as the wage labor draws. A price is a verdict on a hidden contest, mistaken for a fact of nature; and the rate of return on wealth — the price of capital, the toll that ownership commands — is a verdict of exactly this kind, the outcome of the long struggle over how the social product shall be divided between those who own and those who work. What that struggle settles, the language of reward then disguises: the owner's share, won in a contest of institutions and bargaining power and law, is renamed his earnings, his return, the fruit of his capital, as though nature and not the contest had fixed it. To read the income of a great fortune as the reward of a service is to mistake the spoils of the distributional contest for a wage paid by the order of things — and to settle, by a choice of words, the very question the contest leaves open.

The rate of return is not fixed by technology alone. It reflects the relative power of owners and workers to claim the surplus that production leaves over its costs — power that turns on law, on the strength of labor, on the abundance of capital, on the taxing of returns. The same machines yield their owner more where workers are weak and less where strong; the figure is an outcome of the contest, not a datum prior to it, and The Divide, in the volume on surplus, follows the struggle over the residual it records.

The case for reading that income as earned is old, serious, and not to be waved aside; it comes in three strands, each with real force. The first is that property itself is earned: in the account John Locke made canonical, a man who mixes his labor with the world makes what he touches his own, and a fortune that begins in genuine enterprise — a business built to serve millions, risks borne that might have ruined him, wants met that would otherwise go unmet — is the rightful fruit of a real contribution, however large it grows. The second is that the income of capital is the wage of a genuine sacrifice: to save is to abstain, to forgo consumption now for later, and the abstinence Nassau Senior named the third agent of production is a real cost, for which the return is a real reward. The third is that capital is productive: a stock of tools makes labor yield more than it could bare-handed, and the owner's return is no deduction from what others make but his share of the increase his capital made possible — the marginal product of capital, earned exactly as the wage is. On this view the great fortune's income is no claim on others but a reward for a contribution: for having created, for having saved, for having made the work of others more fruitful.

The strongest form of the case rests on the marginal-productivity theory of distribution, worked out by John Bates Clark and others, on which each factor — labor and capital alike — tends to be paid the value of what it adds at the margin, so that capital's return is earned just as the wage is. The abstinence theory of Senior and the time-preference theory of Böhm-Bawerk and Fisher add that it also compensates a real reluctance to wait. Together they form the orthodox justification the plate grants before asking what survives it at the summit of fortune.

The reply grants each strand its truth and then asks how much of the great fortune it actually covers. Grant that property may begin in creation, that abstinence is a real sacrifice, that capital is productive: it remains that the largest fortunes are not, and could not be, the stored wages of such services. They are too large to have been saved from any earnings, however high, and are built and sustained in the main by capital gains on assets already held and by inheritance from those who held them before — routes that pass through neither labor nor abstinence, and reward neither. The billion is not deferred consumption; no one abstained his way to it. And even where the origin was genuine creation, the income does not stop when the creating does: it continues, undiminished, long after the founder's work is done, and passes to heirs who created nothing and abstained from nothing, the reward — if reward it was — collected many times over while the claim rolls on. What is left, when the earned part has been honestly credited, is a residue the language of reward cannot reach: an income that continues without effort, scaled beyond any service, perpetuated by inheritance, and sized by a rate the contest over distribution sets. That residue is the claim, and its justice is precisely what is in dispute.

The point turns on fact: how the greatest fortunes are actually built. Studies of top wealth find that at the summit, accumulation owes far more to the appreciation of assets already owned and to inheritance than to saving from earned income — so the abstinence story, plausible for the modest saver, fails to describe the holdings it is most invoked to justify. The Saving takes up the evidence, and the gap between the thrift supposed to build great fortunes and the gains and bequests that in fact do.

So the question the plate opened with — where the reward of work becomes a claim on the work of others — has no answer in a number, but it has an answer in a discipline. The line is not sharp, but the difference is real, and it is the word earning that blurs it. A price is a verdict on a hidden contest, mistaken for a fact of nature; and the income of a great fortune is such a verdict, the spoils of the contest over distribution dressed in the vocabulary of desert. To call the rentier's receipts his earnings, the heir's inheritance the reward of anything, the magnate's billion the return to a service, is to decide by vocabulary the very question — reward, or claim? — that the contest holds open, and to decide it every time in the owner's favor. The discipline is to refuse that easy settlement: to credit the reward wherever work or saving or genuine creation has truly earned it, and to name the claim wherever the income has outrun its earning into mere position — and above all to keep the question open where the language of earning would close it, in advance and on the side of those who already own.

The vocabulary is not neutral, and its choice carries the argument. To speak of "wealth creators," "job creators," and the "return" to capital is to assert, before any evidence, that the income of wealth rewards a contribution; to speak of "rentiers" and "unearned income" is to assert the reverse. The naming settles in advance what the contest leaves open, which is why so much of the politics of wealth is a politics of words — and why holding reward and claim apart is a political act as much as an analytical one.

A small fortune is work, stored. A great one is a claim on the work of others — and "earning" is the name that hides the difference.

With the claim named, the volume's remaining plates can do their work upon it. They will reckon the claim on the ledger and watch it swell by the windfall of revaluation; trace it down the generations as patrimony and weigh the levy by which a society might reach it; follow it as it concentrates into the fewest hands and measure the buffer of security and freedom it affords; and ask, at the last, what a commonwealth of nominal equals is to make of the great estate in its midst — a reward to be honored, or a position to be checked. The reward and the claim are not always easy to tell apart, and an honest accounting will find both braided together in most real fortunes. But that they are different in kind, and that the difference — not the size, and not the number — is the thing the contest is fought over, is what the rest of this volume keeps steadily in view. The argument remains open.
