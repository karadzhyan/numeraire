The Unpriced

in print · The Surplus · plate x

A free entrance leaves no admission receipt, yet reaching it can be costly and losing it can matter. Travel choices, property prices, and carefully specified surveys offer different kinds of evidence about benefits without market prices. Each method has a boundary; making those boundaries visible allows a decision to preserve a missing value as an open question rather than quietly erase it.

A missing price leaves a value to investigate.The argument of this plate, after the NOAA panel and EPA’s valuation framework

The park has no ticket office. A person who walks through it pays nothing for the shade, the view, or the hour that becomes possible there. The municipal account records the gardener’s wage and the cost of the trees. It has no corresponding entry for the afternoon. If the park is sold, the purchase price appears; if it remains open, its most ordinary service may pass through no cash account at all. The difference between those records can give a decision a misleading direction. The priced alternative arrives with a number, while the unpriced one arrives with a description. A calculation that admits only the first has already made a judgment about the second.

The surplus account can reach beyond the sale. Its question is how much a change is worth to someone, relative to what that person must give up, and an actual payment is one source of evidence about that comparison. The evidence may also lie in a journey, a choice of home, or an answer to a carefully specified question. The central difficulty is to establish the change and the comparison. Cleaner water can mean safer drinking, better fishing, a different view, or the continued existence of a habitat. These are distinct benefits, enjoyed by people who need not buy the same goods or visit the same place. Naming the water does not yet identify the service being valued.

U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, Guidelines for Preparing Economic Analyses, third edition (2024), §7.2, pp. 7-12–7-15. The economic measure compares changes relative to a baseline; willingness to pay and willingness to accept can differ with the entitlement, income effects, and available substitutes.

A free entrance can still be costly to reach. Suppose, in a deliberately simple example, that the marginal value of visits over a season is twenty minus the number of visits, measured in dollars, and that each journey costs four dollars in time and travel. Treat visits as divisible, hold income effects and the other available destinations out of the example, and let the visitor choose freely. Sixteen visits will be made. Their gross value is one hundred and ninety-two dollars; their travel cost is sixty-four; the gain left to the visitor is one hundred and twenty-eight. The entrance receipts are zero. The value of this particular access, on these assumptions, is positive. The missing ticket price has concealed neither the cost of getting there nor the reason to go.

The journey provides evidence only if its purpose is understood. A traveler may visit the park while staying with family, choose among several nearby parks, or take a scenic route whose pleasure is part of the trip itself. Charging every mile to this one destination would give it credit for something else. Time must be valued, alternatives must be described, and the people who do not make the journey must be considered when estimating demand. Travel spending alone is especially easy to misuse: the sixty-four dollars in the example is a cost incurred to obtain the visits, whereas the surplus is the value remaining after that cost. Observing the expenditure does not reveal the entire demand curve. It supplies evidence from which a model, with additional variation and assumptions, may recover it.

EPA, 2024 Guidelines, §7.3.1.2, pp. 7-31–7-36, including notes 32–46. The travel-cost discussion treats the value of time, the choice set, multiple destinations, and multipurpose trips as substantive parts of the valuation problem. The numerical demand schedule here is illustrative.

A house can carry another trace. A buyer may pay more for an otherwise comparable home beside a quiet square, and the price difference may help identify the value of quiet. But homes come in bundles. Schools, transport, crime, space, and neighbors also differ; the household chooses among them together. A premium associated with a cleaner neighborhood can therefore include advantages that the analyst has not measured. Even a well-identified price response must be interpreted with care. The marginal value implicit in a stable housing market need not describe the welfare consequences of a large change that brings different residents, incomes, and choices into the area. The price is informative because people make sacrifices to obtain a location. What those sacrifices measure remains an empirical question.

EPA, 2024 Guidelines, §7.3.1.3, pp. 7-36–7-40, including notes 47–49. The section distinguishes identification of environmental attributes, marginal willingness to pay, and capitalization estimates when households re-sort or preferences change.

Some values leave neither trace. A person can care that a species survives without expecting to see it, or that a distant landscape remains intact without intending to travel there. The NOAA panel on contingent valuation made this absence of a behavioral trail central to its inquiry. A method built from visits cannot, by itself, recover the values of people whose concern never takes the form of a visit. Excluding those people would turn a limitation of the available evidence into a boundary of the thing being measured. Including them requires a different kind of evidence, and a correspondingly different scrutiny of how it was obtained.

Arrow, Solow, Portney, Leamer, Radner, and Schuman, Report of the NOAA Panel on Contingent Valuation, §I, pp. 1–4, distinguishes active uses from existence and other passive-use values. The report considers contingent valuation because ordinary behavioral evidence may not reveal the latter; the distinction does not itself validate a particular survey.

A survey can put a proposed change before the person directly. The useful question has a baseline, an outcome, and a sacrifice: what will happen without the project, what the project would change, and what the respondent would have to pay. An answer about restoring one wetland has little meaning if the respondent imagines restoring every wetland. An answer about a permanent annual payment differs from one about a single contribution. The available substitutes matter, as does the money left for other purposes. A carefully framed choice tries to make these conditions part of the decision the respondent actually considers. It asks for a trade, with its cost attached.

That construction remains vulnerable. A person may express support without expecting payment, misunderstand the environmental change, respond to a suggested starting figure, or answer as an advocate for the cause. The people who respond may differ from those who decline. These possibilities require checks on comprehension, the payment arrangement, sampling, and the sensitivity of the answer to the scope of the benefit. Passing one check cannot certify the whole estimate. Nor does a discrepancy automatically make every response useless: it may reveal a faulty description, a different object of value, or a model imposed on answers that do not support it. The work is to identify which comparison the evidence can sustain.

EPA, 2024 Guidelines, §7.3.2, pp. 7-45–7-54, including notes 56–61. The full section covers commodity and payment definition, strategic responses, framing, validity, hypothetical bias, and non-response. It expressly treats scope tests as neither necessary nor sufficient by themselves to establish validity.

The resulting number also inherits the means of the person who supplied it. A willingness to pay is constrained by income and alternatives. Someone can care deeply about access and still offer less than a wealthier person with a milder preference. The earlier question in The Willing therefore returns even when the benefit has been recovered with care. Extending the monetary account can correct an omission without settling how claims should be weighted. Distribution, entitlements, and protections may still require decisions that the sum of willingness to pay does not make on its own.

When a credible monetary estimate cannot be made, the missing benefit should remain visible. Consider a project costing one hundred, with established benefits of seventy and an additional environmental benefit whose monetary value is uncertain. The threshold is thirty. If that additional benefit is twenty, the stipulated account yields a net loss of ten; if it is forty, it yields a gain of ten. A threshold makes the unresolved issue concrete. It does not supply the missing value. A qualitative account can describe the affected people, the ecological change, and the reasons its importance might exceed or fall below that threshold. The decision then exposes what it depends on, instead of concealing the dependence in an unexplained zero.

EPA, 2024 Guidelines, §7.5, pp. 7-61–7-63, discusses qualitative treatment, cost effectiveness, bounding, and break-even analysis for non-monetized benefits. A cost-effectiveness ranking alone does not establish that the selected project’s benefits exceed its costs. The amounts here are hypothetical.

An absent price leaves the value to be established.

The unpriced enlarges the discipline of the account. It asks who has been left outside, what kind of benefit has been missed, and what evidence could bring it into view. It also makes the limits of addition harder to ignore. A sum can be carefully estimated and still leave the distribution of claims unresolved. The next inquiry, The Source, moves behind the exchange itself, to the work and materials that make anything available to value. The surplus has more than one boundary, and each boundary changes what the account can tell us. The argument remains open.
