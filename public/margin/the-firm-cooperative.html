The Cooperative

in print · The Firm · plate ix

Workers, customers and suppliers can hold the rights usually associated with investors. Comparing the costs of contracting with the costs of ownership shows what each arrangement may save and what it asks its members to carry. Finance, collective decisions and risk then return the inquiry to the difference between a constituency’s gains and the wider value of its authority.

The need for capital does not write the firm’s constitution.The argument of this plate, after Hansmann

The factory joins the money that bought its machines to the people who make them useful. Neither contribution can produce the finished article alone. Yet the ordinary picture of the firm assigns the two contributions different places: capital appoints the people who govern, while labor enters on terms the governed must accept. The cooperative interrupts that picture. Workers can hold the ownership rights; customers can hold them; suppliers can hold them together. Each arrangement still has to obtain capital, coordinate work, and answer for its losses. What changes is the constituency whose claims the governing institution is organized to serve.

Henry Hansmann’s starting point is to separate supplying capital from holding ownership. He calls those who transact with a firm its patrons: the people who buy its products or supply its inputs, including money. Ownership, in his account, generally joins the formal right to control the firm with the right to its residual earnings. Investors are one possible class of patrons to receive these rights. Workers and customers are others. This way of posing the question puts the conventional corporation within the comparison rather than outside it as the natural form from which alternatives depart. Capital must be provided; it does not follow from that necessity alone that those who provide it must possess every residual decision.

Henry Hansmann, “Ownership of the Firm,” Journal of Law, Economics, and Organization 4(2) (1988), §§2.1–2.2, pp. 269–273. His account concerns formal control and residual earnings, and distinguishes them from effective day-to-day authority. It also recognizes organizations, notably nonprofits, in which the two rights are not joined in an owning class.

The comparison becomes concrete in a cooperative dairy. Farmers can own the processor to which they sell their milk, receive an initial payment for deliveries, and share the remaining earnings under the cooperative’s rules. A lender can finance equipment without thereby becoming a farmer-member. The cooperative may also buy milk from suppliers who are not members and receive no ownership share. The boundary of membership matters as much as the name of the form. A firm owned by workers may employ people who are not owners; a consumer cooperative may serve nonmember customers. Nor must every cooperative assign votes or returns in the same proportions. The constitution has to say who belongs, what belonging entitles them to decide, and how their dealings with the firm affect their claims.

Hansmann proposes comparing the costs of ownership with the costs of contracting for those left outside ownership. The class that suffers most from an arm’s-length relationship may gain by taking control, but its ability to govern and bear residual risk also matters. Customers facing a seller whose quality is difficult to inspect have one problem; workers whose skills have become specific to a workplace have another; investors unable to observe what is being done with their funds have a third. Ownership can change the incentives behind those problems. It also creates a continuing obligation to monitor managers, reconcile interests, and carry results that no contract has fixed in advance. The comparison has to include both sides of the change.

Hansmann, §§2.2–2.5, pp. 272–281, compares market-contracting costs with monitoring, collective-decision, and risk-bearing costs. He expressly presents the framework as a way to ask questions, not a precise calculus that identifies the best owner without further evidence. His term “market contracting” includes arrangements more elaborate than a spot transaction.

A small account helps keep the comparison honest. Suppose an enterprise generates a common value of a hundred and fifty before the particular contracting and ownership costs under examination. Investor ownership incurs six in ownership costs and sixteen in contracting costs, leaving a hundred and twenty-eight. Worker ownership incurs twelve and six, leaving a hundred and thirty-two. It costs the workers more to govern, but their ownership saves more elsewhere. Now add eight to the costs of reaching decisions among them. Their remainder falls to a hundred and twenty-four, and the ordering reverses. These figures, carried forward from the earlier treatment, are assumptions for a comparison, not estimates of either form. They show why a single advantage—closer knowledge, easier finance, or a stronger incentive—cannot decide the whole question.

One reason investors may obtain ownership is that lending money while surrendering control can itself be hazardous. The recipients might distribute funds prematurely, choose projects whose gains accrue to them while losses fall disproportionately on lenders, or leave creditors dependent on assets worth little outside the enterprise. Security, covenants, and oversight can address such difficulties, but they have costs. Hansmann therefore treats investor ownership partly as a response to the contracting problems of finance. The worker-owned firm faces this problem too. It can borrow, accumulate earnings, or ask members to contribute capital, but none of these methods makes financing costless. Conversely, finding that capital requires protection does not establish that every other participant’s exposure is best handled through a wage or invoice.

Hansmann, §3.1, pp. 281–283, and §5.2, pp. 293–294, on finance, organization-specific assets, and worker-owners’ capital exposure. The 150-unit comparison is adapted from the later Utility Cooperative; all costs in that illustration are stipulated real losses or resource costs, not payments that should also be counted as someone else’s gain.

Ownership also need not mean that every member decides every operating detail. Worker-owners can elect a board, hire a manager, and delegate authority. The manager may know more about a market than any individual member; the members may know more about the working process than a remote investor. The point of the arrangement is to make those capacities usable, with authority answerable to the appropriate constituency. Hansmann’s distinction between formal rights and effective control is important here. Demonstrating that a complex firm needs management does not demonstrate that investors must appoint it. Demonstrating that workers understand their work does not demonstrate that they possess, without further organization, every competence needed to govern the enterprise.

The difficult part may be agreeing on what the enterprise should do when members’ interests diverge. Three worker-owners could face a proposal worth eight each to two of them and costing twenty to the third. A simple majority would approve it, although the sum of the stipulated gains and losses is minus four. Under a different proposal that benefits each of three members by four, approval produces a total gain of twelve without the same conflict. The first decision is not repaired merely by calling it democratic, and the second is not made costly merely by being collective. What matters includes the decision rule, the interests it has to aggregate, and whether the constitution protects those who can repeatedly be placed on the losing side.

Hansmann puts substantial weight on this heterogeneity. Members may differ in occupation, hours, age, expected tenure, and the value they place on present income against investment. A worker approaching retirement and a new member can have different views about a project whose returns arrive much later. Clear accounts, redemption arrangements, and agreed allocation rules can make some conflicts manageable; familiar work and similar interests can make others less acute. His analysis does not require a cooperative to have identical members, and his discussion rejects the assumption that a departing worker must always forfeit the value of accumulated equity. The rights can be designed in different ways. The design is part of the explanation, not a detail to be fixed after ownership has been assigned.

Hansmann, §2.4, pp. 278–280, and §5, pp. 291–296, including note 50 and note 55’s continuation on p. 297, on conflicting interests, separability, and worker ownership. The majority examples are illustrative comparisons of stipulated gains and losses; they do not claim that money totals exhaust the value of a voting right.

Risk gives the comparison another dimension. A diversified investor can spread savings across enterprises. A worker who puts substantial savings into the firm that supplies the household’s principal earnings can expose both livelihood and capital to the same event. That is a genuine cost of some forms of worker ownership. It is not a reason to pretend that a nonowner has been freed from enterprise risk: layoffs and the loss of specialized skills can impose heavy exposure without an equity certificate. The relevant comparison asks how much risk each arrangement creates or removes, what can be shared, and what safeguards are feasible. A fixed wage is one protection, not a complete inventory of the worker’s security.

A cooperative’s gains also have a boundary. Its members can reduce the cost of dealing with an outside owner while leaving other people in the position the members have escaped. A supplier cooperative may bargain hard with employees; a worker cooperative may face customers with few alternatives; an established membership may prefer distributing more among itself to admitting newcomers. These are possibilities inherent in giving one constituency control, not findings that every cooperative behaves in these ways. They prevent us from equating a benefit to the owning group with a benefit to everyone affected by the firm. The same test applies to investor ownership. Changing the identity of the recipient changes distribution, and may change conduct, but it does not erase the distinction between the two.

The broader inquiry must therefore hold efficiency and authority together without making either a substitute for the other. An arrangement can save resources and still distribute its advantages in a way that invites challenge. A right to participate can have value beyond the amount of output it adds. Hansmann’s framework supplies a disciplined account of the costs that alternative ownership structures may face; it does not settle how all the resulting claims should be valued. The cooperative gives the question a practical form. It asks whether the people whose work, purchases, or supplies sustain an enterprise could govern it on terms that preserve its productive advantages and answer more adequately for its effects.

Hansmann, §§2.1–2.5 and §5, supplies the comparative ownership framework and its limits. The distinction between a constituency’s gains, total resource savings, and the value of participation is the argument developed here; it is not a claim that his efficiency analysis itself supplies a complete standard of justice.

The need for capital leaves open who should govern the cooperation it makes possible.

The cooperative makes visible a choice that the conventional picture of the firm tends to hide. Capital, labor, and customers enter as necessary participants, but their necessity does not write the constitution for them. Someone must decide how control is held, how the remainder is shared, and what duties accompany both. Those decisions can give cooperation an institutional home; they cannot specify everything that will sustain it.
