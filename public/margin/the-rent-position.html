The Position

in print · The Rent · plate ix

The corner is valuable because the crowd passes it; the network is valuable because others have joined. This plate follows the rent of being well placed through the productive cluster, the feedback from earlier adoption, and the power to control access. It asks when timing creates a durable advantage, how a service becomes a toll, and why the success of an established position cannot by itself establish the superiority of its beginnings or the permanence of its claim.

A position can become valuable because it was occupied early, and remain valuable for reasons its first occupant did not create alone.The argument of this plate

The busy corner commands more than the quiet one even when the shopkeeper at each works equally hard. Customers pass the first on their way to other things; the second must persuade them to make a journey. The advantage belongs partly to a location, and the owner of that location can charge for it. Now replace the corner with a marketplace already full of buyers, or a communications system already used by everyone one wishes to reach. The advantage again consists in being where the others are. The Position follows that resemblance from the city's ground to the network's gate, asking how an early arrival can become a durable claim on later activity — and how much the claim tells us about the merits of whoever arrived first.

A locational rent begins with a saving that someone can obtain by being in one place rather than another. Imagine two otherwise identical sites for a business, with the same output, building costs, and working conditions. At the nearer site the annual cost of reaching customers is $10,000; at the farther it is $30,000. If competition for sites passes the whole difference to their owners, the nearer site can command an additional $20,000 a year. These are invented figures, and the assumptions do the explanatory work: the extra payment measures the advantage over an available alternative. It does not measure an additional $20,000 of effort by the landowner. Should the customer move, the road change, or another route become cheap, the differential can change with it. A position earns its premium in relation to what lies around it.

The numerical example is invented: $30,000 − $10,000 = $20,000 in annual differential rent under the stated bidding assumptions. It develops the spatial argument of the Quantities edition's The Location. The differential does not establish the absolute rent of either site.

Much of what makes the location valuable is the productive company it keeps. Marshall's account of localized industry describes workers and employers finding one another, specialized suppliers supported by a cluster, and ideas passing among people engaged in related work. A firm can receive advantages from the neighborhood that its own balance sheet did not finance in full. Those gains give others a reason to join, and a place within reach can become worth bidding for. Yet the cluster's surplus need not pass entirely to landowners: its division depends on the supply of space, the mobility of workers and firms, and the alternatives open to each. The productive crowd and the rent upon admission are connected. They are not the same magnitude, and explaining the first does not by itself determine who receives the second.

Marshall, Principles of Economics, eighth edition, Book IV, chapter X, especially § 3. Alongside the advantages of proximity he discusses labor-market difficulties, high central ground rents, and the vulnerability of a district dependent on one industry. The chapter does not treat every cluster as an unlimited benefit.

An early advantage becomes especially consequential when using a place or a system improves the reasons for using it again. The first workshop may attract a supplier, which makes a second workshop viable; the accumulating skill can then make the district attractive to a third. Some beginnings reflect geography or deliberate construction, others a sequence of choices that could plausibly have gone elsewhere. Once the advantages accumulate, comparing the established center with an empty alternative no longer reveals which had the greater original promise. The empty place lacks the history that would have developed its promise. Success has become one of the causes of further success. This is why timing can enter the explanation of an advantage without rendering the advantage fictitious: the center can now be genuinely better because people began making it better earlier.

Brian Arthur gave a precise form to that dependence on sequence in a model of competing technologies. Adopters differed in their initial preferences, and earlier adoption could improve a technology's appeal to later users. Under strong, continuing increasing returns, chance in the order of those choices could push one technology far enough ahead to draw both kinds of adopters thereafter. The initial choices were sensible for the people making them; the resulting path need not have developed the technology with the best potential. Arthur also showed why the conditions matter. If the improvement from adoption is bounded, different histories can leave the market shared. His basic technologies were open to all, rather than proprietary businesses setting prices. The model explains how a position can become entrenched. A separate account of ownership and pricing is needed to explain who can collect a rent from that entrenchment.

Arthur, “Competing Technologies, Increasing Returns, and Lock-In by Historical Events” (1989), §§ I–II, pp. 117–126. The distinction between unsponsored technologies and strategic firms is explicit. Bounded improvement need not yield single-technology dominance; neither dominance nor inferior lock-in follows from every positive adoption effect.

A network brings this dependence into the value of access itself. A communications service is useful partly because of whom one can contact through it. A system of hardware can become more attractive as its users support the development of complementary software. The first is a direct benefit from other connected users; the second works through the supply of complementary products. In either case, choosing a system can involve expectations about what other people will choose. A technically attractive alternative may begin with too little of the surrounding network to be attractive to the next individual buyer. The difficulty is collective: many might prefer a move if enough others made it, while each has reason to remain if the others stay. The established position offers something real, but its superiority in the present can depend on a coordination problem that protects it from comparison on equal terms.

Katz and Shapiro, “Systems Competition and Network Effects” (1994), pp. 93–100, distinguish direct communications benefits from hardware/software feedback. Whether a network effect is an uninternalized externality depends on pricing and market structure; the terms are not interchangeable.

A small invented example makes the advantage of an installed base visible. Suppose a new user values service A at five units before considering its network and service B at seven. Each existing user reachable through the chosen service adds two units; prices and other costs are equal, and the services cannot communicate with each other. If A has three existing users and B has none, the entrant obtains eleven units from A and seven from B, so chooses A. The next entrant then finds an even larger advantage. At equal network sizes B would be preferred, but that comparison is not available to the lone newcomer. If the services could communicate perfectly, with the same reachable people available through either, B's two-unit advantage would reappear. The example assumes what interoperability achieves; it does not assume that building or governing it is free. It also contains no price-setting owner, so its adoption result alone establishes no monopoly profit.

The step from a useful network to a rent-collecting position occurs when someone can control access to it. A network may span several providers, with compatible services allowing users to change suppliers without abandoning everyone else. It may instead sit behind an exclusive interface, a controlled membership, or contracts that make access depend on the permission of one owner. In the latter case the owner bargains from an advantage that the users helped create by joining. The analogy with the central acre now becomes exact in one respect: a person seeking access encounters someone able to withhold it. It remains incomplete in another. A platform may have built the system, attracted its first users, paid for reliability, and undertaken the coordination that made any gathering possible. Its fee can contain a return to those services alongside a surplus sustained by the difficulty of going elsewhere.

Katz and Shapiro, pp. 100–105, examine sponsorship, investment, commitment, and ownership as responses to network problems. At p. 107 they caution that a dominant position's quasi-rents may repay earlier expenditures. The Quantities Gate supplies the earlier editorial treatment of service and toll within the same platform fee.

The timing of the reckoning matters here as it did for the scarce gift. A successful founder may now possess a valuable position, but at the beginning faced costs, rival systems, and a real possibility that nobody would come. Returns after establishment cannot be compared only with the small cost of admitting one more user if the question concerns the viability of the original investment. Equally, the fact that a founder bore risk does not establish that every later restriction or toll is needed to reward it. The investment's anticipated returns, its costs, and the alternatives available after it succeeds are distinct parts of the account. A buyer who purchases the established business may pay away the expected surplus in its acquisition price; customers can still face a protected toll even when that buyer earns an ordinary return on what it paid. The history of the asset and the position of its present user do not become identical merely because both appear in the same price.

The first arrival therefore receives no theorem promising collection forever. Users can value different features strongly enough to sustain competing systems. An entrant can subsidize adoption, coordinate a group of users, or offer an improvement large enough to overcome the cost of moving. Compatibility can preserve the advantages of connection while making individual providers easier to replace. A new use can shift the relevant network altogether, just as a changed trade route can alter the value of a harbor. These possibilities do not make an entrenched position harmless; they identify the evidence needed to judge its durability. One must ask which users depend on whom, what switching leaves behind, who can supply a bridge, and whether the incumbent can prevent that bridge from being built. Longevity and market share describe an outcome. They do not, by themselves, explain the source or permanence of the power behind it.

Katz and Shapiro, pp. 105–112, discuss differentiation, tipping, sponsorship, innovation, and compatibility. Their analysis allows both excessive inertia and excessively rapid switching that strands earlier users. Compatibility can also involve technical costs and sacrifices of variety or innovation; it is not a costless universal remedy.

What survives these qualifications is the reason to question the inference from success to prior merit. The winner may be better, may have built something better, and may have benefited from getting started while rivals were still gathering the means. These explanations can reinforce one another. The problem begins when the market's present verdict is treated as proof that every earlier alternative was inferior and every later toll is deserved. A crowd's coordinated presence can make today's choice sensible while obscuring the other histories under which the crowd could have prospered. The same distinction governs reform. Preserving the benefits of a common system and preserving the owner's exclusive power over it are separate objectives; a proposed change must show what it does to each. The purpose of examining position is to make those objectives visible before the price quietly settles both.

Arthur, pp. 126–128, explains why observed predominance need not reveal innate superiority, while acknowledging the information problem facing a public authority choosing alternatives. Katz and Shapiro's conclusion, pp. 112–113, likewise separates possible market inefficiency from the further case needed for a particular intervention.

A position can become valuable because it was occupied early, and remain valuable for reasons its first occupant did not create alone.

The answer to the plate's question lies between the origin of the advantage and the work that sustains it. Timing can matter greatly where earlier choices change the options open to later people; it matters less where alternatives remain easy to reach and improvements can be copied. The return to position is the portion of a payment that survives because access to a valuable place is scarce, whether the place is a corner, a cluster, or a network. Its existence establishes neither the owner's complete innocence nor a complete indictment. It requires an account of what was built, what others supplied, what alternatives remain, and who can say no. The first arrival does not necessarily collect forever. The pressing question is why the next arrival must pay, and how much of that payment buys a service rather than the permission of a person already there. The argument remains open.
