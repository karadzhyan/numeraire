The Unearned

in print · The Rent · plate ii

Ricardo's theory showed that the rent of land is a surplus the owner does nothing to create. Henry George drew the conclusion Ricardo declined to: that an income no one earns, made by the whole community, is an income the community may justly reclaim — through a single tax on the value of land. This plate follows that argument and the case against it, to the hardest question in the subject: when is an income earned, and when is it merely owned?

They grow richer, as it were in their sleep, without working, risking, or economizing. What claim have they, on the general principle of social justice, to this accession of riches? John Stuart Mill, Principles of Political Economy · 1848

In 1879 an American printer and journalist named Henry George published a book called Progress and Poverty, and it became, for a season, one of the best-selling works of economics ever written — outsold, in its day, by nothing but the Bible. Its question was the one that had troubled the classical economists, asked now with the force of a prophet: how can it be that material progress, which so swells the wealth of nations, leaves so many in poverty, and gathers its gains into so few hands? George's answer fixed on rent. The value of land, he argued, is created not by its owner but by the presence and the labor of the whole community around it — and to let private owners pocket that value is to let them charge the rest of us for access to the earth we were all born to.

It is hard, at this distance, to credit how loudly that argument rang. Progress and Poverty was not a tract for specialists but a book the ordinary reader devoured, in parlors and union halls on both sides of the Atlantic, and it lifted its author from obscurity to something near sainthood among the discontented of the age. George had felt the paradox in his own bones — he had known poverty as a young man, and watched, in booming California, land values soar while laborers scrambled for work — and he wrote with the urgency of a man who believed he had found the single key to the misery of the modern world. The economists of the universities were wary of him; the public was not. For a generation, to be a single-taxer was to belong to a movement that promised, by one clean stroke of policy, to abolish want without abolishing freedom.

Few books of economics have ever struck the public so hard. Progress and Poverty sold in the hundreds of thousands, was translated across Europe, and made its author, an obscure California journalist, into a prophet with a following. George turned his fame to politics, running for mayor of New York in 1886 on the single-tax cause and finishing second — ahead of the young Theodore Roosevelt, behind the machine candidate. The movement he founded, Georgism, outlived him, and the question he pressed has never quite gone away.

At the center of his case lay a question the classical theory had raised but never pressed: who makes land valuable? Take a bare acre on the open prairie, far from any settlement, and it is worth almost nothing; no one will pay to use it, for there is endless land as good beside it. Now let a town rise nearby — roads cut, a railway laid, neighbors gathered, commerce thickened — and that same acre, untouched by its owner, may come to be worth a fortune. What has happened? The community has happened. Every soul who settled and built and traded in the vicinity has added, by his mere presence and effort, to the worth of that ground; and the owner, who lifted not a finger, harvests the whole of the increase. This was George's unearned increment: the value that society creates and the landholder, by virtue of his title alone, quietly pockets.

George's pivot was to ask who makes land valuable. Not the owner, he answered: an acre in the wilderness is worth little, the same acre at the center of a great city worth a fortune — and the difference is made entirely by the city, by the millions who have settled and built and traded around it. The owner did nothing to summon them. Yet as they arrive, his land rises in worth, and he pockets the rise. This is the unearned increment: value created by the community, captured by whoever holds the title to the ground it gathers upon.

From this one observation George drew both his explanation of the great enigma and his cure for it. The explanation: as a society grows richer, the value it generates flows ever more into the price of the land on which all its activity must take place, so that the owners of land capture the gains of progress while those who merely work or invest compete for what remains — and poverty, far from being relieved by advance, is deepened by it, because advance chiefly swells the rents. The cure followed with a terrible simplicity. If the rent of land is value the community creates, let the community take it back: lay a single tax on the value of land — the bare site, not the buildings or crops, which are the fruit of labor — heavy enough to absorb the whole rent. It would suffice, George believed, to fund the entire work of government, so that every other tax, on wages and trade and enterprise, might be swept away.

From this George built both a diagnosis and a cure. The diagnosis: as a society advances, the value it creates is increasingly soaked up by land rent, so that landowners gain what labor and capital are denied, and want marches in step with wealth. The cure: a single tax on the value of land — not its improvements, only the bare site — set high enough to take the whole rent for the public. It would fund all government, permit the abolition of every other tax, and, since land cannot flee or hide or shrink from taxation, do so without the smallest discouragement to industry.

George did not invent this idea, though none had urged it with such heat. The physiocrats of eighteenth-century France, holding land the only true source of surplus, had called for an impôt unique on its net product a hundred years before him. John Stuart Mill, the most respected economist of the mid-century, had argued that the future rise in land values, owed to no exertion of the owner, might justly be taxed away, and lent his name to a society formed to demand it. Thomas Paine, earlier still, had declared the earth the common inheritance of all mankind, the landholder a debtor to the community for the ground he fenced. And behind them all stood Ricardo, whose theory of rent — examined in the plate before this — had shown with cold precision that rent is a surplus the owner does not create. George took that bloodless theorem and made of it a cause.

George was less an inventor than a trumpet. The physiocrats had urged a single tax on the net product of land a century before him; John Stuart Mill had proposed taxing the future unearned increment and founded a society to press it; Thomas Paine had argued that the earth was the common property of all, and ground-rent a debt owed back to the community. And Ricardo had supplied the engine — the demonstration that rent is a surplus the owner does not create. George's genius was to take that cool theorem and set it on fire.

The single tax has never wanted for critics, and their first objections are eminently practical. To tax the value of land while sparing the value of improvements upon it — the very distinction on which the scheme rests — presumes the two can be cleanly told apart; but in any settled parcel the worth of the site and the worth of the draining, building, and tilling lavished on it over generations are knotted together, and prying them apart would daunt the ablest assessor. A second objection cuts at the justice of the thing. The men who own land today did not, for the most part, seize it; they bought it, at prices that already had the future rent folded in. To strip that rent away now would punish not the original engrossers, long dead, but the present holders who gave good value for their titles — a windfall loss visited on the blameless, indistinguishable, to the one it falls on, from plain confiscation.

Two objections meet the single tax at the threshold. The first is practical: the value of bare land and the value of what has been built or drained or planted upon it are tangled together in any real parcel, and to tax the one without the other demands an assessor of more than human discernment. The second is a matter of fairness: today's owners did not steal their land but bought it, at prices that already counted in the rent — so to confiscate that rent now would fall not on the original engrossers but on those who paid them full value, an expropriation of the innocent.

Two deeper objections strike past the practical to the principle. The first asks why land should be singled out at all. If what offends is unearned gain, land is but one of its shapes: the monopolist draws an unearned rent from his protected position, the heir from his accident of birth, the speculator from his lucky guess, the celebrated name from a gift it did nothing to deserve — and a tax that falls on land alone, leaving every other rent untouched, begins to look less like a principle than a grudge against one kind of owner. The second is bolder, for it denies that "unearned" implies anything. A gain need not be deserved to be justly held, runs the argument; what makes a holding rightful is not the merit of the holder but the justice of how he came by it — by honest purchase, free gift, fair exchange — and on this reckoning the landlord's lawful title is no less valid for the fact that he slept while its value grew.

And why land alone? If the wrong is unearned gain, land is only one of its forms — the monopolist's rent, the heir's fortune, the windfall of the lucky speculator are no more earned, and the single tax passes them all by. There is, besides, a deeper objection that denies the premise: that a gain's being unearned makes it no less justly held. On this view, defended by the libertarian, what matters is not whether wealth was deserved but whether it was justly acquired and freely transferred — and an honest title to land, however little its holder did to earn its rise, is a title still.

Strip the long quarrel to its core and a single distinction remains, the one this whole volume circles: between the income a man earns by contributing something and the income he draws by owning something. Ricardo's achievement, in the plate before, was to prove that rent belongs unmistakably to the second kind — a surplus that falls to the owner not for any service but for his command of the scarce and superior. His failure, if it was one, was to stop at the proof and say nothing of its justice. George refused that reticence. He saw that a morally neutral theorem had a moral edge after all: that if rent is a value the owner does not create, then it is, in the plainest sense, unearned — and an income earned by no one, drawn from a worth that society itself brought into being, is an income society may with a clear conscience reclaim. It was the move from mechanism to indictment, and it has agitated political economy ever since.

The force of George's case is the force of a distinction: between income earned by contributing something and income drawn by owning something. Ricardo had proved that rent belongs to the second kind — a surplus the owner does not produce — but had stopped there, declining to say what followed. George supplied what followed, and it was incendiary: that an income no one earns is an income the community may justly reclaim, because the value behind it was the community's own making. He turned a theorem of distribution into a charge of injustice.

That George's remedy failed is a matter of record; that his question survives is the more important fact. No nation ever adopted the single tax entire, and the prophecy that land rent would swallow the fruits of all progress did not unfold with the inevitability he foretold. Yet the idea would not lie down. It surfaces wherever a community watches the value its own growth has created flow into private hands — when a new transit line doubles the worth of the land beside it, when a stroke of rezoning makes a fortune overnight, when the price of merely living somewhere climbs beyond the reach of those who do the living. A price is a verdict on a hidden contest, mistaken for a fact of nature; and the rent George anatomized is the verdict on a contest whose prize, he insisted, was forged by everyone and awarded to whoever held the deed. Each generation rediscovers the grievance because each rediscovers the rent.

The single tax was never adopted whole; no nation took George at his word. Yet the idea has proved strangely durable — surfacing in the land-value rates of a few cities and countries, in every argument over who should reap the gain a new railway or rezoning confers, in each fresh alarm about the cost of a place to live. It recurs because the question recurs: whenever a fortune is seen to grow from mere ownership of the scarce, someone asks, as Mill and George did, by what right it is kept.

What George could not do was settle the matter, and the reason is the one this volume keeps returning to. The line between the earned and the unearned, so bright in the case of prairie land that gains value while its owner sleeps, blurs the moment one moves an inch from it. The owner who drains and builds has earned something; the speculator who guessed right bore a risk; the talent that commands a fortune was honed as well as given. Land was George's clearest case because it was his purest — a thing made by no one, fixed in supply, its rise of value most visibly the community's gift. But rent does not confine itself to land, and the further one follows it — into scarce skills, into protected positions, into the thousand advantages an economy throws off — the harder the bright line is to hold. George drew it at the water's edge of the soil. Whether it can be drawn at all, across the whole of economic life, is the question the rest of these plates inherit.

An income no one earned, drawn from a value all created — once the rent is seen so, the only question left is who may keep it.

And that question, George understood, is not finally one of economics but of justice, on which the science can shed light without pronouncing the verdict. Ricardo showed how the rent arises; George showed that it is unearned; neither could show, by any theorem, whether the unearned may rightfully be kept — for that turns on what we believe property is for, and whether desert has any claim against a lawful title. The two plates together frame the oldest argument in the subject and decline, honestly, to end it. The value of land is the community's making; the rent is the owner's taking; and between the making and the taking lies a gap that no measurement can close and every politics tries to. The argument remains open.
