Ex vii

The Permit

in print · The Externality · plate vii

The authority fixes a quantity, then lets firms trade the permission to emit it. A two-plant example shows how that exchange can move the cuts to where they cost least, and why freely granted permits still carry an opportunity cost. The cap’s price reveals the cost of its constraint; uncertainty, local exposure and market rules determine what it protects.

The price of permission cannot choose its proper limit.The argument of this plate

The permit begins with a number the market has not supplied. So many tons may enter the air; the authority issues claims sufficient to cover them, and each emitter must surrender a claim for each counted ton. Then the claims may change hands. The plant that finds a cheap way to clean its exhaust can sell what it no longer needs to the plant whose next reduction would be dear. A common limit becomes a field of separate choices. The earlier Permit found its elegance here: a market deliberately made in an activity whose consequences the ordinary market had left outside its reckoning. Yet the elegance belongs to the allocation beneath the limit. It does not relieve anyone of deciding what that limit should be.

The invention joins two decisions that are often confused. One fixes the environmental requirement; the other finds a way to meet it. In W. David Montgomery's 1972 analysis, the requirement was given, and the problem was to achieve it at minimum joint cost. Competitive trading could do that under his assumptions, with licences designed to respect the relation between emissions and pollution at particular places. The argument developed the proposal associated with John H. Dales. It was not a theorem that any printed quantity of permits would make the world better, nor that a ton emitted anywhere must be interchangeable with a ton emitted elsewhere.

W. David Montgomery, “Markets in Licenses and Efficient Pollution Control Programs” (1972), pp. 395–403 and 406–410. The model uses fixed locations, competitive behaviour, convex control costs and specified environmental constraints. Dales is J. H. Dales, author of Pollution, Property & Prices (1968); Montgomery cites that work. Cost effectiveness at a chosen target does not establish the target's desirability.

Imagine two plants, each initially emitting fifty tons, with a combined cap of sixty. Forty tons must therefore be removed. In this invented case the first plant's marginal cost of abatement equals the number of tons it has already cut; the second's is three times that number. Ordering each to cut twenty makes the first spend two hundred and the second six hundred. The last ton costs twenty at one plant and sixty at the other. That difference is an opportunity: shift a little abatement from the second plant to the first, and the same air, under the example's assumption that the emissions are interchangeable, is obtained with fewer resources. Equality of percentage cuts has no special claim to equality of economic sacrifice.

The invented costs are CA(a) = a²/2 and CB(b) = 3b²/2, with a + b = 40 and initial emissions of fifty each. A twenty/twenty cut costs 200 + 600 = 800. Costs include real resources used; permit payments will be recorded separately.

Trading carries the cuts towards thirty at the first plant and ten at the second. Both then face a marginal abatement cost of thirty. Total cost is four hundred and fifty plus one hundred and fifty: six hundred, two hundred less than under equal cuts. A permit price of thirty supports this allocation. Each plant compares the cost of removing another ton with the price of the allowance it would otherwise need, or could otherwise sell. If each starts with thirty allowances, the first emits twenty and sells ten; the second emits forty and buys ten. The three hundred paid between them divides the financial burden. It does not add three hundred to the resources spent cleaning the air.

Minimising a²/2 + 3(40 − a)²/2 gives a = 30, b = 10, with positive second derivative four. Allowance demand is twenty plus forty, equal to sixty. At price thirty the plants' net costs, after a ten-allowance trade, are 150 and 450; together they remain 600.

A freely allocated allowance still has a cost when it is used: the sale that is forgone. That is why giving a plant its initial permits need not remove the incentive to abate. In the example, a ton avoided saves or releases a claim worth thirty regardless of whether the government sold or granted the original allocation. What the initial gift changes is wealth. An auction can bring the value of the claims into the public account; a grant leaves it with the recipients. This separation is sharp only within the stated setting. Financial constraints, market power, entry, or allocations conditional on future output can reconnect the distribution of permits with the decision to emit. A fixed initial grant in a competitive model should not be mistaken for every scheme that calls itself grandfathering.

Montgomery (1972), pp. 408–410, especially Theorems 3.3–3.4 and their qualifications. Initial allocations act as lump-sum transfers in that model; firm behaviour is independent of asset position, and convexity imposes conditions concerning continued operation. The present example holds those features fixed.

The market can discover the cost of the cap. It cannot discover, from that price alone, whether the cap is right.

The price that emerges measures the scarcity of permission under the chosen rule. In the two-plant case it is the cost of the marginal required reduction. It is not automatically the damage another ton would do to the people breathing the air. For those numbers to coincide, the authority would have to choose the cap with that damage and the benefits of the activity properly in view. A permissive cap can leave the market efficient at achieving very little; a severe one can require costly reductions whose benefits do not justify them. The market supplies a method for allocating the obligation. The difficult judgment about the obligation itself remains where it began.

A tax of thirty on each emitted ton would produce the same thirty/ten abatement in this fully known example. The distinction appears when costs change after the policy has been set. With a fixed tax, a plant whose next cut becomes unexpectedly expensive can emit more and pay; with a binding cap and effective compliance, the total allowed quantity holds while the price of scarce allowances can rise. Weitzman's inquiry was which error would cost more. In his local, smooth model, with independent uncertainty in benefits and costs, the comparison depends on the relative slopes of marginal benefit from abatement and marginal abatement cost. A rapidly changing benefit can make departures from the planned quantity costly; a comparatively flat benefit can favour letting the quantity respond to cost. The rule compares two slopes, under specified uncertainty. Calling a pollutant a stock, or uttering the word catastrophe, does not by itself perform that comparison.

Martin L. Weitzman, Prices vs. Quantities, MIT Working Paper 106, April 1973, §§2–4, pp. 6–22. This is the working-paper version. Its local result allows uncertain benefits, assumed independent of cost uncertainty; it does not assume all damage is known. Correlated shocks, large discontinuities and contingent or hybrid policies require further analysis.

The ton itself also needs scrutiny. Suppose one plant's emissions contribute twice as much to a particular neighbourhood's concentration as the other's. Exchanging permission ton for ton can then worsen that neighbourhood's air while leaving the aggregate cap untouched. Montgomery's analysis gave this difficulty a place inside the market's design, distinguishing licences tied to pollution at receiving locations from unrestricted permissions to emit. The general lesson is physical: define what must be kept within a limit, then ask which trades preserve it. A market in undifferentiated tons is appropriate only to the extent that those tons are equivalent for the constraint being enforced. A ceiling on emissions is not a ceiling on every illness, every exposure or every local injustice they may cause.

Montgomery (1972), pp. 401–403 and 411–417, on pollution versus emission licences and location-dependent exchange. In Montgomery’s one-receptor example (p. 403), emissions of five at each source with weights one and two yield concentration fifteen; moving one ton from the first to the second raises it to sixteen despite unchanged total emissions.

The American acid-rain programme made such institutional design concrete. EPA's 1993 account of the rules described transferable sulfur-dioxide allowances, each covering a ton, and a requirement that utilities hold enough to cover their annual emissions. It also described banking unused allowances and stressed that an allowance did not excuse violation of federal ambient air-quality standards. The instrument was therefore a measured permission within a larger set of rules. Banking added time to the bargain: a claim saved from one year could support later compliance. Even the phrase the annual cap needs to be read with the provisions governing which years' claims may be used.

US EPA, “EPA Announces Final Rule on Acid Rain Emission Trading,” March 5, 1993. This contemporary administrative account supports the historical design described here, including the planned 1995 first phase, banking, transferability and the ambient-standard qualification. It is not used as an ex-post estimate of savings or as proof that trading alone caused every subsequent reduction.

The choice between fixing a price and fixing a quantity is thus a choice about which adjustment the rule will permit, within a system able to count and enforce it. A cap does not make measurement unnecessary, and a tax does not make environmental judgment unnecessary. Either can share work with direct local limits; either can be revised as knowledge changes, at the cost of altering the expectations built upon it. The permit's achievement is to make one person's cheap reduction available to another without asking a regulator to name every plant's cheapest technique. The cap still has an author, the market still has rules, and the people exposed still have claims that the price of a traded certificate cannot settle for them.
