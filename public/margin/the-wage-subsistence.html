The Subsistence

in print · The Wage · plate iii

Before the wage was explained by what the worker produces, it was explained by what it costs to keep him in being. Ricardo gave that cost a regulating price; Marx asked what it means to purchase the capacity of a living person. Their question survives the failure of a rigid subsistence law, because the worker’s maintenance must still be paid for — by the wage, by someone outside the bargain, or through a life being worn down. This plate follows the cost through those accounts and asks what can turn a bodily necessity into a claim the worker can enforce.

The cost of keeping a worker alive does not tell us who will be made to pay it.The argument of this plate, after Ricardo and Marx

Before the worker can sell an hour, someone must have kept him alive through the hours that were not for sale. He must have eaten, slept, recovered from yesterday; someone must once have raised the child who became him. The preceding plate looked toward what his work adds to the product. The oldest account of the wage looked in the other direction, toward what it costs to keep its maker in being. It found a sum beneath the bargain — the cost of maintaining the worker and replacing the working generation — and proposed that this sum governed the wage itself. The question seems almost impossible to dispute. Surely a market that depends upon living workers cannot pay less than it costs them to live. Yet that apparent certainty conceals a change of subject: from what a life requires to what a particular employer must pay for its use. Much of the darkness of the subsistence wage lies in the distance between those two sums.

David Ricardo gave the older theory its enduring distinction between the natural and the market price of labor. The market price is the wage actually paid, high when hands are scarce and low when they are plentiful. The natural price is the amount that maintains the laboring population without increasing or diminishing it. The unit of the inquiry is therefore larger than the worker standing at the gate this morning. It includes a family and a succession: food for those who cannot yet earn, maintenance for the body while it works, enough to bring another generation into the labor market when this one is gone. Nor is the natural price simply a number of coins. If bread becomes dearer, the same money carries less life inside it. The account must be made in what the wage purchases, and the apparently impersonal price of labor turns out to have a household behind it.

Ricardo, Principles of Political Economy and Taxation, chapter 5, in the third edition of 1821. The natural price maintains the laboring population; the market price is the payment actually made. The distinction allows the market wage to fall below the natural price.

Even Ricardo's household inhabits a society, rather than a cage supplied with a scientifically measured ration. What must be purchased includes conveniences made necessary by habit, and the habits differ between places and change through time. A dwelling endured by one population can be beneath the standard another has come to expect; something once counted a luxury can enter the ordinary equipment of a working home. This makes the word natural treacherous. It names a regulating price while carrying, inside that price, the history of what people have learned to require. There are bodily needs beneath the account, but no timeless shopping list exhausts it. Once custom has entered the necessities, the question of subsistence is already a question about how a class lives, what it has been able to insist upon, and how far its standard can be pressed downward before the life it recognizes becomes impossible.

In the same chapter, Ricardo expressly denies that the natural price, even reckoned in necessaries, is fixed across countries or periods. His customary standard should be distinguished from a physiological minimum. He also allows continuing capital accumulation to sustain market wages above the natural rate for an indefinite period.

The force supposed to hold the wage near this standard was the pressure of numbers. Higher wages permit families to grow; more children survive to become workers; the enlarged supply of labor presses against its demand. Low wages bring privation, restrain the growth of numbers, and can eventually make labor scarce again. This is the Malthusian reasoning within Ricardo's account, and its cruelty is clearest at the lower end. The mechanism does not prevent a wage from falling below what maintains the worker. It describes how the suffering caused by that fall might reduce the supply of workers until wages recover. An adjustment accomplished through privation is no protection for the person who undergoes it. The theory's long run has room for a restored population balance and for the ruin of the particular families through which the balance was restored. To call the regulating level a floor can make a terrible passage sound like a barrier that cannot be crossed.

Ricardo's account of adjustment includes both reduced numbers through privation and renewed demand for labor. The passage therefore establishes a proposed mechanism of return, not an inviolable minimum for each payment. Its result depends on the movement of demand as well as population.

Marx placed the cost of sustaining the worker inside a different account of what the employer buys. The commodity is labor-power, the capacity to work for a time, whose value in his theory is governed by the labor required to produce the means of its reproduction. Food, shelter, renewal of strength, the upbringing of replacements, and the expense of training all enter. So does the historical and moral element in what a class has become accustomed to need. The capacity purchased and the value produced by exercising it are distinct; their separation is what permits Marx to explain a surplus even when the capacity is bought at its value. But the value of labor-power is not a claim that every pay packet invariably contains the full cost of a normal working life. Marx explicitly considers a price so low that the capacity can be maintained only in a damaged condition. The body can still be present at work while the terms of its maintenance are being broken.

Marx, Capital, volume I, chapter 6, distinguishes labor-power from its use, includes generational replacement and training, and explains the historical and moral element. His discussion of physical indispensability expressly distinguishes the minimum from maintenance in normal condition.

And the pressure upon wages in Marx's industrial account need not wait for a generation of children to grow up. Accumulation draws workers into employment and changes the means by which production is carried on; machinery and contraction can also release them into a reserve from which employers recruit. The employed bargain in the presence of those seeking work. That account permits rising consumption while the dependence upon selling labor-power continues. It is therefore a mistake to join Ricardo, Lassalle, and Marx into a single voice predicting that workers can never live better than their grandparents. Marx attacked Lassalle's iron law directly: if poverty were imposed by a natural population law, abolishing the wage system would not abolish its cause. His quarrel reached the organization of production and the appropriation of its product, even where the worker was better paid. The theories meet at the cost of reproducing labor, then part company over what holds the worker there and what would count as an escape.

Capital, chapter 25, sections 1 and 3, permits higher wages and consumption while analyzing accumulation and the reserve army. The rejection of Lassalle's formulation appears in Critique of the Gotha Programme, part II. Neither text licenses reducing Marx's position to a fixed biological wage.

The historical escape from earlier living standards was real, and it took longer than the word escape makes easy to remember. Robert Allen's reconstruction of British industrialization follows a period when output per worker grew much faster than the real wage, followed by a later period of substantial wage gains. The timing and size depend on the historical series, whose disagreements he addresses; the lesson does not require every worker everywhere to have followed one curve. It requires taking the interval seriously. A larger product can be made before those making it obtain a substantially larger life. Later prosperity refutes an inescapable return to an unchanged ration, but does not repay the generations who lived through the delay. Neither the proposition that industry must hold the worker at subsistence nor the proposition that its advance must immediately lift him can describe that passage. Productive capacity, population, and the distribution of the product have histories that must be followed together.

Allen, “Engels’ Pause” (2009), pp. 418–421, describes the British sequence and the competing wage and output estimates. This particular historical reconstruction supports the distinction between productivity growth and the timing of wage gains; it supplies no universal history of an identical subsistence level.

The cost of maintaining a worker also survives when an employer does not pay all of it. Consider an invented household account: a week's ordinary maintenance costs five hundred dollars, while the job brings home four hundred. Another household member supplies seventy dollars and savings supply thirty. The account balances. The worker returns on Monday, and the employer can continue paying four hundred without having proved that a working life costs only four hundred. With three hundred dollars initially saved and everything else fixed, the reserve lasts ten weeks. Afterward the account must change: another source of income, more work, a lower standard, a debt, a missed payment. The example contains no estimate of a living wage. It exposes an error in reading the survival of an employment relationship as evidence that its wage covers the cost of sustaining it. Some of that cost can be carried outside the employment contract, quietly enough to disappear from the employer's books.

The household figures are invented: 400 + 70 + 30 = 500, and 300 ÷ 30 = ten weeks. The example develops the reproduction question in Marx's chapter 6, which averages recurring and less frequent maintenance costs. The household financing arrangement is this plate's illustration, not an observation or a model attributed to Marx.

Even an account that no longer balances need not stop the worker reaching the gate tomorrow. A repair can be postponed, a meal made smaller, a debt allowed to grow, rest exchanged for another shift. These possibilities differ profoundly: a second income may maintain the household indefinitely, while a spent reserve cannot; a lower expense may be an economy, while an omitted necessity is a deterioration. Their common significance is that the wage can be paid today while part of the cost of earning it is transferred elsewhere or deferred. A body is not a machine that halts at the first overdue maintenance payment. It can be used while being worn down. The fact that work continues tells us that labor has been supplied; it cannot, by itself, tell us whether the conditions of a sustainable working life have been met. Here the supposed floor reveals its most dangerous ambiguity. The limit of what can be endured for another day is not the cost of remaining whole through a working life.

The worker's reservation wage asks a different question from the cost of that life. It asks what offer the worker will accept given the alternatives available at the time. A person can reject four hundred because another job pays five hundred, or accept four hundred because refusing leaves nothing. The household's needs can remain the same in both cases. Need enters the choice, sometimes urgently, but does not oblige an employer to offer enough to meet it. This is why hunger can weaken a bargaining position even as it makes the demand for a higher wage more compelling. The cost of maintenance describes what must somehow be supplied; the reservation wage describes a choice among what is presently obtainable. Confusing them turns the worker's willingness to accept a bad offer into proof that the offer is sufficient. It may establish only that every available refusal is worse.

The distinction continues the final paragraph of the expanded Subsistence and the unequal alternatives of the Margin Bargain. The hypothetical choices isolate the difference between a maintenance requirement and an acceptance threshold; they do not estimate anyone's actual reservation wage.

A society can change those alternatives, and in doing so change how much of the worker's need reaches the bargaining table. Support during a search can make a refusal possible; an accessible rival employer can give it a destination; an enforceable wage rule can forbid an offer that the worker might otherwise accept. These are different instruments, with different costs and consequences. None follows automatically from the discovery that people must eat. Each supplies an institutional connection between a need and a claim that can be made against someone. The later plates on the legal floor and insurance take up those connections. Their place in the volume is already visible here: the body supplies the necessity, while a law, a resource, or an alternative employment can supply the means of insisting that the necessity be met on better terms.

Calling a wage a living wage therefore makes a claim about the life it should sustain, and the claim deserves to be stated in full. A week of calories is one standard; a household able to raise children, maintain health, replace worn necessities, and participate in its society is another. The standard must specify whose needs are counted, what time is allowed for rest and care, and which costs are shared through public provision or other earnings. Its social content does not make it arbitrary. Bodies impose requirements, prices impose costs, and the organization of life makes some goods necessary that another age could do without. But choosing that standard is an act for which a society must answer. It cannot be smuggled into a market wage and then announced as something the market has independently certified.

The cost of keeping a worker alive does not tell us who will be made to pay it.

There is a boundary beneath a working life: deprivation eventually destroys the capacity on which the employment depends. There is no corresponding promise that each wage will meet the cost before the damage is done. The cost can be shared, postponed, or left to the person who bears the damage; the market can continue recruiting while particular workers fall away. The oldest theory of the wage remains unsettling because it insists on bringing these costs into view. Its strongest enduring question is whose account contains them, and who can compel that account to be paid. A price is a verdict on a hidden contest, mistaken for a fact of nature; subsistence names the natural necessity inside this contest without settling its verdict. What the body needs is real. What the worker can be brought to endure is real too. The work of keeping the latter from becoming the measure of the former is the part no natural price performs for us. The argument remains open.
