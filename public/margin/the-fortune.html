The Fortune

in print · The Premium · plate ii

The premium for bearing risk is paid, in the end, only to those who bore it and survived — and survival, in a world of chance, is not the same as skill. This plate gives the volume's thesis. For every fortune built on a bold bet, many equally bold were ruined and forgotten, so that the survivors are mistaken for the wise and luck is read as judgment. It asks what a great fortune actually proves — the wisdom to have seen what others missed, or the luck to have been on the winning side of a gamble many played and most lost — and why the difference, so hard to draw, matters so much.

Fortune is the arbiter of one half of our actions, but she still leaves us to direct the other half, or perhaps a little less. Niccolò Machiavelli, The Prince, 1532

Behind every great fortune, the saying goes, lies a great crime; but more often there lies a great risk that happened to come good. The successful entrepreneur, the celebrated investor, the founder whose bet paid off — each is honored for the courage and the skill with which he bore uncertainty and prevailed. Yet for every one who bore that risk and won, others bore the same risk and were ruined, and they do not write memoirs. The premium that rewards the survivor is real; the question is what it rewards. Is it the wisdom to have seen what others missed, or merely the luck to have been on the winning side of a gamble many played and most lost? The price of risk, paid out to those who bore it and lived, cannot easily tell the brave apart from the fortunate — and a great deal turns on whether we can.

We tell the story of a great success as a story of vision. The founder who saw, before anyone, what the world would want; the investor who kept his nerve when others fled; the entrepreneur who read the future and bet on it. The shelves groan with these accounts, each tracing a fortune back to a quality of mind — foresight, daring, discipline, an eye for the main chance. The winner himself, asked the secret, will rarely answer luck; he will recall the conviction that carried him through the dark months, the principle he never abandoned, the bet everyone told him was mad. And we believe him, because the story is satisfying and because he is, after all, standing in front of us, rich and vindicated. The premium he was paid for bearing his risk is taken as the market's own verdict: this man was right, and his reward is the measure of how right.

The memoir of the successful is a reliable form: the early doubters, the lonely conviction, the vindication. What it cannot contain is a control group — the others who held the same conviction, ran the same risk, and are not available to be interviewed, having failed. Every such book is written from the one chair at the table that was not cleared away, and tells, with perfect sincerity, the story that chair affords.

The trouble is that the story is told only by those who lived to tell it. For every founder whose bet built an empire, a hundred made bets just as bold, just as considered, and lost everything; and the bankrupt do not publish, do not lecture, do not endow the chairs from which the lessons of success are taught. We study the winners because they are there to be studied, and from them we draw conclusions about what works — never noticing that we have selected our evidence by its outcome, and so can learn nothing about whether the choice was wise. The error has a name and a famous illustration. In the Second World War, a statistician asked where to armor returning bombers noted that the planes came back peppered with holes everywhere but the engines — and concluded that the armor belonged precisely where the holes were not, since the planes hit in the engines were the ones that had not come back at all. To study only the survivors is to read the wounds of the living and miss the wounds that kill.

The statistician was Abraham Wald, and the reasoning is the canonical case of survivorship bias: conditioning on survival hides exactly the information one needs. The returning planes are a biased sample, over-representing those whose damage was survivable; the fatal damage is invisible because its victims are absent. The same bias governs every inference drawn from winners alone — successful funds, surviving firms, celebrated founders — and it runs, almost always, toward crediting the survivors with what was merely the luck of not being hit where it counts.

Selection does more than hide the losers; given enough players, it manufactures the appearance of genius from nothing but chance. Set ten thousand people to flipping coins, eliminating any who flip tails, and after ten rounds you will be left with nine or ten who have flipped heads ten times running. They have done nothing skillful — the coins were fair — yet there they stand, an elite of one in a thousand, and if coin-flipping carried prestige they would be feted as masters, invited to explain their technique, their discipline, their feel for the coin. The market runs this contest continuously and at vast scale. With millions placing bets, some will assemble long records of success by luck alone, and those few, plucked from the throng by their results, will be indistinguishable, to themselves and to everyone watching, from the genuinely skilled. The longer the lucky streak, the more certain everyone becomes that it cannot be luck — which is precisely the inference the contest is built to defeat.

The point is not that skill never exists but that a large enough population of triers will produce, by chance alone, a tail of spectacular records that mimic it exactly. To tell the two apart one needs many independent trials per person — enough to average out luck — and the world rarely supplies them: a career holds a handful of big bets, a startup a single life. With so few trials, the lucky and the skilled are statistically entangled, and the winner's own certainty that he is the latter is worth no more than the coin-flipper's.

Why are so many at the table to begin with? Because most people badly overrate their own chances — a fact Adam Smith saw with his usual clarity. The young, he wrote, are full of an absurd presumption in their own good fortune; every man overvalues the chance of gain and undervalues the chance of loss, which is why so many crowd into the risky, glittering professions and the lottery of life is, on the whole, underpaid. Keynes saw the same force at the heart of enterprise, which he thought moved less by cold calculation than by animal spirits — a spontaneous urge to action that, faced with a future it cannot compute, simply leaps. The overconfident many enter the contest; the few who happen to win emerge as its visible survivors, and their winnings are, in part, the forfeited stakes of the losers they will never meet. So the premium for risk is not only a reward for bearing uncertainty but a prize drawn from a pool the overconfident filled — and the prize-winner, surveying his fortune, concludes not that he was lucky in a game most lose but that he was wise where others were foolish. The conclusion is natural, flattering, and, in the generality of cases, unprovable.

Smith's discussion of the lottery of professions in the Wealth of Nations is among the first to treat overconfidence as a systematic economic force. Because people overrate their chances in risky callings — the law, the arts, the speculative trades — those fields are overcrowded and their average returns fall below what the risk would warrant; the prizes are real but rarer than the entrants believe. The successful, seeing only their own success, mistake the long odds they beat for proof of a superiority the odds themselves deny to almost everyone who tried.

Here, then, is the volume's thesis in its sharpest form. The premium for risk is paid out to those who bore a risk and survived, and we read the payment as the market's verdict on their merit — this one brilliant, that one a fool — when it is in large part a record of who was spared. A price is a verdict on a hidden contest, mistaken for a fact of nature; and the price of risk, totted up into the fortunes of the survivors, renders a verdict on a contest between the player and chance that it has no power to adjudicate. We see the fortune and infer the skill, as if the size of the reward measured the soundness of the judgment that won it; but the reward measures only that its owner was on the winning side of a gamble, and which side that would be was, in most cases, not his to know. The courage was real, and the risk was real. The step from there to desert — the step that turns a fortunate survivor into a proven seer — is the one the price cannot warrant and is everywhere taken to have made.

A single outcome, however large, carries almost no information about the skill behind it, because luck and skill produce the same observable result — a win. Inference would require repetition under independent conditions, the way a casino learns its edge over millions of hands; a person who makes a few great decisions in a lifetime offers no such sample. The market nonetheless renders its verdict on the outcome alone, and the verdict, lacking the trials that could ground it, is mostly a reading of fortune dressed as a reading of worth.

The case against this skepticism is real, and it must be put fairly. Skill plainly exists. Some investors have beaten the market over horizons too long, and through conditions too varied, for chance to be a comfortable explanation; some entrepreneurs build not one success but several, in different fields, as if carrying the cause within them; some judgment genuinely sees what the crowd misses, and the seeing is rewarded. Frank Knight built his theory of profit on exactly this — that the entrepreneur is paid for bearing an uncertainty no formula can price, and that bearing it well, choosing which leaps to make and which to refuse, is a real and scarce capacity, the very thing the premium exists to call forth. A world that paid nothing for good judgment under uncertainty would get less of it, and be the poorer. The premium is not a pure lottery; it rewards, among the lucky, a genuine discernment the merely lucky do not possess, and the best risk-bearers are not the most fortunate but the most clear-eyed.

The strongest evidence for skill is persistence beyond what luck would produce: records long enough, across regimes different enough, that the coin-flipping model strains. A few investors and founders clear that bar, and their success is rightly read as judgment. The difficulty is that they are rare and known only in retrospect, and that for every one of them the same long record is claimed by many whose streak chance will end — so that even genuine skill is hard to certify at the moment it would matter, and easy to counterfeit with luck.

Grant skill its due; the difficulty is that the premium cannot tell it from luck, and the inability is not a technicality but the heart of the matter. Faced with a fortune, we usually cannot say how much was judgment and how much was being spared, because the evidence — a handful of outcomes, selected for success — will not bear the weight of the distinction. And because we cannot tell, we default to crediting the winner, and that default does real work in the world. It launders luck into desert: the fortune, once attributed to merit, becomes earned, and so beyond the reach of any claim that it be shared or even questioned. It miseducates: the young are taught to copy the habits of survivors whose true lesson is only that they were not hit where it counts. And it absolves the order that dealt the cards — who was allowed to play, who could afford to lose and try again — by telling a flattering story in which the winners simply deserved to win. The premium for skill and the premium for luck are paid in one coin, and a world that cannot distinguish them pays both as though they were the first.

The attribution of fortune to merit is not a harmless courtesy; it is load-bearing. It converts an outcome into an entitlement, and so supplies the moral premise — that the successful earned what they hold — on which much resistance to redistribution rests. To notice how much of the reward was luck is not to deny that skill exists but to deny that the existing distribution of fortunes is its faithful measure; and that denial is exactly what the flattering story is built to prevent.

So the price of risk, at the end, hands down a verdict it cannot justify. A price is a verdict on a hidden contest, mistaken for a fact of nature; and the contest here is the oldest in the book — between what we do and what merely befalls us — fought out in every fortune and settled, by the market, in favor of whoever happened to win. The premium is honest about what it is: a payment to whoever bore a risk and came through. It is the world's reading of that payment — fortune as proof of foresight, wealth as the wage of wisdom — that is the verdict, and the verdict outruns its evidence. To price a risk is a matter of arithmetic; to say who deserved its reward is a matter the arithmetic cannot reach, and the confident attribution of success to skill is where this table's whole suspicion comes most sharply to a point. The fortune is real. Whether it is earned is the question the price answers without the right to.

The market is a faithful recorder of survival and a poor judge of virtue, and the founding error this volume names is the habit of reading the first as the second. Survival is rewarded because the premium flows to whoever bore the risk and remained; virtue, if it was present, is rewarded only incidentally, indistinguishable in the payment from the luck beside it. To take the reward as the measure of the merit is to let the bookkeeping of who survived stand in for the harder accounting of who deserved to — an accounting the price was never able to perform.

A great fortune proves its owner was on the winning side of a gamble — not that he knew which side that would be.

With this the volume's first pair is complete: the price of risk, and the contest beneath it. The plates that follow will take the premium apart piece by piece — the calculus of the odds, the curve of aversion that sets the price, the pooling of insurance and the spreading of the hedge, the puzzle of the equity premium, the tail the everyday price forgets, and the Knightian uncertainty no premium can cover — before the volume turns, at its end, to who should bear the risks that none can escape. But the suspicion raised here runs through all of them: that the reward of risk and the reward of luck are paid in the same coin, and that the world's confidence in telling them apart is mostly misplaced. The fortune that wears the look of the wage of courage is kin to the rent of mere ownership, the return that flows to a holding rather than a deed; the surplus we cannot explain and so credit to a virtue is the residual by which growth is measured and ignorance confessed; and the great fortune, however won, is in the end a claim on what others produce, whose justice this table will not stop asking after. The price of risk is real, and it renders a verdict; whether that verdict is just is another matter entirely. The argument remains open.
