Ut xi

The Axiom

in print · The Utility · plate xi

A utility representation is conditional on relations among choices. This plate distinguishes a finite ordinal ranking from the stronger structure of expected utility, works through the original Allais and Ellsberg comparisons, and identifies precisely which restrictions the paired preferences violate. It then separates the model’s descriptive task from the reasons a chooser might have to accept its rules as a standard.

A contradiction identifies the choice; it does not make it for us.The argument of this plate

The number arrives with conditions attached. To say that a person maximizes utility can mean that his choices possess a certain order, that his gambles obey a particular arithmetic, or that he ought to choose as though they did. These claims have different burdens. A numbering can accurately record an order without explaining its origin. A theorem can establish what follows from assumptions without showing that anyone meets them. A rule can expose a conflict without deciding which side of the conflict deserves allegiance. The axioms make those burdens visible if we resist letting their common name do all the work.

Begin with the modest task of arranging a finite set of alternatives. Completeness requires a comparison: one is at least as good as the other, or both are equally ranked. Transitivity requires the comparisons to fit together. If A is at least as good as B, and B as C, A cannot fall below C. A complete, transitive ranking of finitely many alternatives can be represented by numbers, with equal numbers for ties and higher numbers for higher positions. This is a useful representation, but the distances between those numbers are free to change. The numbering has not discovered how much better A is than B, or why the person prefers either.

For a finite complete and transitive weak ordering, group tied alternatives into indifference classes and assign successive integers in their order. This is a direct construction, not a claim about every infinite choice space. Von Neumann and Morgenstern discuss the contrast between ordering and numerical measurement in Theory of Games and Economic Behavior, ch. I, §§ 3.1–3.3, pp. 15–20.

An inability to make a comparison must not quietly be entered as indifference. Indifference says the alternatives occupy the same place in the ordering; incomparability says that ordering has not supplied their relative places. Nor is every hesitation evidence of a contradiction. The person may need information, may be comparing consequences differently, or may have changed his mind. A claim about stable preferences has to specify the alternatives and circumstances across which stability is expected. Otherwise, the model can be protected against every observation by inventing a new preference after each choice, and the impressive word representation has become little more than a transcription.

Lotteries demand more than this ordinal discipline. Continuity connects an intermediate alternative with suitable mixtures of better and worse alternatives. Mixture independence requires a ranking to survive when each option is combined with the same third lottery in the same proportion. Together with the ordering and mixture structure, such conditions support the expected-utility representation developed in The Wager. Independence here concerns probabilistic mixtures. It is not merely the claim that adding another item to a menu should leave the ranking of the original items unchanged. The distinction matters because the famous tests change the structure of gambles, not simply the number of dishes on a table.

Von Neumann and Morgenstern, §§ 3.3–3.7, pp. 17–29. Their axioms organize ordering, continuity and combinations of alternatives, including reduction of successive mixtures. The modern common-mixture statement describes the independence property of the resulting expected-utility ordering; it is distinct from menu-based independence of irrelevant alternatives. Allais made the restriction confront certainty. His 1953 questionnaire offered a hundred million francs for certain against a gamble: a ten-per-cent chance of five hundred million, an eighty-nine-per-cent chance of a hundred million, and a one-per-cent chance of nothing. In a second pair, an eleven-per-cent chance of a hundred million competed with a ten-per-cent chance of five hundred million, with nothing otherwise in either case. Consider someone who chooses the sure hundred in the first pair and the chance of five hundred in the second. The first choice protects a fortune against a small chance of getting nothing; the second sacrifices one percentage point of winning for a much larger prize. Each has an intelligible motive. Their combination is the test.

Allais, “La psychologie de l’homme rationnel devant le risque: la théorie et l’expérience,” Journal de la Société de statistique de Paris 94 (1953), p. 59, questions 35–36. The amounts and probabilities here are those of the original. Instructions at pp. 55–57 treat the offers separately, once only, under present personal circumstances, net of tax and with unchanged purchasing power. The stated preference pattern is being examined, not assigned to every respondent.

Call the four prospects A, B, C and D in that order, and measure prizes in millions. Preferring A to B requires the utility of a hundred to exceed eighty-nine per cent of itself, plus ten per cent of the utility of five hundred and one per cent of the utility of nothing. Remove the common term. Eleven per cent of the utility of a hundred must exceed the other two terms. But preferring D to C requires precisely the reverse inequality. No additional curvature in the utility of money can reconcile them. An eighty-nine-per-cent branch paying a hundred in both of the first two prospects has become a branch paying nothing in both of the second two. Expected-utility arithmetic requires that common change to preserve their order.

The two strict comparisons require both .11u(100) > .10u(500) + .01u(0) and its reverse, holding background wealth and outcome values fixed. Allais’s theoretical dispute appears in § I, pp. 47–55. The contradiction is between the paired choices and expected utility, not between either choice alone and aversion to risk.

The failure is narrower than the declaration that the chooser has no utility function. He could still give all four lotteries a complete and transitive ordering. What he cannot do, while retaining these two strict preferences, is represent that ordering by the expectation of one fixed utility assigned to their monetary consequences. An ordinal score for each whole lottery can record more patterns than the expected-utility formula permits. Allowing that flexibility saves the possibility of representation, but gives up part of the structure that allowed choices among mixtures to determine a cardinal scale. The lost restriction should be named, rather than mistaken for the disappearance of every order.

Ellsberg’s example tests what happens when the chances are incompletely known. An urn contains thirty red balls and sixty divided between black and yellow in an undisclosed proportion. A person first chooses between a hundred dollars on red and a hundred on black, with nothing otherwise. He then chooses between a hundred on red-or-yellow and a hundred on black-or-yellow. Preferring red in the first pair and black-or-yellow in the second selects the known chance each time: one-third, then two-thirds. The change between the pairs is a hundred-dollar payment on yellow, added to both options. The same event has been altered in the same way on both sides.

Ellsberg, “Risk, Ambiguity, and the Savage Axioms,” Quarterly Journal of Economics 75 (1961), pp. 653–656. His observations were informal; p. 655, note 6, calls for controlled experimentation. The urn and payment amounts are his, while the paired strict preferences specify the pattern under examination.

Suppose one additive probability distribution and the same value for the prize in every colour govern both choices. The first preference requires red to be more probable than black. The second requires black plus yellow to be more probable than red plus yellow; cancelling yellow requires black to be more probable than red. Those demands cannot both hold. This is the force of the sure-thing test. Greater concavity in the utility of money does not answer it, since every bet offers the same two monetary consequences. Nor does the contradiction show that the person has no beliefs about the urn. It identifies a particular way of recovering beliefs and values from choices that cannot represent the whole pattern.

Ellsberg, § II, especially pp. 648–650 and p. 655, note 5. With state-independent utility payoffs normalized to 1 and 0, the pattern requires pR > pB and pB + pY > pR + pY. Either single comparison can be represented; the pair cannot be represented in that way.

An axiom tells us what cannot be kept together. It does not, by itself, tell us what to surrender.

As descriptions, the models must confront such patterns in the circumstances where they occur. A useful idealization may still explain other decisions, provided its departures and its domain are made clear. As standards, the axioms ask a different question: would the chooser endorse the restriction after seeing what his answers imply? Ellsberg reported disagreement even among reflective respondents, including people who revised their answers and people who wished to retain the offending preferences. Allais objected to settling that disagreement by defining rationality as obedience to the very axioms in dispute. The formal result can sharpen the discussion. It cannot replace it.

Allais, § I.9, pp. 54–55; Ellsberg, § I, pp. 645–646, and the end of § II, pp. 655–656. These sources establish the authors’ dispute and reported reactions, not population-wide frequencies or the superiority of every departure from the axioms.

A defended standard must say why the feature it excludes should not matter. Perhaps a common consequence really supplies no reason to reverse a choice; perhaps uncertainty about the evidence is itself something a decision procedure should accommodate. The alternatives can be examined without declaring every hesitation irrational or treating every instinct as authoritative. What matters is whether the account survives the comparisons it asks us to accept. The final plate, The Worth, then takes a further step. Even a preference consistently represented, or reflectively endorsed, leaves the question of what that preference should be worth when another person’s life is placed beside it.
