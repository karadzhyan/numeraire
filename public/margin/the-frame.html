The Frame

in print · The Rule · plate i

There is no market without rules. Before two people can trade, something must already have settled what each owns, what a promise is worth, what serves as money, and who will come if one of them simply takes the other's goods. The market is not a state of nature but a structure of rules, made and enforced — and the free market, conceived as a market free of rules, is a place that has never existed. This plate lays the ground for the volume: the market is built, not found.

The sense of justice and injustice is not deriv'd from nature, but arises artificially, tho' necessarily, from education, and human conventions. David Hume, A Treatise of Human Nature (1739–40)

There is no such thing as a free market, if by free one means a market free of rules. The phrase conjures a state of nature — exchange as it would occur if only the State would stand aside — but no such state exists, and never has. Before two people can trade, something must already have settled what each of them owns, what counts as a fair promise between them, what they will use for money, and who will be called if one of them simply takes the other's goods and runs. These are not features of the landscape; they are rules, made by human beings and enforced by human power, and a market is nothing but the elaborate structure they compose. The question is never whether the market shall have rules. It is only whose rules, and in whose service.

Begin by taking the claim apart, for it is easy to grant and hard to feel. A market — any market, from a village stall to a global exchange — rests on at least four things, not one of which grows wild. It rests on property: there must be a settled answer to what may be owned, and by whom, or there is nothing one is entitled to sell and nothing the buyer can be sure he has bought. It rests on contract: a promise to pay next month, or to deliver on receipt, must be more than a hope — a thing the law will hold the breaker to. It rests on money: some token all will take, in which prices can be quoted and debts discharged, whose worth does not evaporate between the sale and the spending. And beneath these it rests on enforcement: a power, somewhere, that will pursue the thief and compel the cheat, without which property is a wish and contract a courtesy. Strip all four away and the market is simply gone. Every one of them is an institution — the rules of the game, in North's phrase, within which the playing happens — made, upheld, and paid for.

Take the plainest transaction — a loaf bought for a coin — and count what must already be in place. The baker must own the loaf, and own it so that he may part with it; you must own the coin. There must be a settled understanding that when the coin changes hands the loaf is yours, and that the law will side with you should he pocket both. The coin must be a thing the baker will take, whose worth he trusts. And somewhere a power must stand ready, should either of you simply seize what the other holds. Four institutions behind the simplest purchase, and not one of them given by nature.

The error the plate exists to correct is the picture of a natural market lying beneath all regulation, which the State may approach or retreat from but did not create. On that picture the free market is the default — what one gets when government does nothing — and every rule is an intrusion upon a prior, self-subsisting thing. But there is no prior thing. Dissolve the courts and the police and the law, and you do not lay bare a pure market humming underneath; you lay bare the war of all against all, in which the strong take what they please, no promise binds beyond the reach of the promisee's own arm, and exchange shrinks to what two wary parties can manage at knife-point. The orderly market the laissez-faire imagination supposes to be natural is in truth the achievement of an enormous and unceasing exertion of public power — the very power it fancies itself free of. To leave such a market alone is not to remove its rules. It is to enforce, in silence, the particular rules already in place.

The picture under attack is familiar: that beneath all regulation lies a natural market — exchange as it would run if only the State withdrew — and that policy decides how far to depart from it. But there is no such market to withdraw to. Withdraw the State entirely and you uncover not a pristine economy but the war of all against all, in which the strong take what they please and no contract outlasts the strength behind it. To leave the market alone is not to remove the rules but to keep a particular set of them, and to dignify their enforcement with the name of non-interference.

This was the burden of Polanyi's great argument, leveled at the laissez-faire faith in its confident heyday. The self-regulating market of the nineteenth century, he showed, was no spontaneous growth that enlightened governments had at last ceased to strangle; it was a deliberate construction, and a violent one. To make labor into a commodity a market could price, old bonds and customary rights were torn up and the rural poor driven into the mills; to make land a commodity, the commons were enclosed; to make money behave, currencies were chained to gold at brutal social cost. The free market was planned into being by the concentrated power of the state, and needed that power's ceaseless backing to endure. What looked like the economy left to its own devices was the economy remade, root and branch, to a design — one that then took pains to present its own outcomes as the natural and inevitable workings of trade. The market was not what remained when planning stopped. It was itself a plan.

Polanyi wrote amid the wreckage that the self-regulating market had helped to make, and his thesis was that this market was the most planned thing of all. The free economy of the nineteenth century did not arise from leaving things alone; it was built, deliberately and often brutally, by states that abolished customary rights, drove peasants from the common land into wage labor, and bound their currencies to gold. An immense exertion of power was required to manufacture the conditions under which the market could appear to run itself. The laissez-faire order was planned — only the planning was the planning of a frame, inside which the outcomes could then be left to look spontaneous.

Once this is seen, the familiar opposition between the market and the State, between freedom and regulation, dissolves into something subtler and more honest. The choice a society faces is never between having rules and going without them, for the marketless state of nature is no option anyone proposes; it is always and only a choice among different sets of rules. What we call deregulation is thus never the removal of rules but the swapping of one set for another: strike down the rule that shielded the borrower and you have not freed the loan but enthroned the lender; abolish the tenant's protection and you have not emptied the field of rules but handed it to the landlord. Each such change makes winners and losers as surely as the arrangement it replaces, and may be defended on its merits — but never as a return from artifice to nature, since there is no nature to return to. The deepest trick the word free performs is to lend the rules now in force the dignity of having no author, so that to question them looks like quarreling with the order of things itself.

It follows that deregulation is a misnomer. One never removes the rules; one exchanges one set for another, and the new set, like the old, picks its winners and losers. To strike down a protection for tenants is to install a freedom for landlords; to free a market is to bind whoever the old restraint had shielded. The change may be wise or foolish, but it is never a retreat from rules into their absence, for there is no absence to retreat into. The peculiar danger of the word is that it lets a change in the rules pass for the lifting of an unnatural burden — so that the new arrangement quietly inherits the prestige of the natural.

And here the market as a whole shows itself the largest instance of the conviction these volumes have pressed from the first. A price is a verdict on a hidden contest, mistaken for a fact of nature — and the market is the arena in which every such verdict is rendered, the structure of rules within which all the prices form. If each price is a made and contested thing wearing the mask of a natural fact, the arena that produces them is the same thing raised to a higher power: the most elaborate artifact our species has built, mistaken, in its entirety, for a state of nature. We speak of the market as we speak of the weather and the tides — a force with laws of its own, indifferent to our wishes, simply there. But the weather was not enacted and the market was; the tides need no courts and the market collapses without them. To take the market for a fact of nature is to commit the master mistake at its grandest scale: to mistake the whole made and contested frame of economic life for a feature of the world that no one chose and no one need answer for.

The market is the arena in which all the prices of the foregoing volumes are struck, and we have learned to read each price as a verdict on a hidden contest. The arena itself is the deepest case. For the market as a whole presents itself as a fact of nature — the way exchange simply works, the default condition of a free people — when it is in truth the most elaborate thing our species has built, a vast machinery of rules demanding constant maintenance and backed at every point by power. To take it for natural is to commit, at the largest scale there is, the error these volumes were written to name.

The case on the other side is real, and the plate would be dishonest to slight it. There is a profound sense in which the market is not made: no one designs the price of wheat, or decrees how many shoes a city needs; these emerge, unwilled, from the scattered choices of millions, and the order they compose is genuinely no one's plan. This was Hayek's deep insight, and a later plate will honor it — the market as a marvel of spontaneous coordination, achieving without a planner what no planner could. There is, too, a true distinction between rules that merely set up the game, like property and contract, and rules that reach in to fix its results, like a decreed price: the first leave the spontaneous order free to work, the second override it. All granted. But spontaneity is not naturalness, and the distinction does not rescue the picture of a natural market. The prices may be undesigned, yet the frame within which they form — what may be owned, which promises bind, what counts as money, who enforces it all — is designed, maintained, and fought over. That no hand directs the outcomes does not mean that no hand wrote the rules; it means only that the rules, once written, let the outcomes arrange themselves. The order is spontaneous within a frame that is made.

Grant the market's defenders their strongest point. No one plans the price of wheat; it emerges from millions of scattered choices, and the resulting order is genuinely no one's design — Hayek's great insight, and a true one. There is, besides, a real difference between rules that enable the game, like property and contract, and rules that dictate its outcomes, like a fixed price. But spontaneity is not naturalness. The prices may be undesigned, yet the framework in which they form — what may be owned, what promises bind, what serves as money — is made, and made by power. That no one directs the outcomes does not mean that no one wrote the rules.

What the plate yields, then, is less a doctrine than a question, to be carried to every market one meets. Not only: what are its prices, and how do they move? But: what rules constitute it, and in whose favor do they run? The market in labor is one thing where workers may lawfully combine and quite another where they may not; the market in credit one thing where interest is capped and another where it is loosed; the markets in land and water and data and human attention are shaped, at the root, by rules that most who trade in them never see and almost none have chosen. To ask after those rules is to see each market for the made and alterable thing it is, rather than the fixed arena it pretends to be. And it is to decline the standing invitation, folded into the very word free, to mistake a particular and partisan set of rules for no rules at all, and so to wave through unexamined the arrangement that the prevailing rules have built.

The upshot is a question to put to any market whatever: not only how its prices move, but what rules constitute it, and whom they serve. The labor market is one thing where workers may combine and another where they may not; the credit market one thing where usury is capped and another where it runs free; the markets in land, water, data, and human attention are shaped at the root by rules most participants never see and few ever chose. To ask after the rules is to refuse the invitation, built into the word free, to mistake the rules in force for no rules at all.

This is no merely philosophical nicety, for nearly everything that matters rides upon it. Because the market passes for natural, its rules sink beneath the surface at which a society argues. We contend without end over prices and wages and rates of interest — the visible outcomes — while the rules that generate them, the truest and deepest determinants of who shall have what, rest below the waterline, unexamined, mistaken for the permanent frame of the picture when they are in fact its most decisive strokes. A price is a verdict on a hidden contest, mistaken for a fact of nature; and the market's rules are that contest's standing terms — hidden best of all by being taken for no terms at all. To recognize the frame as made is to haul those rules up into the light, where they may be defended, contested, and changed. Which is precisely why those whom the present rules enrich labor so hard to have them seen as natural — and precisely why the plate that follows must ask, at last, who those people are.

Here is why the disguise matters so much. Because the market passes for natural, its rules slip beneath the level at which we argue. We quarrel without end over prices and wages and rates — the outcomes — while the rules that generate them, the deepest determinants of who gets what, sit unexamined, mistaken for the fixed frame of the picture rather than seen for what they are: its boldest strokes. To recognize the frame as made is to drag the rules into the light, where they can be questioned and changed — which is exactly why those whom the present rules favor are so eager to have them seen as natural.

There was never a state of nature to return to — only rules we have made, mistaken for the way things are, and waiting to be remade.

So the first plate of the volume lays the ground for all that follow. It has not yet asked who writes the rules, how property comes to be defined, or what binds the power that enforces them; those are the questions of the plates to come. It has established only the thing without which none of them can even be posed: that the market is not found but made, an artifact of rules and not a region of nature, so that there is no neutral ground beneath our arrangements to which we might appeal or return. The free market, conceived as a market free of rules, is not a country we have left and may go back to; it is a country that never existed. What exists is a structure of rules — property, contract, money, enforcement, and the thousand finer rules raised upon them — composing the arena in which every price is struck and every fortune made. And a structure of rules is a thing that was decided, and can be decided otherwise. The argument remains open.
