On the order of discovery

The Order of Discovery

A periodic table is arranged by logic, not by history. Mendeleev did not list the elements in the order they were found; he arranged them by their properties, so that the order on the page would reveal a structure in the world. This work has done the same. Its twenty-six elements are grouped by kind — prices, quantities, failures, institutions, aggregates, behaviors — and not one of the companions before this one has had a word to say about when any of them was discovered. The table is a snapshot of a finished science, with the scaffolding taken down.

But the ideas in it were not found all at once, nor in the order they are filed. They arrived over three centuries, made by particular people working on particular problems, through a sequence of upheavals each generation lived through as a revolution: the classical economists building the first system on Smith’s wealth of nations; the marginalists of the 1870s moving value from labor to desire; the neoclassicals assembling the supply-and-demand apparatus; Keynes inventing macroeconomics in the wreckage of the Depression; the postwar formalists turning all of it into mathematics; and the moderns taking up information, institutions, strategy, and the stubborn irrationality of the mind.

This companion restores that axis. Below, the key idea of each element is set into the history of economic thought, in seven ages from the first measurers before Adam Smith to the inequality economics of the present day. Every figure carries verified dates, a single sentence on what they added, and a link to the volume their idea became. Use the filter to light only the figures who built a chosen part of the table, and a second pattern surfaces beneath the first: the failures and the behaviors are largely a story of the last hundred years, while the prices and the quantities run back to the beginning. The order of discovery is not the order of the table — and the distance between them is its own kind of argument.

What the dates reveal

Logic and History

Two orders lie over the same twenty-six elements. There is the order of the table — logical, synchronic, the elements grouped by what they are — and the order of discovery — historical, diachronic, the elements found one after another by people who could not see the whole. The first is how the work has presented itself on every other page; the second is what this companion restores. Holding the two together is uncomfortable in a way worth sitting with. The table implies a settled structure, fixed and complete; the chronology shows a science still visibly in motion, its newest ages — behavior, information, inequality — barely a lifetime old, and plainly unfinished.

The history also refuses to be a clean march. After Keynes it stops being a relay race, one school handing off to the next, and becomes something closer to several programs running at once, often against each other: in the very same decades, one set of economists pushed rationality to its limit with rational expectations and efficient markets while another pulled it apart with prospect theory and the nudge. The arrangement into seven tidy ages is itself a simplification — a story imposed on a tangle, as any periodization must be. And it carries the standing temptation of all such histories: to read the past as a ladder built to reach the present, each thinker a rung beneath us, when in truth Smith and Ricardo and Marx were answering their own questions, not ours, and would scarcely recognize the table their ideas were eventually filed into.

Which is the last reason the table and the time-line need each other. Read only the table, and economics looks like a finished cathedral, every stone in its logical place. Read only the chronology, and it dissolves into a sequence of arguments with no structure at all. Both are true at once: there is a real order in the ideas, and that order was built, slowly and contentiously, by people in time. A periodic table without a history is a claim that the science is done; a history without a table is a claim that it is only one thing after another. The work has wanted, from its first page, to be neither.

Return to the atlas for the table these ideas became, the constellation for the bonds among them, the properties to weigh them by trait, the compounds for what they form together, the diagonals to read one idea across the table, the objections to hear the case against it, the laboratory to set the models moving, the preface for the argument entire, the guided tour for a curated path, the index to find any name, the measures for the numbers themselves, the cases to see the table at work, the priceless for where it stops, the gaps for what it never named, the first person for how it is lived now, the schools for the traditions side by side, the sources for the works themselves, or the unsettled for the contests still being fought. The table is timeless; the science is not. The argument, which is to say the history, remains open.
