On combination

When Two Elements Meet

The atlas isolates the elements; the constellation joins them; but neither says what the joining produces. In chemistry the answer is the whole of the science: two elements bond and a compound appears with properties belonging to neither — sodium burns, chlorine poisons, and together they season food. Economic life is the same. The wage is one thing and power another, but a wage under a single great employer is monopsony, a third thing, with a logic the wage alone does not have. A premium is the price of risk and an asymmetry is hidden knowledge, but a premium sold to people who know their own risks better than the insurer is adverse selection, the slow poisoning of a market. The elements are the alphabet; the compounds are the words.

This companion names them. Each of the sixty bonds the constellation draws is here given its compound — the specific relationship the two elements form, with the concept it is known by, the formula where one is clean, and a sentence on how the reaction runs. They are the familiar furniture of the science seen from a new angle: the monopoly markup is margin meeting power, the Pigouvian tax is the externality meeting the tax, present value is the rate meeting the stock, capital accumulation is growth meeting the stock, labor’s share is the share meeting the wage. Read this way, an economics that can look like a heap of disconnected models becomes a small number of elements reacting in lawful, repeatable ways.

It is built for wandering. Click any element in the table and it lights, together with every element it bonds with; below, each compound it forms is set out in full. Click a partner to swing the view to it and follow the reactions onward — from the margin to power, from power to the wage, from the wage to the share — or open either volume to read the element entire. The whole table of sixty can be unfolded at once for those who would rather see the reactions listed than discovered. However you move through it, the lesson is the same: the cells of the table are where we store the elements, but the compounds are where the economy actually happens.

What the compounds reveal

Everything Is a Compound

Set out together, the compounds make an argument the separate volumes cannot. They show that the great named results of economics — the ones with a person’s name attached, Ricardo’s rent and Coase’s theorem and Akerlof’s lemons and Ostrom’s commons — are not isolated discoveries but combinations of the same few elements, meeting under different conditions. Adverse selection and moral hazard are both the asymmetry, bonded once to the premium and once to the contract. The deadweight triangle of a tax and the deadweight triangle of a monopoly are the same surplus, lost to two different causes. Monopsony and rent-seeking are both power, turned toward the wage and toward the privilege. The economy does not have a hundred separate problems; it has a few elements, and the trouble is always in how they combine.

And this is the final form of the work’s argument. If every compound is a reaction between elements, then the pure element — the wage as such, the price as such — is something that exists only on the page. In the world there is no wage that is not already bent by some power, some norm, some asymmetry, some tax; no price that is not already a compound of the contests around it. The periodic table was never a claim that these things exist in isolation. It was a way of naming the elements so that their combinations could be understood — and the combinations, not the elements, are the economy we actually live in. The cells hold the alphabet still. The compounds are the only language anyone ever speaks.
