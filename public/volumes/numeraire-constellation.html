The Bonds Beneath the Table

The atlas lays the twenty-six elements out in rows and groups, each in its own cell, as a periodic table must. But that orderly separation is a convenience of presentation, not a fact about the subject. The elements of economic life are not isolates; they are bound to one another by the very logic that defines them. The marginal-cost curve of one volume is the supply curve of another. The adverse selection of one is the insurance market of a third. A tax is the externality’s remedy; a wage is the meeting of a labour market’s power; a growth rate is a stock of capital, accumulating. To learn any one element well is to find the others already inside it.

This companion draws those bonds. Each of the twenty-six volumes is a point; a thread joins two of them wherever the argument of one genuinely reaches into the other, and the heavier the thread, the deeper the dependence. The colours mark the six groups of the atlas — the prices, the quantities, the failures, the institutions, the aggregates, the behaviours — and the layout is found by letting the points repel and the threads pull until the whole settles into the shape its own connections dictate. No position is assigned; the figure arranges itself, and where it places a volume tells you how central its ideas are to the rest.

It is meant to be handled. Hover any volume and it lights, together with everything it touches, the rest of the web falling back; the line beneath the figure names its bonds in words. Click a volume to open it. Click a colour in the legend to lift a whole group out of the tangle. Drag a point and the web flexes and re-settles around it. What the atlas presents as a list, the constellation reveals as a single connected thing — which is what the argument was all along.

Nothing Stands Alone

Drawn this way, the argument confesses its shape. A few volumes sit near the centre, thick with threads — the margin, the price, the contest of power — because their logic is borrowed everywhere; the marginal principle alone reaches into supply, monopoly, wages, rent, and the division of the whole. Others sit nearer the edge, but none sits apart: every element, followed far enough, arrives at the others. The six groups cluster, as they should, yet the threads cross freely between them, because a failure is also a price and an institution is also a behaviour. The boundaries the atlas draws are real but porous.

This is the deepest claim of the work, made visible. The quantities of economic life are not separate facts to be looked up one at a time; they are a single fabric, and to pull on any one thread is to feel the others move. A wage cannot be understood without power, nor power without the game, nor the game without the norm, nor the norm without the wage that tests it. The table was only ever a way of holding the fabric still long enough to name its threads. Here it is, moving again.
