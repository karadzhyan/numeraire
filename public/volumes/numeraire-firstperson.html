On the voice the book has used

In the First Person

This entire book is written in the third person. It is the native voice of economics — the impersonal grammar in which the wage is, the cycle turns, the aggregate moves, the price clears, as though these were things that happened on their own, in a world with no one in it. The voice has its reasons; a science needs the distance. But the distance can come to seem like the whole of the truth, and it is not. Every verdict this book has anatomized — every price, every rate, every share — is, for the person who lives beneath it, not a structure but a morning; not a contest in the abstract but the contest their own day is made of.

So this companion does the one thing the rest of the book does not. It turns the table from the third person into the first. Beside each element’s cool verdict — the sentence the book itself would write — it sets the lived day: what the contest feels like from inside a single life, in the person who is actually having it. The two are the same phenomenon seen from two sides. The wage that the ledger calls a settled price is also the money that lands on a Friday and is gone by the next; the rate that the textbook calls the price of time is also the figure in the statement you read twice.

The danger here is sentiment, and the book has no more wish to manipulate you now than it had to bore you before. These are not sad stories; economic life is not only hardship, and the pages that follow hold as much of the morning coffee and the quiet relief as of the dread. They are only the part the third person leaves out — that the science is, the whole time, about someone. Read each day paired with its verdict, or strip the verdicts away and read only the days.

The Wage

In the third person

In the ledger, the wage is the price at which the long contest over the product of an hour’s labor is, for the moment, settled.

In the first person

The money lands on a Friday, and for an hour or two the account is a number you do not have to think about. You did the work; here is what the work was worth, or what it was priced at, which you have learned are not the same thing. There is a small pride in it, and beneath the pride a smaller arithmetic — the hours given against the sum returned — that you try not to run too often, because it does not come out the way the pride would like.

The Rent

In the third person

Rent is the payment extracted for access to something fixed — here, the right to occupy a space that someone else holds.

In the first person

It is the largest number of the month, and it leaves first, before the rest can be argued with. You are paying, you understand on the bad days, simply to be somewhere — to have a door that is yours to close — and the price of being somewhere has climbed faster than the wage that meets it. You run to stay in place. Most months you make it, and making it is its own quiet relief, the kind no one thinks to congratulate you for.

The Rate

In the third person

The rate is the price of time — the premium the present charges the future, set in rooms you will never enter.

In the first person

Somewhere a committee meets, and a number changes, and the cost of what you already owe moves with it while you sleep. You did not vote, were not asked, will not be told directly; you find out in the statement, in a figure a little larger than last month’s, and you read it twice. The debt is yours and the dial is theirs. You have made your peace with this the way one makes peace with the weather.

The Surplus

In the third person

Consumer surplus is the gap between what a thing is worth to you and the lesser sum you actually pay — the daily, unbilled gift of a working market.

In the first person

The coffee costs less than you would have given for it, which is a small marvel you long ago stopped noticing, and the phone in your pocket holds more than a whole room of machines once held, for the price of a few hours’ work. Most of what you buy is, in this quiet way, a bargain — the world handing you a little more than you pay for. It is worth saying, on the days when the larger numbers press, that the market is not only the thing that takes. Some mornings it simply hands you the coffee, cheap and hot, and asks for very little back.

The Margin

In the third person

At the margin is where the decision is actually made — the next hour, the next unit, the one whose worth is finely balanced.

In the first person

The shift is offered late, and you weigh it: the extra hours against the cost of getting there, against the evening you would otherwise have had. You are, in the firm’s arithmetic, the marginal worker — the one whose hour is barely worth buying, which is to say the one let go first when the buying stops. You feel the thinness of that margin without quite having the word for it. You take the shift. The math is close, and close, this month, is enough.

The Asymmetry

In the third person

An asymmetry of information is a contest in which one side can see the cards and the other cannot.

In the first person

You are signing something long, in a font built to be skimmed, and you sign it, because the alternative is not signing it and you need the thing it gives you. The other side wrote it; the other side knows what is in it; you know, roughly, that it favors the side that wrote it. Across the desk the salesman is pleasant, and knows the one fact about the car that you do not, and will not be the one to mention it. You are not a fool. You are simply on the side that cannot see the cards.

The Power

In the third person

Market power is the ability of one side to set the terms the other can only take or leave.

In the first person

There is one large employer where you live, or one platform that has the customers, or one company whose form you are required to use, and the terms arrive already written, with a place for your name at the bottom. Take it or leave it, the terms say, politely, and leaving it is not really on offer. You sign where the name goes. It is not that anyone is cruel; it is that the choosing was done before you arrived, and your part is only to consent to it, which the form is kind enough to call a choice.

The Money

In the third person

Money is the promise everyone agrees to accept — a claim on the work of others, made portable and put in your pocket.

In the first person

There is a stretch near the end of the month when you check the balance more often than the hour, and the figure has a weight to it, light or heavy, that colors the day before you have decided anything at all. With some in the account the world loosens; the small choices — the better bread, the cab in the rain — stop being choices and become simply what you do. With little, every small thing tightens back into a decision. The same coins, the same prices; only the figure in the account is different — and the figure in the account is, it turns out, most of how the day will feel.

The Share

In the third person

A share is a position in a distribution — not what you have, but where what you have places you among the rest.

In the first person

It is not the size of the thing but its size next to others that you feel; the raise that pleased you on Friday is smaller by Monday, when you learn what the next desk got. You do not, most days, measure yourself against the truly distant rich; you measure yourself against the people one rung up, whose lives are visible and almost yours. The gap between you and them is the one that stings, because it looks closeable, and is not. Knowing the comparison is foolish has never once stopped you making it.

The Bias

In the third person

A bias is a regularity in the way the mind departs from the cold optimum — not so much a failure as a feature, quietly costed.

In the first person

You keep the subscription you do not use, because cancelling feels like a loss, though the loss is already happening, monthly, in silence. You stay in the slower line because you have waited in it, and to leave now would waste the waiting, which is gone whether you stay or go. You buy the ticket whose odds you can recite, for a dollar’s worth of imagining a different life. None of this is stupidity. It is a mind built for a different world doing its honest best in this one, and being charged, in small sums, for the difference.

The Norm

In the third person

A norm is a rule no one wrote and everyone enforces — an equilibrium held in place by the cost of breaking it.

In the first person

You leave the tip though no one is watching and no law requires it, because not leaving it would sit wrong in you for the rest of the night. You bring the bottle to the dinner; you do not invoice the friend for the favor; you feel, when someone breaches one of these unwritten things, a small heat that is not quite anger and is enforced by no one but you. These are the prices the market never set and could not collect, and you pay them gladly, mostly, because they are the whole difference between a transaction and a life lived among others.

Why the person matters

Whose Life It Is

It is worth being plain, at the end, about why this companion exists, because it is not decoration. The book’s thesis is, at its root, a humane one. It says that the numbers which rule your life — what your hour is worth, what your shelter costs, what your debt charges, where your share places you — are not facts of nature, fixed and beyond appeal, but verdicts on contests that were fought, and could have gone otherwise. That is the whole argument, and it has been made, until now, entirely in the impersonal voice.

But a thesis about human lives that never shows a human life is only half made. The first person is not a softening of the analysis; it is its subject. Every contest the book anatomized is someone’s payday, someone’s last week of the month, someone’s signature at the bottom of a form they could not read. The third person is the language of the science, and it is the right language for the science. The first person is the language of the stakes — and the stakes are the reason the science is worth doing at all.

And the two voices, set side by side, say the thing the book has most wanted to say. To call the wage a verdict rather than a law is no abstraction when it is your wage; it is the difference between a number you must simply accept and a number you might yet contest. A law of nature cannot be argued with. A verdict can be appealed. The whole purpose of denaturalizing the price — of insisting, plate after plate, that it is a contest and not a fact — is that someone is living inside it, and what was made by a contest can be reopened by one. The argument remains open. It was always, in the end, an argument about lives.

The book is in the third person; the life is in the first. The argument remains open.
