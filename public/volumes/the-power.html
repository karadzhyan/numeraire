too few on one side

The question of the volume What is a fair price worth, when one side has nowhere else to go?

in print · twelve plates
Numéraire · Volume X

The Argument

Across the three volumes of this table that concern distribution, one word has done more work than any other and has never yet been made to account for itself. The wage, it was said, falls below the worker's product by the measure of the employer's power; the surplus is sized by efficiency but kept by power; the share is filled, in the degree of freedom technology leaves open, by power. Power was everywhere invoked and nowhere anatomized — named as the force that bends a price away from the cost that would justify it, but never examined as a thing with sources, a magnitude, and a price of its own. This volume is that examination. It asks what market power is, where it comes from, how it is measured, what it costs, why it is sometimes defended, and how — when a society chooses — it is restrained.

The definition is exact and, once seen, organizes everything that follows. A firm has market power when it can profitably set its price away from the price competition would impose — for a seller, above the marginal cost of what it sells; for a buyer, below the marginal value of what it buys. The competitive ideal of the textbooks, in which every actor is a price-taker confronting a price it cannot move, is the special case: the frictionless limit in which power is exactly zero. Power is the general case, present in degree almost everywhere, and the question is never simply whether a market has power but how much, on which side, and at what cost. The phrase this volume travels under names its structural essence — power is what arises when there are too few on one side of the market.

It wears two faces, and the burden of the volume is to show them one anatomy. The famous face is monopoly — too few sellers, the single seller in the limit — which restricts what it offers in order to lift the price above cost. The less famous face is its mirror, monopsony — too few buyers, the single buyer in the limit — which restricts the custom it gives in order to push the price below value. The Wage met one organ of this body already, in the single buyer of labor who pays the worker less than his marginal product; this volume shows that organ to be part of a whole. Buyer power and seller power are the same phenomenon seen from opposite ends of an exchange, governed by the same mathematics, sprung from the same barriers, and costing society in the same coin. Monopoly restricts the output it sells; monopsony restricts the custom it buys; both transfer surplus to the stronger side and destroy some of it at the margin.

What is a fair price worth, when one side has nowhere else to go? "Nowhere else to go" is the whole of power — the missing alternative, the absent substitute, the outside option that the earlier volumes turned upon.

The animating question sets the matter in the register of the table entire: what is a fair price worth, when one side has nowhere else to go? For "nowhere else to go" is the heart of the thing — the absence of an alternative, the want of a substitute, the missing outside option that the bargaining theory of the earlier volumes made decisive. A buyer with somewhere else to go disciplines the seller; a worker with somewhere else to go disciplines the employer; a price is held to cost only by the credible possibility of walking away. Power is what remains when that possibility is gone, and the measure of power, the plates will show, is exactly the measure of how few the alternatives have become.

The plates trace the anatomy from definition through remedy. They begin with the price-maker and the diagram of monopoly, and with the measure of power — the markup, which proves to be the simple inverse of the elasticity of the demand a firm faces, so that power is precisely the scarcity of alternatives made quantitative. They turn to the sources, the barriers to entry from scale economies and network effects to patents and the control of essential things, for power without a barrier is power that competition dissolves. They set the real world on the spectrum between the competitive and monopoly poles, where monopolistic competition and oligopoly live, and confront the strategic heart of it — the game a few firms play against one another, in which collusion beckons and cheating tempts. They examine power's uses, the leveraging of it from one market into the next, and its mirror in the buyer, the monopsony that makes The Wage general. Then the reckoning of what power costs a society — the deadweight triangle, the rent-seeking rectangle, the slack of the unpressured firm. Then the great defense, Schumpeter's claim that the prospect of monopoly is the engine of progress, and the inverted-U in which the claim is half-tamed. And finally the remedy: the law of antitrust, its capture by the doctrine of consumer welfare, its present contest over the power of platforms — and the synthesis, in which power stands revealed as the structure beneath the whole distributional triangle, and competition as the exception it was always taught to be the rule.

The thesis may be stated plainly, for the plates to earn. Market power is not an anomaly that mars an otherwise competitive economy; it is the ordinary condition of which perfect competition is the rare and frictionless limit, and the price that bears its mark — above cost where the seller has power, below value where the buyer does — is the clearest verdict in the whole table on a contest the absence of alternatives has already decided. To call such a price fair because it is what the market yielded is to mistake the outcome of power for the judgment of competition. What power is worth, the party with nowhere else to go pays; and what it costs, the whole of a society does.

Plate the First · The Failures · № X

The Maker

the price-maker, and the diagram of monopoly

The plate asks what divides a firm that takes the price from one that makes it?

The whole of market power is contained in a single difference between two demand curves. In perfect competition, the firm is so small against the market that it faces a flat, horizontal demand: it may sell all it can produce at the going price and not a penny above, for the smallest premium would send every customer to an identical rival. Such a firm takes the price; it is handed to it by the market, and in equilibrium it equals the marginal cost of the last unit made — the equality, P equals MC, that is the signature of efficiency and the reason the competitive ideal is so cherished. A firm with market power faces, instead, a demand curve that slopes downward — in the limit of pure monopoly, the entire market's own demand — so that to sell more it must lower its price, and to charge more it loses some custom but not all. Such a firm does not take the price; it makes it. It chooses. That choice is power, and the downward slope of the demand it faces is power's entire origin.

From the downward slope the rest follows by an iron logic, drawn in the figure. Because selling one more unit forces the price down on every unit already sold, the extra revenue from that unit — its marginal revenue — is less than its price, and the marginal-revenue curve lies below the demand curve. The firm, seeking the greatest profit, pushes output only to the point where the marginal revenue of the last unit just covers its marginal cost; beyond that, each further unit would add more to cost than to revenue. That point lies at a quantity short of the competitive one — where price, not marginal revenue, equals marginal cost — and at a price above marginal cost. The monopolist, in a phrase, restricts output to raise the price. And the restriction has two consequences the figure separates: a rectangle of surplus transferred from buyers to the firm, the profit that power extracts; and a triangle of surplus simply destroyed — the deadweight loss, the gains from trade on the units never made, the exchanges that would have benefited both buyer and seller but for the price held high to protect the rest.

A price-taker accepts the verdict of the market; a price-maker writes it.

The signature of power, then, is the inequality the competitive ideal forbids: price greater than marginal cost. Where a market is competitive, the price a buyer pays equals the cost of making the last unit, so nothing is extracted and nothing is wasted; where there is power, the price exceeds that cost, and the gap measures at once the extraction and the inefficiency, the transfer and the loss. This single inequality, P greater than MC, is the deviation from the competitive benchmark that the whole of this volume sets out to measure, to source, to cost, and to remedy. And it is already tied to the question the volume travels under, for the monopolist's power to hold price above cost rests entirely on the buyer's having nowhere else to go — no substitute to flee to, no rival selling the same thing for less. The steeper and less yielding the demand the firm faces, the further above cost it may set its price; and how far, exactly, is the burden of the next plate.

the edge The pure monopoly of this diagram — one seller, a single homogeneous product, a demand curve known with certainty, no threat of entry — is as much an idealization as the perfect competition it is contrasted with, and the two are the poles between which all real markets fall. Almost no firm is a pure price-taker, for even the wheat farmer meets some friction; and almost none a pure monopolist, for even the holder of a patent faces imperfect substitutes and the ever-present possibility of a rival. The diagram earns its place not as a portrait of any actual market but as the benchmark of pure power, against which the gradations of the real — monopolistic competition, oligopoly, the contestable market — can be read. That price exceeds marginal cost is the mark of power; how far above, and whether the gap reflects scarcity or merely the recovery of fixed costs, the plates that follow must decide.

Plate the Second · The Failures · № X

The Markup

measuring power; the Lerner index and the inverse of elasticity

The plate asks if power is the gap between price and cost, how wide is it — and what sets the width?

If the signature of power is that price exceeds marginal cost, the natural measure of power is the size of the excess — the markup. Abba Lerner, in 1934, made the measure precise and gave it his name: the Lerner index, the difference between price and marginal cost expressed as a fraction of the price. It runs from zero, where price equals marginal cost and there is no power at all, toward one, as marginal cost shrinks to nothing beside the price. It is the single cleanest number for the degree of market power a firm possesses, and it is exactly the markup that The Share watched rising across the economy, the degree of monopoly that Kalecki made the determinant of the wage share. To measure power is, in the first place, to measure how far above cost the price is held.

And here the measure yields a result of startling economy, which ties the whole volume to its animating question. The profit-maximizing markup, Lerner showed, equals the inverse of the elasticity of the demand the firm faces. The reasoning is brief: a firm's marginal revenue is its price reduced in proportion to the elasticity of its demand, so setting marginal revenue equal to marginal cost and rearranging leaves the markup as one divided by that elasticity. Power is therefore, precisely and quantitatively, the inverse of the elasticity of demand — which is to say the scarcity of the buyer's alternatives, made into a number. A firm whose customers have many close substitutes faces highly elastic demand, for they will flee at the smallest premium, and so can mark up almost nothing: it has little power. A firm whose customers have few substitutes faces inelastic demand, for they have nowhere else to go, and so may mark up a great deal: it has much power. The figure is the whole of it. "Nowhere else to go," the phrase of the volume's question, is not a metaphor but the very quantity that sets the markup: the less elastic the demand, the fewer the alternatives, the greater the power, the wider the gap between price and cost.

Power is the inverse of the elasticity of demand — the scarcity of alternatives, made into a number.

This single relation unifies the threads the table has been spinning. The markup is the Lerner index is the inverse of the elasticity is the measure of how captive the buyer is — and the same logic, turned to the buying side, gives the monopsonist's markdown as the inverse of the elasticity of supply, the measure of how captive the seller is, which is the wage-below-product of The Wage's eighth plate and the subject of this volume's seventh. Power, on either side of any exchange, is the inverse of the other party's elasticity — the inverse of how readily they can walk away. What sets that elasticity, in turn, is the availability of substitutes and the freedom of entry, which the structure and barriers of the next plates determine. The markup measures power; the elasticity explains it; the barriers produce it.

the edge The Lerner index is the textbook measure and a treacherous one in practice, for marginal cost — the very thing it sets price against — is seldom observed, and must be inferred from contestable assumptions about technology and cost, so that estimated markups, and the claim that they have risen, inherit all the uncertainty of those assumptions, as The Share's plate on the superstar firm conceded. And the markup measures power's result, not always its source or its harm: a firm with large fixed costs and low marginal costs — much of the modern economy — must price above marginal cost merely to break even, with no harmful power at all, so that a high markup may signal genuine scarcity, or fleeting advantage, or only the ordinary recovery of fixed costs under competition. Power leaves its mark on the markup; but not every markup is the mark of power, and the index must be read alongside the structure and the barriers, never alone.

Plate the Third · The Failures · № X

The Barrier

the sources of power; entry, scale, networks, patents

The plate asks power that competition would erode is no power at all — so what holds the rivals off?

A price held above cost is an advertisement. It announces profit, and profit summons entrants, who shave the price to win custom until it falls back to cost and the power is gone. It follows that market power, to last, requires something that keeps the advertisement from being answered — a barrier to entry, a reason the rivals do not come or the substitutes do not appear. Without such a barrier, any power is fleeting, competed away as fast as it arises; with one, the price can rest above cost indefinitely. The sources of durable power are therefore the sources of barriers, and they are worth cataloguing, for they are the organs by which the body of power is fed.

The deepest barrier is economies of scale, drawn in the figure as the natural monopoly. When the average cost of production falls across the whole reach of the market — because the fixed costs are vast and the cost of each further unit small, as in a railway, a power grid, a pipeline, or a piece of software written once and copied for nothing — then a single firm supplies the entire demand more cheaply than two could, for splitting the output would send each producer back up its falling cost curve. Here competition is not merely kept out but is positively wasteful, and the barrier is inseparable from a genuine efficiency, which is why the remedy for such a monopoly is not to break it up but to regulate it — a tension the tenth plate takes up. Beside scale stand the other classical barriers: the control of an essential input or facility that rivals must have and cannot obtain; the patent and the copyright, which are monopolies the state itself grants, by deliberate design, to reward invention; and the brand built by sunk advertising, which makes a product non-substitutable in the buyer's mind even where a chemical twin sells for less.

The modern age has raised one barrier above all the others: the network effect, where a product grows more valuable as more people use it — the telephone, the marketplace, the social network, the payment system. A large incumbent then offers something a small entrant simply cannot, an existing crowd, and users will not switch to a better-engineered rival that no one else has joined; the barrier is the incumbent's own scale, self-reinforcing and nearly insurmountable, and it is the subject of this volume's eleventh plate. To it one may add the switching cost, which holds a customer captive not by love but by the expense of leaving — the migrated data, the learned interface, the locked contract — and the strategic barriers a firm erects by its own conduct, the excess capacity held ready to flood a market upon entry, the price cut below cost to drive a rival out and warn the next away.

A supra-competitive price is an advertisement for entry; power is whatever keeps the advertisement from being answered.

A crucial refinement, owed to William Baumol and his colleagues in the theory of contestable markets, corrects the instinct to count firms. What disciplines a price, they argued, is not the number of incumbents but the freedom of entry and exit. If entry were perfectly free and exit perfectly costless — if a firm could swoop in, undercut, profit, and depart before the incumbent could react — then even a single seller would be forced to price at cost, for any premium would invite such hit-and-run entry. The barrier that confers lasting power is therefore not concentration as such but the presence of sunk costs, the costs an entrant cannot recover if it leaves, which make hit-and-run impossible and entry a gamble. This sharpens the whole inquiry to its proper variable. A market with one firm and no sunk costs may be competitive; a market with several firms behind high sunk costs may not. Power lives not in the count of sellers but in the height of the wall around them.

the edge Which features truly constitute barriers to entry, and which are merely the ordinary costs every firm including the incumbent once bore, is among the oldest disputes in the field. Bain counted economies of scale and capital requirements as barriers; Stigler and the Chicago school insisted that a genuine barrier is only a cost the entrant must bear that the incumbent escaped, and on that narrower definition concluded that many apparent barriers are nothing of the kind. The quarrel is not pedantry: it decides whether a concentrated, profitable industry betrays entrenched power or merely houses efficient incumbents that no entrant can match — and so whether the law should move. Contestability theory pointed to the right variable in sunk costs; but measuring contestability in a living market, and telling a barrier from an efficiency, remains as much judgment as science.

Plate the Fourth · The Failures · № X

The Spectrum

between the poles; structure, concentration, and conduct

The plate asks between the one and the many, where do real markets fall — and does counting the firms tell us their power?

Perfect competition and pure monopoly are the poles of a spectrum, and almost every real market lies somewhere along it. Two intermediate structures deserve names. The first, described independently by Edward Chamberlin and Joan Robinson in the same year, 1933, is monopolistic competition: many firms, each selling a slightly differentiated product — a restaurant, a brand of coffee, a neighborhood shop — so that each faces a gently downward-sloping demand and holds a sliver of power, able to charge a little above cost because its product is no one's perfect substitute. Yet entry into such a market is free, and entry competes away the profit that the sliver of power would otherwise yield.

The figure shows the long-run resting place. Entry continues until the typical firm's demand curve is merely tangent to its average-cost curve, touching at a single point and lying below it everywhere else — which means the firm earns exactly zero economic profit, its price just covering its average cost. But the tangency falls on the falling part of the cost curve, to the left of the lowest-cost scale, and this has two consequences that define the structure. The price still exceeds marginal cost, so a residue of allocative inefficiency remains even with profits competed away; and the firm produces less than the quantity that would minimize its average cost, leaving "excess capacity" — the unused scale that is the standing price of variety, the efficiency a society forgoes in exchange for the differentiation it enjoys. Whether that exchange is worth it — whether we are over-supplied with nearly identical brands at a small cost in efficiency, or properly supplied with the variety we value — is a question the model poses sharply and does not answer.

The second intermediate structure is oligopoly, the market of a few, where each firm is large enough that its choices move the market and it must therefore anticipate the reactions of its rivals — the strategic interdependence that is the subject of the next plate. Between monopolistic competition's crowd and oligopoly's handful, the mid-century economists of the Harvard school, Edward Mason and Joe Bain, drew a paradigm to organize the whole field: structure shapes conduct, and conduct determines performance. The concentration of a market and its barriers (structure) would govern how firms priced and whether they colluded (conduct), which would fix their profits and efficiency (performance). To make structure measurable they offered the concentration ratio, the combined share of the largest few firms, and later the more refined Herfindahl–Hirschman index, the sum of the squared market shares, which weights the giants heavily and survives today in the thresholds of merger control.

Counting the firms measures the market's shape, not its power; a crowd behind a wall has less freedom than two in an open field.

But the structure-conduct-performance paradigm carried a hidden assumption that the Chicago school exposed and that the previous plate already undermined: that concentration implies power. Harold Demsetz and others objected that a concentrated, profitable industry need not be the symptom of power at all — it may be the reward of efficiency, for the best firms grow large and earn high returns precisely because they are better, and the very correlation between concentration and profit that the Harvard school read as power is equally consistent with the dominance of superior firms. The objection broke the simple inference. One cannot read power off a concentration ratio, because the same ratio may describe a cosy cartel sheltered behind barriers or a contest won by merit in an open field. What distinguishes them is not the count of firms but the height of the wall — the barriers of the third plate — and the conduct of the firms within, which only the game theory of the next plate can model. Concentration is a clue to power, never a verdict.

the edge The relationship between concentration and market power is real but loose, and the long quarrel between the structural reading of the Harvard school and the efficiency reading of Chicago has never been fully settled — high concentration is consistent both with entrenched power and with the dominance of genuinely superior firms, and the same index can describe a cartel or a meritocracy. Modern industrial organization largely abandoned the hunt for universal structure-performance laws in favor of the close study of conduct in particular markets; yet the recent revival of concern over economy-wide concentration and markups, in The Share's ninth plate, has reopened the old structural questions. The truth, as so often in this table, is that structure matters but does not by itself decide: power must be read from barriers and conduct together, with concentration a clue and not a conclusion.

Plate the Fifth · The Failures · № X

The Game

oligopoly, the strategy of the few, and the temptation to collude

The plate asks when a few firms each watch the others, what price emerges from the watching?

Between the crowd and the single seller lies the market of a few, and it is the hardest of all to read, because each firm is now large enough that its own choices move the market and must therefore reckon with how the others will respond. This is strategy in the exact sense — the outcome for each depends on the choices of all — and its proper language is the theory of games, to which the table devotes a later element. The oligopolist cannot simply read its optimum off a demand curve, as the monopolist does, for the demand it faces depends on what its rivals do, which depends on what they expect it to do. The price that emerges from this mutual watching is the subject of the oldest and still the most instructive models in the field.

Augustin Cournot, writing in 1838 with a mathematical sophistication a full century ahead of his readers, gave the first answer. Suppose the firms choose quantities, each taking its rivals' output as given and producing its own best response; then each firm's optimal output is a declining function of the others', the reaction curves of the figure, and the market settles where these curves intersect — a point at which every firm is doing the best it can given what the others do, and none wishes to change. This is the Nash equilibrium, discovered by Cournot more than a century before John Nash gave it a general theory, and its location is the most important fact about the market of a few: it lies between monopoly and competition, more output and a lower price than a single seller would set, less output and a higher price than perfect competition would yield. And it moves: as the number of firms grows, the intersection slides toward the competitive corner, price approaching marginal cost; as it shrinks toward one, toward monopoly. Power, in the Cournot world, is a smooth function of the number of competitors.

Joseph Bertrand, reviewing Cournot in 1883, objected that firms set prices, not quantities, and that the change wrecks the result. If two firms sell an identical product at constant cost and compete in price, then each can seize the entire market by undercutting the other by a penny, and the undercutting cannot stop until price equals marginal cost — so that two firms alone suffice to reproduce the competitive outcome. This is the Bertrand paradox, and it is troubling precisely because the world does not behave so: real duopolies do not generally price at cost. The resolution is that the mode of competition matters as much as the number of competitors. Let the firms first choose capacity and then compete in price, and the outcome reverts to Cournot's; let their products differ even slightly, and undercutting no longer captures the whole market, so price floats back above cost. The lesson is not that one model is right but that the price the few will set depends on the fine grain of how they compete — quantities or prices, once or repeatedly, with capacities fixed or free.

Collusion is what the few would choose together and each would betray alone — the prisoner's dilemma wearing a price tag.

The deepest feature of the market of a few is the standing temptation to stop competing altogether. The firms would jointly do best by colluding — restricting their combined output to the monopoly level and dividing the monopoly profit between them, the interior point in the figure. But that point is not an equilibrium of a single encounter, for each firm, taking the others' restraint as given, can profit by quietly expanding, and if all reason alike the restraint collapses and output swells back to the competitive level. Collusion is a prisoner's dilemma: collectively rational, individually unstable, betrayed by the very logic that recommends it. What rescues it, in the real economy, is repetition. When firms meet in the same market period after period, collusion can be sustained without a word of agreement, held together by the threat of future retaliation — the tacit understanding that to cheat today is to invite a price war tomorrow. The folk theorem of repeated games confirms that if firms value the future enough, a whole range of outcomes up to full monopoly can be quietly maintained. This is why cartels form and why they crack, why antitrust watches for the parallel pricing and price leadership that signal coordination without contract, and why the number of firms, the frequency of their dealings, and the visibility of their prices all bear on how much power the few can jointly wield.

the edge Oligopoly theory is a menu, not a law: Cournot, Bertrand, and their many descendants yield sharply different predictions from slightly different assumptions about what firms choose, whether they move together or in turn, and whether they meet once or forever — and no single model is the theory of oligopoly. The indeterminacy is not a defect but a truth about the world, for the price a few firms set genuinely depends on the structure of their interaction, which is why the same number of rivals can produce cutthroat competition in one industry and cosy coordination in another. Predicting the conduct of the few — the central task of antitrust in concentrated markets — is for this reason irreducibly hard, and the honest analyst offers a range of possibilities where the monopoly and competitive models each offered a point.

Plate the Sixth · The Failures · № X

The Leverage

extending power; tying, bundling, and foreclosure

The plate asks can a firm with power in one market reach into the next — and should the law let it?

Power does not stay where it is born. A firm secure in one market is forever tempted to use that security to win an adjacent one — to leverage the position it holds into a position it covets. The instruments are familiar. There is tying, the requirement that a buyer take a second product to obtain the first; bundling, the sale of two goods only together; exclusive dealing, the contract forbidding a distributor to carry a rival; and, most consequential in the modern economy, vertical foreclosure, in which a firm controlling an essential input, channel, or platform denies its rivals access, or degrades it, to disadvantage them in a market where it also competes. In each, the lever is the power already held, and the prize is power in the market next door. The historic instances are landmarks of the law: the tying of punch cards to tabulating machines, and, most famously, a software giant's binding of its web browser to the operating system that nearly every computer required, the better to foreclose the rival browser that might one day have grown into a platform of its own.

Against the instinct that such leverage is plainly anticompetitive, the Chicago school raised an argument of real power, the theorem of the single monopoly profit. A firm with one monopoly, it observed, can extract its monopoly profit only once; it cannot multiply that profit by reaching into a second, competitive market, because it already captures the available surplus through the price of the monopoly good itself. Tie a second product to the first, and the monopolist does not enlarge its take but merely relocates it — so tying and bundling, far from being engines of conquest, must be sought for their efficiencies: the cost savings of selling together, the assurance of quality, the quiet practice of price discrimination. On this reasoning the law should presume such conduct benign, and for a generation it largely did, softening the old per se prohibition of tying into a permissive rule of reason.

Power, like water, seeks the next room; the question is whether the law builds the wall or removes it.

The theorem was an insight, and it disciplined a great deal of loose talk about leverage. But it holds only within walls the real economy does not respect. Its logic assumes that the second market is genuinely competitive and unprotected, that the goods are consumed in fixed proportions, and — crucially — that the first monopoly faces no threat from the second. Lift any of these and the conclusion reverses. When the adjacent market has scale economies or network effects of its own, foreclosing a rival there can defend or extend the original monopoly, for the rival, left to grow, might have become a platform that eroded it; the leverage is then not a relocation of profit but an insurance of power, and Michael Whinston showed formally that tying can be a profitable strategy precisely when it deters the entry that would otherwise threaten the monopolist's home market. This is the foreclosure concern that the Chicago argument had prematurely buried, and it has returned in its sharpest modern form as the question of platform self-preferencing — the marketplace that ranks its own goods above its rivals', the search engine that favors its own services — which the eleventh plate takes up as the central battleground of present competition policy. The doctrine has swung with the argument: from the per se illegality of the mid-century, through the Chicago-inspired permissiveness, to a renewed and unsettled scrutiny as the platforms revive the old fear.

the edge Whether a given tie, bundle, or foreclosure entrenches power or merely delivers an efficiency is among the genuinely hard questions of competition policy, for the same conduct — a platform absorbing a new feature, a firm bundling complements — can be the natural shape of a useful innovation or the instrument of exclusion, often indistinguishable from outside and even to the firm itself. The Chicago theorem was a true correction to loose leverage theories; the limits of the theorem were a true correction to Chicago; and what remains is not a rule but a balance struck case by case, between the efficiencies that integration offers and the rivalry it forecloses — the balance on which, as the platform plate will show, the largest cases of the age now turn.

Plate the Seventh · The Failures · № X

The Buyer

the mirror; monopsony and buyer power, The Wage made general

The plate asks the seller who restricts what it sells has a mirror — the buyer who restricts what it buys; are they not the same power?

Every exchange has two sides, and so does power. The volume has so far watched the seller — the firm that restricts what it offers to lift the price above cost. Its mirror is the buyer that restricts what it purchases to push the price below value: the monopsonist, the single buyer in the limit, of whom The Wage met one organ in the single employer of labor. That plate showed an employer paying its workers less than their marginal product; this plate shows that organ to belong to a whole body, for the same power operates wherever a dominant buyer faces sellers who have nowhere else to take their wares.

The mechanics are the monopoly diagram reflected. A buyer facing an upward-sloping supply curve knows that to purchase one more unit it must raise the price on every unit it already buys, so the true marginal cost of the input — the marginal factor cost — lies above the supply curve, just as the monopolist's marginal revenue lay below demand. The buyer purchases only up to the point where this marginal factor cost equals the marginal value of the input to it, a quantity short of the competitive one, and pays the price read off the supply curve at that quantity — a price below the input's marginal value. The gap between value and price paid is the markdown, the precise mirror of the monopolist's markup, and by the logic of the second plate it equals the inverse of the elasticity of supply, the measure of how few alternatives the seller has, as the markup equalled the inverse of the elasticity of demand. Monopoly restricts what it sells; monopsony restricts what it buys; both transfer surplus to the powerful side and destroy some at the margin; and both are governed by a single equation read from opposite ends.

Monopoly restricts what it sells; monopsony restricts what it buys. They are one power, read from the two ends of an exchange.

The body of buyer power is as large as the body of seller power, and was long the more neglected. In the labor market — the case The Wage anatomized — it reaches far beyond the company town that first defined it: the concentration of employers in a local market, the occupational license that bars entry to a trade, the non-compete clause that chains a worker to a firm, the no-poach agreement by which employers quietly promise not to hire one another's people, each confers the power to hold wages below the worker's product. In the supply chain it is the dominant retailer squeezing its manufacturers, the processor dictating terms to the farmers who must sell to it, the platform setting the rate it will pay the drivers and merchants and makers who depend upon it. And it carries a complication seller power does not, which Galbraith named countervailing power: a mighty buyer that beats down a mighty seller may pass the savings to consumers as lower prices, so that buyer power can shield the public even as it squeezes the supplier. The antitrust authorities, having focused for decades on seller power and the price the consumer pays, have only lately turned to the buyer — prosecuting the no-poach agreement, weighing the effect of mergers on workers and suppliers, moving against the non-compete — and the recognition that the single-buyer model describes far more of the labor market than the company town it was built for has made buyer power one of the liveliest frontiers in the field.

the edge Buyer power is the true mirror of seller power in its mechanics, but its welfare verdict is the less straightforward, for a powerful buyer that squeezes its suppliers may hand the savings to consumers as lower prices — Galbraith's countervailing power — so that the same power harms one party while it benefits another, and the law must ask not merely whether power exists but whose surplus it takes and whether anyone's it returns. In the labor market the verdict is clearer, since the worker held below his product has no consumer to receive the gain but the employer's owners; and it is there, in the revival of monopsony as a theory of wages, that the mirror of power has lately drawn the most light.
viii

Plate the Eighth · The Failures · № X

The Cost

what power costs; the triangle, the rectangle, the slack

The plate asks monopoly transfers, and monopoly wastes — but how much does it truly cost a society?

That power transfers surplus from the weak side to the strong is plain from the first plate; the harder question is how much it costs society as a whole — not who gains and who loses, but what is destroyed in the destroying. The textbook answer is the deadweight loss: the triangle, in the monopoly diagram, between the demand curve and the cost curve, spanning the units the monopolist declines to make. These are exchanges that would have benefited both buyer and seller, foregone because the firm holds output back to protect the price on the rest — gains from trade simply annihilated, belonging to no one. This allocative loss is the canonical cost of monopoly, and for a long while it was thought to be the whole of it.

Arnold Harberger put a number to the triangle in 1954, and the number was startlingly small. Measuring the deadweight losses across American manufacturing, he found them on the order of a tenth of one percent of national income — because the markups he observed, and the demand elasticities they implied, were modest, and the area of a triangle shrinks with the square of its small sides. The implication was reassuring and became a pillar of the case against vigorous antitrust: if monopoly costs a society so little, the apparatus of competition policy was a remedy in search of a disease. But the Harberger triangle measures only the allocative loss, and two later arguments found the cost of power to lie mostly elsewhere, in places his triangle could not see.

The first is rent-seeking, named by Gordon Tullock and pressed by Richard Posner. The rectangle of monopoly profit — the markup times the quantity, the surplus transferred from buyers to the firm — is conventionally not counted as a social loss at all, for it is a mere transfer, a movement of surplus from one pocket to another. But this overlooks the contest to possess it. If the monopoly rent is a prize, firms will spend real resources to win and to keep it: lobbying legislatures for protective regulation, litigating to exclude rivals, building excess capacity to threaten entrants, pouring money into advertising whose only function is to fortify position. To the extent the rectangle is competed for, it is dissipated — the resources poured into the fight produce nothing, and a transfer becomes a waste. Since the rectangle is typically far larger than the triangle, the social cost of monopoly, properly counted, may be an order of magnitude greater than Harberger found. The second argument is Harvey Leibenstein's X-inefficiency. A firm sheltered from competition lacks the discipline that forces costs to their minimum, and so runs slack — the padded payroll, the unpressed supplier, the comfortable routine, the quiet life that an unchallenged monopoly can afford. The cost of power is then not only that the firm makes too little, but that it makes it badly, at a cost above the necessary; and Leibenstein suspected this productive waste might dwarf the allocative triangle too.

Harberger measured the triangle and found monopoly cheap; Tullock and Leibenstein found the rectangle and the slack, and found it dear.

The modern verdict has swung back toward the cost being large. As The Share's ninth plate recorded, markups across the economy appear to have risen substantially since 1980; and a bigger markup means a bigger triangle, a bigger rectangle to be dissipated, and more slack to hide behind. Economists now study the macroeconomic shadow of rising market power — output and investment held down, the labor share eroded, business dynamism and the founding of new firms in decline, inequality widened — and few now regard the cost of power as the trifle Harberger's triangle suggested. The full reckoning has three layers: the allocative loss of the triangle, modest and measurable; the rent-seeking dissipation of the rectangle, large and conjectural; and the productive slack of the unpressured firm, larger still and harder to see — to which the dynamic and distributional consequences must be added. Power is not cheap. It only looked cheap when only the smallest part of its cost was being measured.

the edge Each layer of the cost is harder to measure than the last, and the estimates range across orders of magnitude: the allocative triangle is the most tractable and the smallest, the rent-seeking rectangle and the X-inefficiency the largest and the most conjectural, for one cannot readily observe how much of a monopoly's profit was burned in seeking it, or how much waste hides inside its costs. The honest range is wide, and where an economist has landed within it has long tracked his prior — Harberger and the Chicago school, doubting power was a serious problem, measured the small triangle; the critics, suspecting it was, found the large rectangle and the larger slack. What the recent rise in markups has changed is the burden of proof: if power has grown, even the conservative triangle is no longer negligible, and the conjectural costs, whatever their true size, are growing with it.

Plate the Ninth · The Failures · № X

The Engine

the defense; Schumpeter, innovation, and the inverted-U

The plate asks is monopoly only a cost — or also the prize that pays for progress?

Every cost the previous plate tallied is a static cost — measured at a frozen instant, with technology given and the only question how the fixed pie is priced and divided. The most powerful defense of market power denies that this is the question that matters. Joseph Schumpeter, in 1942, argued that the essential fact about capitalism is not the pricing of existing goods but the perennial gale of creative destruction — the ceaseless innovation that destroys old products, methods, and firms and throws up new ones — and that the competition which counts is not the textbook jostling at the margin of price but the competition of the new commodity, the new technology, the new form of organization, which strikes not at the profits of incumbents but at their foundations and their lives. And the engine of that gale, Schumpeter insisted, is the prospect of monopoly. The entrepreneur innovates in hope of the temporary monopoly that success confers; the rent is the prize, and without the prize the incentive to innovate withers. On this view the static deadweight triangle is a rounding error beside the dynamic gains from the innovation that the hope of monopoly calls forth — and the law itself ratifies the bargain in the patent, a monopoly the state grants by deliberate design, trading the static cost of monopoly pricing for the dynamic gain of invention rewarded.

The defense met a formal rebuttal from Kenneth Arrow, who in 1962 argued that Schumpeter had the incentive backwards. Consider who gains more from a given innovation: a monopolist, or a firm in a competitive market. When a monopolist innovates, it partly cannibalizes the profit it already earns — it replaces itself, so its net gain is only the increment of the new over the old. A competitive firm or a fresh entrant, having no existing rents to lose, captures the whole of the new market, and so has the larger incentive to innovate. By this replacement effect, monopoly should retard innovation, not drive it: the secure incumbent rests in Hicks's "quiet life," while the hungry challenger races ahead. Arrow's logic and Schumpeter's point in opposite directions, and for decades the evidence seemed to support now one, now the other.

Schumpeter said monopoly is the prize that pays for progress; Arrow said the monopolist, holding the prize, stops running. The inverted-U says both are right, and the truth is in the middle.

The reconciliation, drawn in the figure, came from Philippe Aghion and his colleagues in 2005: the relationship between competition and innovation is an inverted-U. Where competition is faint and monopoly entrenched, innovation is low — Arrow's replacement effect and the quiet life, little pressure and much to cannibalize. Where competition is fierce and rents are instantly competed away, innovation is low again — Schumpeter's point, for there is no prize to reward the effort. Innovation is greatest at intermediate competition, where firms running neck-and-neck innovate hardest to escape their rivalry and seize a temporary lead — the escape-competition effect. Schumpeter and Arrow, it turns out, each described one arm of a single curve. And the lesson for the law is double-edged: a degree of market power, the hope of temporary rents, is indeed necessary to the innovation on which long-run welfare most depends — but too much power is as deadening as too little, and the entrenched, unchallengeable monopoly sits on the wrong, low slope of the hill. The innovation Schumpeter prized issues from the contest for power, not from its secure possession.

the edge The dynamic case is the strongest argument the defenders of power possess, and it carries a real truth: the prospect of rents motivates the innovation that dominates long-run welfare, so the static triangle may indeed be trivial beside the dynamic gains. But the case is routinely overstated into the claim that more power is always better, which the inverted-U denies — entrenched monopoly is as hostile to innovation as cutthroat competition, and the replacement effect explains why. The defense justifies the temporary monopoly of the successful innovator, and the patent that rewards invention for a term; it does not justify the permanent monopoly that uses its position to foreclose the next innovator. Telling the prize that pays for progress from the fortress that blocks it is, yet again, the unfinished work of the plates that follow.

Plate the Tenth · The Failures · № X

The Trust

the remedy; antitrust and the consumer-welfare standard

The plate asks if power is a failure, what is the cure — and has the cure mistaken the disease?

A society that judges market power a failure must decide what to do about it, and the instrument it has built is the law of antitrust — competition law, in the European phrase. Its founding text is the American Sherman Act of 1890, a child of the Gilded Age and its great trusts, the oil and railroad and sugar combinations whose power had grown to rival the state's; the Act forbade in its first section the contracts and combinations that restrain trade, the cartels and conspiracies of the few, and in its second the act of monopolizing itself. The Clayton Act of 1914 reached specific practices — the merger that lessens competition, the tie, the exclusive deal — and the same year's statute raised the Federal Trade Commission to police unfair methods of competition. Across the Atlantic, a parallel apparatus would grow around the prohibition of abuse of dominance and anticompetitive agreement. Two doctrinal tools order the whole edifice: some conduct, the naked fixing of prices or division of markets, is condemned per se, illegal without inquiry into its effects; the rest, the vertical restraint and the single firm's conduct and the merger, is weighed under the rule of reason, its harms and benefits balanced case by case. Much of antitrust history is the migration of practices between these two boxes.

The largest migration was the work of the Chicago school, and it amounted to a revolution in what antitrust was for. Through the 1960s and 1970s, Aaron Director, Richard Posner, and above all Robert Bork, in a book whose title named the perceived problem, The Antitrust Paradox, argued that the law had lost its way in a thicket of incompatible goals — protecting small competitors, resisting bigness as such, dispersing power for its own sake — and should be disciplined by a single lodestar: consumer welfare, which Bork largely identified with economic efficiency and measured by prices and output. Practices the courts had long condemned were, on the Chicago analysis, usually efficient or harmless; predatory pricing in particular was held to be irrational and rare, for the predator bleeds more than its prey and the prey may simply wait. Markets, the school taught, correct monopoly faster than courts can, so intervention should be sparing. The courts and the enforcement agencies embraced this view from around 1980, and the consequence was a generation of permissive antitrust: monopolization cases grew scarce, mergers passed easily, and the price-focused consumer-welfare standard reigned. To its admirers this was the maturation of a once-incoherent field into a rigorous one; to its later critics it was the law falling asleep while concentration and markups, the rising power of The Share's superstar firms, climbed unchecked.

Antitrust must decide what it is for before it can decide what to forbid — and that decision is a politics, not a theorem.

The reaction, gathering through the 2010s, took the name of Louis Brandeis, the old foe of bigness, and found its manifesto in a law student's article. Lina Khan's Amazon's Antitrust Paradox, in 2017, argued that the consumer-welfare standard is structurally blind to the power of platforms: a firm that keeps its prices low, even below cost, while integrating across markets and foreclosing rivals and amassing dominance, registers as no harm at all to a standard that watches only price — and yet its power grows. The New Brandeisians would return antitrust to a concern with structure, with the competitive process rather than the consumer price alone, with power over workers and suppliers and the conditions of innovation and even the health of democracy — the dispersal of concentrated economic might that the framers of the Sherman Act, suspicious of private power in every form, had chiefly sought. Khan came to chair the Federal Trade Commission; major cases issued against the great platforms; new and stricter merger guidelines lowered the thresholds of concern and widened the recognized theories of harm to reach labor markets and the acquisition of nascent rivals. The consumer-welfare standard, unquestioned for a generation, is now the central battleground of the field — and the contest beneath the doctrine is the oldest question of all: what should the law maximize? The consumer's price, precise and narrow; total efficiency, broader; or the competitive process and the dispersal of power, broadest and hardest to measure. The choice is a normative one wearing technical dress, and it decides what counts as a failure worth curing.

the edge The consumer-welfare standard's virtue was discipline: it gave antitrust a measurable lodestar and curbed the incoherent, sometimes protectionist enforcement of the mid-century, which had shielded small firms at the consumer's expense. Its vice, the Brandeisians charge, is that it sees only one face of power — the price to the consumer — and is blind to power over workers and suppliers, over innovation and the political order, and to the platform that buys dominance while keeping prices low. Whether to keep the disciplined-but-narrow standard or to broaden it at the risk of returning to incoherence is the unresolved question of competition policy, and economics cannot settle it alone, for it turns on what a society wishes its economy to protect: efficiency only, or also the dispersal of power that concentrated wealth, in every age, has threatened.

Plate the Eleventh · The Failures · № X

The Platform

the modern frontier; networks, data, and two-sided markets

The plate asks what happens to power when the market is a platform, the product is free, and the price is your attention?

The digital platform has become the characteristic form of market power in our age, and it gathers nearly every theme of this volume into a single, novel shape. Its first peculiarity is that it is a two-sided market, in the sense Jean-Charles Rochet and Jean Tirole gave the term: it serves two or more groups whose participation each makes the other more valuable — buyers and sellers on a marketplace, riders and drivers on a ride service, users and advertisers on a search engine or social network, cardholders and merchants on a payment system. Because the value to each side rises with the participation of the other, the platform's pricing must balance the two, and it commonly charges one side heavily while subsidizing the other to nothing — free search paid for by advertisers, a free social network monetized by attention. This balancing breaks the intuitions the rest of the volume relied upon: a price below cost on one side may be no predation but the natural way to bring both sides aboard, and a high price on the other no simple mark of power. Antitrust built for one-sided markets has struggled even to define the relevant market here.

The platform's second peculiarity is the ferocity of its network effects, which turn the barrier of the third plate into something close to destiny. When a service grows more valuable as more people use it, and more valuable on each side as the other side fills, a market can tip decisively toward a single dominant platform — and once tipped, the dominant platform is nearly impossible to dislodge, for no user will cross to a rival the other side has not joined. The winner-take-most outcome of The Share's superstar firms is here a structural tendency, and it is reinforced by data: the feedback loop in which more users yield more data, more data a better product, and a better product more users, is self-propelling, and the incumbent's accumulated data becomes a barrier no entrant can vault — power and its own moat in one. The platform's third peculiarity defeats the chief instrument of the previous plate. When the service is free, there is no price for the consumer-welfare standard to examine, and yet power is plainly wielded — the true price being the user's attention and data, and the dimensions along which power tells being the quality of the service, the privacy surrendered, and the terms on which data is extracted, all of which a price-watching standard cannot see.

When the product is free, the price is the user — and a standard that watches only prices watches the wrong thing.

Upon these foundations the platform practices a power that is the digital reprise of this volume's earlier plates. It engages in self-preferencing — the marketplace ranking its own goods above its rivals', the search engine steering users to its own services — which is the leverage and foreclosure of the sixth plate in a new room. It defends itself by the kill zone, acquiring nascent competitors before they can grow into threats, the purchase of potential rivalry that the inverted-U of the ninth plate marks as innovation's enemy. It exercises buyer power, in the seventh plate's sense, over the merchants and drivers and creators who have nowhere else to reach their customers, dictating the rates and the terms. And it acts as a gatekeeper, the indispensable intermediary that can tax and exclude at will. The policy response is itself dividing along the fault line of the last plate: Europe's Digital Markets Act imposes structural rules on designated gatekeepers in advance, forbidding self-preferencing and mandating interoperability, a regulatory approach in the spirit of the natural-monopoly remedy; the United States proceeds case by case, with great suits against the dominant search, mobile, marketplace, and social platforms, and a live debate over whether to regulate them as utilities or to break them apart.

the edge Whether the platforms are a genuinely new kind of power needing new tools, or old market power in novel dress that existing principles rightly applied can handle, is the living dispute. The Chicago-descended view reads low prices and rapid innovation as competition working, holds that today's dominant platform is tomorrow's forgotten one, and warns that intervention may freeze a dynamic sector; the Brandeisian view holds that network effects and data make platform dominance durable in a way the old self-correction story never anticipated, and that the zero price hides real harm to privacy, to rivals, and to the competitive process. The truth is not yet known, because the experiment is running — the cases are in the courts, the new rules are freshly in force — and whether platform power proves as self-correcting as its defenders promise or as entrenched as its critics fear is, at the time of writing, genuinely open: the gravest test the economics of power has met since the trusts that gave antitrust its birth.

Plate the Twelfth · The Failures · № X

The Sovereign

the structure beneath the price; toward The Rent, The Tax, The Rule

The plate asks if competition is the exception and power the rule, what does the price beneath our prices truly measure?

The volume opened with a definition and may close by collecting what the definition has yielded. A firm has market power when it can set its price away from the price competition would impose — above the cost of what it sells, below the value of what it buys — and the entire inquiry has been the anatomy of that single deviation. Power's signature is the gap between price and cost. Its measure is the markup, or on the buying side the markdown, which proves to be the inverse of the other party's elasticity, and so the scarcity of their alternatives made into a number. Its source is the barrier to entry, for power without a barrier is power competition dissolves. Its degrees run along a spectrum from the competitive crowd to the single seller, with concentration a clue to its presence but never a proof. Among the few it becomes strategic, a game of anticipation in which collusion beckons and betrayal tempts. It is extended by leverage from one market to the next, and mirrored exactly in the buyer who holds the seller below value. It costs a society the triangle of trade destroyed, the rectangle of rent dissipated in the seeking, and the slack of the firm no rival presses. It is defended, with real force, as the prize that pays for innovation — though the inverted-U shows that too much of it deadens what a little of it spurs. It is restrained, when a society chooses, by the contested instrument of antitrust. And in our age it gathers, above all, in the platform.

From this anatomy the volume's thesis stands clear, and it inverts the order in which economics is usually taught. Perfect competition — every actor a price-taker, every price equal to marginal cost, power exactly zero — is not the normal state of markets from which monopoly is a departure. It is the rare and frictionless limit, the special case that obtains only when both sides of every exchange have somewhere else to go. Power, present in degree, is the general case, the ordinary condition; the question is never simply whether a market has power but how much, on which side, behind what barrier, and at what cost. The competitive ideal is the exception taught as the rule, and the price that bears power's mark — above cost where the seller commands it, below value where the buyer does — is not the verdict of competition but the outcome of a contest that the absence of alternatives has already settled.

This is why the volume answers, at last, the question it has carried: what is a fair price worth, when one side has nowhere else to go? The fair price of the competitive ideal, equal to cost, presupposes that both sides could walk away — that each disciplines the other by the credible refusal the bargaining theory of The Wage made decisive. Power is precisely the absence of that refusal, the missing alternative, the "nowhere else to go." A price set under power therefore measures not the worth of the thing but the want of an alternative, and to call it fair because the market yielded it is to mistake the verdict of power for the verdict of competition. Fairness in price presupposes alternatives; power is their absence; and so the "fair price" extracted from a party with nowhere else to go is a contradiction the market quietly conceals — the deepest instance of the whole table's lesson, that a price is a verdict on a contest and only as fair as the contest behind it.

A fair price presupposes that both sides could walk away. Power is the absence of the walking away — so the price it sets measures not worth, but the want of an alternative.

And here the volume reveals its place beneath the three that concern distribution, for it is their foundation. They were each, in the end, accounts of how the product is divided, and each invoked power to explain a division that productivity alone could not. The Wage found the worker paid below his product by the single buyer of labor — and that buyer was monopsony, the buyer power of this volume's seventh plate. The Share found labor's portion of the whole falling, and its leading explanation the rise of markups and superstar firms, the loss flowing not to capital but to pure profit — and that markup is this volume's measure of power, that pure profit its rent. The Surplus found efficiency silent on who keeps the gains from trade, and the keeping decided by power, its cost the deadweight triangle and the rent-seeking rectangle — which are this volume's eighth plate exactly. The great result of The Share was that the division of the product has a degree of freedom technology leaves open, filled by power; this volume has named what that power is, structurally — the ability to set price away from cost, conferred by barriers, measured by the markup and the markdown. The distributional triangle stands on the anatomy of power; remove it, and the wage, the surplus, and the share have no account of the force that fills their open degree of freedom. The four volumes are one structure: three of distribution, and beneath them the power that distributes.

The foundation hands on what it cannot itself contain. It hands to its neighbors in the family of failures — to The Externality, The Asymmetry, and The Commons — the recognition that power is the first of the market's failures but not the last, and that they intertwine, the data that confers platform power being also the information asymmetry of its sister element. It hands to The Rent the profit that power yields, for the rent is power's revenue — the return to a protected position, the pure profit The Share watched rising — and that element will anatomize the rent itself, in land and patent and scarcity, where this one has only named its source. It hands to The Tax the state as the counterweight, the antitrust and the regulation and the levy by which a public answers private power. It hands to The Firm the truth that the firm is itself an island of power and planning within the market, its very boundary a question of where command displaces price. And it hands to The Rule the law that both confers power — in the patent it grants, the charter it issues, the property in essential things it protects — and restrains it, for power is made and unmade by rules, and is never the brute fact of nature it pretends to be.

So the series' conceit completes another turn. It began by denying that prices are facts of nature, insisting that each is a verdict on a hidden contest; and this volume has named the contest most directly of all. The price beneath every price is a measure of who had somewhere else to go. Where both parties did, the price rests at cost, and competition has spoken; where one did not, the price departs from cost by the measure of the other's power, and what has spoken is not the market's wisdom but the scarcity of an alternative. To read a price as fair because the market set it is to forget that the market's verdict is only as just as the contest behind it, and that the contest is decided by power — by the barrier, by the markup, by the want of a substitute. This volume has made the hidden contest visible, and it has found power not at the margins of economic life but at its center, the sovereign that the competitive story was written to forget. The argument remains open, as it must — for the measure of power is contested, its cost spans orders of magnitude, its part in innovation cuts both ways, and its remedy turns on a choice of ends that economics cannot make. But the price will no longer pass for nature. It is a verdict on a contest; and where one side had nowhere else to go, it is power's verdict, read at last for what it is.
