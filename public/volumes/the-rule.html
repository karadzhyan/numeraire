Group IV · The Institutions

the design of markets

The question of the volume Markets are built, not found — and built by whom, in whose interest?

in print · twelve plates
Numéraire · Volume XVIII

The Argument

There is a picture of the market so common that it passes for nature: a place that arises of itself wherever people meet to truck and barter, needing no author and governed by no hand but the invisible one, a spontaneous order the state can only distort. The picture is false, and its falseness is the subject of this volume. A market is not found in nature but built — constructed, rule by rule, out of property and contract and enforcement and a hundred deliberate choices about what may be traded, by whom, on what terms, and what shall happen when the bargain breaks. The free market is not the absence of rules but a particular and intricate set of them; laissez-faire, as Karl Polanyi observed, was itself planned, enforced by the very state it claimed to exclude. With this volume the institutions reach their most abstract member, for the rule is the institution of the market itself — not the firm, the contract, or the money that operate within markets, but the design of the market as such, the rules of the game that make exchange possible and shape every price struck inside it.

Once the market is seen as built, two questions open that the natural picture concealed, and they are the questions the gloss poses. The first is who builds it — who writes the rules of exchange, the antitrust and the regulation and the law of property and contract by which a market is constituted and constrained. The answer is never no one, for rules do not write themselves; it is some mixture of legislatures and courts and regulators and the interests that lobby them, and the identity of the rule-writer is the first thing the natural picture hides. The second question follows: in whose interest are the rules written? For rules are not neutral. A rule that looks a mere technicality of exchange — the length of a patent, the terms on which a platform may treat the sellers upon it, the standard to which a product must conform — distributes advantage, and whoever shapes it shapes the contest it governs. The recurring discovery of this volume is that the design of a market matters as much as the competition within it: that the same buyers and sellers, under different rules, produce different winners, different prices, and different worlds.

The market is not the absence of rules but a particular set of them. The only questions are who writes the rules, and in whose interest — for the design decides as much as the competition it governs.

The market's dependence on rules begins at the foundation, with property and contract. One cannot trade what one cannot own, so a market in anything presupposes a rule assigning ownership — and that rule, as The Rent insisted, is a creature of law and not of nature, made and remade by enclosure and patent and the slow construction of what may be held. One cannot rely on a bargain without a power to enforce it, so a market presupposes a law of contract and a means to compel performance. Ronald Coase showed that the assignment of these rights, in a world without the frictions of bargaining, would not affect which uses prevailed, only who paid whom; but the world is full of frictions, and so the assignment matters for efficiency as well as for justice, and the choice of who holds which right is a choice about the shape of the economy. Upon this foundation the further rules are raised: the antitrust that keeps markets competitive, since competition, as The Power showed, does not maintain itself; the regulation that corrects the failures of externality and asymmetry; the standards and protocols that let strangers transact at all.

But the rules that govern a market are written by fallible and interested hands, and here the volume turns from the construction of markets to its corruption. George Stigler taught that regulation, far from serving the public it claims to protect, is often captured by the industry it governs — written, in effect, by the regulated for the regulated, a barrier raised against rivals under the banner of the public good. The rules become a prize, and the contest to write them a form of the rent-seeking The Rent described, in which resources are spent not to produce but to capture what others produce. So the question of whose interest the rules serve has a recurring and unlovely answer: often the incumbent's, the organized few who care intensely about a rule pitted against the many who scarcely know it exists. The deepest power in an economy is not market power, the power to set a price within the rules; it is the power to write the rules themselves, and that power is contested in arenas the price-taker never sees.

Yet the same insight that exposes the corruption of rules has bred a constructive science, for if markets are designed, they can be designed well. The field of market design, the work of economists such as Alvin Roth and Paul Milgrom, treats the economist as engineer, building markets that achieve what unstructured exchange cannot — the auctions that sell the radio spectrum or the government's debt, where the choice of format decides whether billions are raised or squandered; the matching markets that pair doctors to hospitals, children to schools, kidneys to patients, allocating without prices the things that prices cannot or should not allocate. These markets are not found; they are made, to specification, and their performance turns on the rules far more than on the competition the rules enclose. And the science has a moral edge, for in deciding what to make into a market it must confront what should not be one — the repugnant transactions, in organs and votes and persons, that most societies place beyond price, marking the boundary of the market as itself a designed and contested choice.

The thesis, for the plates to earn, may be stated plainly. A market is not a fact of nature but a designed institution, a structure of rules, and the rules are a verdict on a contest over who shall write them and in whose interest. This completes the argument the series has pressed from its first volume. We began by denaturalizing the prices struck within markets, showing each to be a verdict on a hidden contest rather than a fact of nature; we end by denaturalizing the market itself, showing it too to be made and not found — so that every price is doubly a verdict, struck within rules that are themselves the outcome of a contest over their authorship. The invisible hand, where it works, works inside a structure of visible rules that some hand has written; and to ask who wrote them, and for whom, is to reach the deepest level at which the contests of this whole table are fought. The design of the market is the design of the economy, and it is no more given by nature than any price within it.

Plate the First · The Institutions · № XVIII

The Artifice

markets are built, not found; the market as a made thing

The plate asks if the market is the most natural thing in the economy, why does it collapse the moment the law that upholds it is withdrawn?

Adam Smith found in human nature a certain propensity to truck, barter, and exchange, and from that propensity a picture has grown that passes, in much of our thought, for nature itself: the market as a spontaneous order, springing up wherever people meet to trade, requiring no author and tolerating no governor but the invisible hand, a thing that the state can only distort and never improve. On this picture the free market is the baseline, the default, the way of things when nothing interferes, and every rule is an intrusion upon a natural liberty. The picture is among the most consequential errors in the whole of economic thought, and the business of this volume is to correct it. For the market is not found in nature. It is built — constructed, rule by rule, out of property and contract and enforcement, out of a hundred deliberate decisions about what may be sold, by whom, on what terms, and what shall follow when a bargain is broken.

Consider what a market requires before a single trade can occur. It requires property, for one cannot sell what one cannot own, and ownership is not a fact of the physical world but a rule of the social one — a claim that others are bound to respect, enforced by a power that will make them. It requires contract, for one cannot rely on a promise of future performance without some assurance that the promise can be compelled, and that assurance is again a rule, backed again by a power. It requires a medium, the money of the sixteenth volume; it requires standards, so that a pound is a pound and a grade is a grade; it requires a law against fraud and force, so that the trade is an exchange and not a robbery. Strip these away and no market remains, only the barter of the strong with the weak at the point of a sword. The market, far from being what exists when the rules are removed, is precisely what the rules create.

This is why the phrase free market misleads. The free market is not the absence of rules but a particular and intricate set of them — the rules of property and contract and tort, of corporations and securities, of antitrust and bankruptcy, a vast inherited architecture that we mistake for the state of nature because it is so familiar and so old. Karl Polanyi, tracing the birth of the market economy in his great study, found that the self-regulating market was no natural growth but a deliberate construction, imposed and sustained by the very state it claimed to exclude: laissez-faire, he wrote, was planned. The land and labor and money that the market treats as commodities had first to be made into commodities, by enclosure and statute and often by force, for they are not commodities by nature — no one produces land or people for sale. The market economy was a political achievement, wrenched into being against the grain of older arrangements, and it has required, ever since, the constant maintenance of the rules that constitute it.

The market is the most artificial thing in the economy, not the least — a structure of rules so successful that it has come to pass for a fact of nature.

The proof is in the collapse. Withdraw the legal order that upholds the market — let the courts fail, the property rolls burn, the enforcement cease — and the market does not revert to some pure natural exchange; it disintegrates into force, into theft and protection rackets and the rule of whoever holds the gun, as the failed states of the world repeatedly show. The sophisticated market, with its credit and its contracts reaching years into the future, is the most law-dependent of all human arrangements, and the least able to survive the dissolution of the rules. That the most artificial thing in the economy should feel the most natural is the deepest illusion this series confronts. The whole table has worked to denaturalize the prices struck within markets, to show each a verdict on a hidden contest rather than a fact of nature; this volume denaturalizes the market itself, the supposed natural stage on which those contests play, and finds it too a construction — a verdict, in its rules, on a contest over who shall write them.

the edge The claim that markets are built, not found, is sometimes heard as a partisan point, an argument for more government, and it is important to see that it is not — it lies prior to the partisan dispute, and reframes it. To say the market is constructed is not to say it ought to be more regulated or less; it is to say that the choice is never between the market and its absence, or between rules and no rules, but always among rival sets of rules, every one of them a construction. The libertarian who wants a freer market and the social democrat who wants a more managed one are not disagreeing about whether to have rules but about which rules to have — what the property regime shall include, how strong the patents, how tightly the contracts may bind, what the antitrust shall forbid. Both are proposals for a designed market; neither is the natural market, because there is none. The error the thesis corrects is not a political preference but a category mistake — the treating of one particular set of rules, usually the familiar one, as the baseline of nature from which all others are departures. Once that mistake is cleared away, the real question comes into view, and it is not whether to build the market but how, and for whom — the question every plate that follows takes up.

Plate the Second · The Institutions · № XVIII

The Scaffold

the rules a market needs; property, contract, money, enforcement

The plate asks what must be in place before two strangers can safely trade — and who puts it there?

If the market is built, it is worth setting out what it is built from — the rules without which no exchange between strangers is possible, the scaffold on which every price in this table is hung. The figure draws them as the architecture they are: a market resting on pillars, the pillars resting on the ground of the state. The first pillar is property, the rule of ownership that says what may be held and by whom, with the rights to use, to exclude, and to transfer that ownership confers. Nothing can be traded that cannot first be owned, and ownership, as the next plate will press, is a rule and not a thing — a claim that others are bound to honor, made good by a power that will compel them. The second pillar is contract, the law of enforceable promise, without which no bargain could reach beyond the simultaneous swap of goods in hand; it is contract that lets a party bind itself to deliver next year, to pay on credit, to perform its share of a deal whose other half comes later, and so contract that makes possible every transaction extended in time.

The remaining pillars complete the structure. Money, the medium of the sixteenth volume, spares the traders the double coincidence of wants that pure barter demands, letting any good exchange for any other through the common third thing. Standards — the weights and measures and grades, the assurance that a pound is a pound and a grade is a grade — spare them the cost of verifying, at every trade, exactly what is changing hands, a cost that the twelfth element, The Asymmetry, shows can be ruinous. And a law against fraud and against force ensures that what passes between them is an exchange and not a robbery, voluntary and informed rather than coerced or deceived. Beneath all four pillars lies the base on which they stand: the legal order, the courts and the police and the state's monopoly of legitimate force, without which property is a wish and contract a hope. The market that styles itself the enemy of the state is upheld by the state at every point; take away the base, and the whole structure falls into the rubble of force from which the rules had raised it.

Two strangers can trade only because a thousand rules stand invisibly behind them — and every one of those rules was written by someone, and tilts the ground beneath the bargain.

And who puts the scaffold in place? Some hand always does — chiefly the state, through its legislatures and courts and regulators, but also custom, and the private ordering of merchants, and the international agreements that let trade cross borders. The rules do not arise of themselves; they are made, and remade, by identifiable authors with identifiable interests, which is the subject the volume turns to next. But it is worth marking now what the legal realist Robert Hale saw a century ago, that these background rules are not the neutral frame they appear to be. The property rule that lets me exclude you from what I own is, however quietly, a grant of power over you, for it compels you to pay me, or to labor for me, to gain access to what you need; and the whole free-seeming play of bargaining proceeds within a structure of legal coercion that the bargaining never questions. The scaffold does not merely enable exchange; it distributes the power with which the parties come to it. The rules are not neutral, and could be otherwise, and who writes them, and how, decides the terms of every contest they enclose.

the edge The rules that scaffold a market are easiest to see when they are absent and hardest to see when they work, which is why their political character is so often missed. When property is secure and contracts are honored and the courts function, the scaffold turns invisible, and the trades that rest upon it seem to float free, the unaided product of willing buyers and willing sellers; the rules are felt only as background, the neutral air in which exchange breathes. But the rules are not neutral, and Hale saw why: the property rule that lets me exclude you is in its quiet way a grant of power over you, compelling you to pay or to labor for access to what you need, so that the apparently free play of bargaining proceeds within a structure of legal coercion the bargaining itself never questions. Change the rules — make the patent shorter, the property weaker, the contract harder to enforce against the desperate — and the bargains shift, the power moves, the prices come out otherwise. The scaffold is not a neutral frame but a distributive one, setting the terms of every contest it encloses; and because it is built, and could be built differently, the choice of how to build it is among the most political a society makes, however technical and invisible the rules in which the choice lies hidden.

Plate the Third · The Institutions · № XVIII

The Enclosure

property as the foundational rule; its creation and assignment; Coase

The plate asks what does it take to turn a thing into property — and does it matter who is given the right?

The first pillar of the market is property, and it is worth pausing on how strange a thing property is, for nothing is property by nature. A field, an idea, a stretch of river, the capacity of the air to carry away smoke — none of these is ownable in itself; each becomes property only when a rule decides that it shall, and assigns it to someone. Land was not always private. For centuries much of it was held in common, grazed and gleaned by all who had the customary right, until the enclosures of the English centuries fenced it off, extinguished the common rights, and made of the open field a private holding — a deliberate construction of property where none had been, accomplished by statute and often by force, which Polanyi counted among the founding violences of the market age. What looks like the most solid and ancient of institutions was, at its origin, an act of legislation, the conversion of a commons into a commodity.

The construction continues to this day, most visibly in the property we make from ideas. An invention or a song is not naturally excludable — once known, it can be used by anyone, costing its maker nothing to share — so it becomes property only because the law decrees, through patent and copyright, that for a term no one may use it without the owner's leave. Intellectual property is property by pure fiat, and the scope of the fiat — what may be patented, for how long, how broadly — is a design choice with vast consequences, conjuring the rents that The Rent traced from nothing but a rule. And the frontier of property keeps advancing: we decide, in our own time, whether one may own a stretch of the radio spectrum, a sequence of a genome, a quantity of carbon emissions, a pool of personal data. Each is a choice to create property where none existed, to enclose a new commons, and each choice — what to make ownable, and who shall own it — is as distributive and as political as the enclosures of old.

Once a thing is made ownable, a second question follows: who shall hold the right? Here stands the most cited and most misread result in the economics of law, Ronald Coase's theorem of 1960. In a world without the costs of bargaining, Coase showed, the initial assignment of a right does not affect which use prevails, only who pays whom. The figure shows why. A factory's production benefits the factory and harms its neighbors, and the efficient level of it is Q*, where the marginal benefit to the one equals the marginal cost to the other. If the neighbors own the right to clean air, the factory will pay them for permission to pollute up to Q*, since below Q* its gain exceeds their loss; if the factory owns the right to pollute, the neighbors will pay it to cut back down to Q*, since above Q* their loss exceeds its gain. Either way the activity settles at Q*, the efficient level; what changes between the two assignments is only the direction of the money, and so the distribution of the gains. The right is worth having for what it puts in one's pocket, but the world reaches the same efficient point regardless of who has it.

Nothing is property by nature. Land, ideas, water, the air's capacity to carry off smoke — each becomes ownable only when a rule decides it shall, and hands it to someone rather than to someone else.

That is the theorem in the frictionless world, and the world is not frictionless. Bargaining is costly; the parties may be many and scattered; information is poor; the holdout and the free-rider defeat the deal, as The Commons shows. The moment transaction costs are admitted — and they are everywhere — the assignment of rights begins to matter for efficiency as well as distribution, for now the efficient outcome may not be reached by private bargain at all, and the law must approximate it by placing the right where it does the most good and the least harm. So the design of property — what may be owned, and by whom, and with what clarity the right is drawn — becomes an economic question of the first rank, which is why Coase is the father of the economics of law. Property, the market's foundational pillar, turns out to be doubly designed: once in deciding what shall be ownable, and again in deciding who shall own it. The most solid institution of the market is, at bottom, a structure of choices, and like every choice in this table it favors some over others.

the edge Coase's theorem is among the most cited and most misunderstood results in economics, and the misunderstanding runs in a revealing direction. Read carelessly, it says that externalities take care of themselves — that if property rights are clear, private bargaining reaches the efficient outcome with no need of the state, so that Pigou was wrong and regulation superfluous. This reading inverts Coase's purpose. He invoked the zero-transaction-cost world not to describe reality but to isolate, by its absence, the thing that matters; for in the real world transaction costs are pervasive and often prohibitive, and it is precisely because they are that the assignment of rights and the design of institutions carry such weight. When a factory's smoke harms a thousand scattered households, they cannot costlessly assemble to bargain; the holdout and the free-rider defeat them, and the efficient outcome must be approximated, if at all, by a rule — a liability standard, a tax, a regulation, a deliberate placing of the right on one side. Coase's true legacy is therefore the opposite of the laissez-faire moral so often drawn from him: that institutions matter, that the legal rules of property and liability have economic consequences which cannot be assumed away, and that the design of those rules is an economic question of the first importance. The theorem quoted to prove that the rules do not matter in fact proves that they matter most.

Plate the Fourth · The Institutions · № XVIII

The Referee

antitrust and regulation; the rules that keep markets working

The plate asks if competition is the market's great virtue, why will it not maintain itself?

The foundational rules of property and contract let a market exist; a further set of rules keeps it working, and chief among the things that must be kept is competition. For competition is the market's great engine of discipline — the force that pares prices toward cost, drives the laggard to improve, and turns the self-interest of rival sellers to the buyer's advantage — and yet competition will not maintain itself. This is the fact on which the plate turns, and it is easily missed by those who imagine the competitive market a natural equilibrium that persists once reached. It is nothing of the kind. Left untended, as The Power showed, markets drift not toward the textbook crowd of small price-takers but toward concentration: the economies of scale and the network effects that reward bigness, the mergers that swallow rivals, the predation that drives them out, and, beneath it all, the simple truth Adam Smith named — that people of the same trade seldom meet without the conversation ending in a conspiracy against the public. Competition is a fire that goes out unless it is tended, and the tending is done by rules.

The rules that tend it are the antitrust, or competition policy, that this volume must count among the market's designers rather than its interlopers. They forbid the cartel, the price-fixing and the carving-up of markets by which rivals agree not to be rivals; they block the mergers that would concentrate a market past the point of discipline; they police the abuses by which a dominant firm pulls up the ladder behind it — the predatory pricing, the exclusionary dealing, the leverage from one market into the next that The Power traced. And the shape of these rules is a design choice of the greatest consequence, not a technical given. What counts as the harm antitrust exists to prevent — high prices to consumers, or slackened innovation, or sheer concentration, or the monopsony power that presses down wages — has been answered differently in different eras, and the answer has remade the economy each time. The Sherman Act's framers feared bigness itself; the Chicago school narrowed the law to a single test of consumer prices, and under that narrowing the concentration of recent decades was permitted to proceed; a newer movement would widen the lens again. The rules of competition are written, and rewritten, by choice, and the market they govern changes with them.

Beyond competition lies conduct, and here the maintaining rules are the regulation that governs how the market may behave — the second great body of designed rules, aimed at the failures the Failures group anatomized. Where buyers cannot verify what they are sold, as The Asymmetry shows, regulation mandates the safety and the disclosure that the market alone would not supply. Where production harms third parties, as The Externality shows, regulation limits the pollution and prices the harm. Where the failure of one firm can topple many, as the runs of The Money show, regulation requires the capital and the transparency that contain the contagion. The rationale, in each case, is the public interest: the market, left to itself, produces outcomes — the unsafe drug, the fouled river, the fraud, the panic — that rules can prevent, and the rules exist to prevent them. This is the public-interest theory of regulation, and it is the ideal against which the institution must be judged.

Competition is not the market's natural state but its maintained one — a fire that goes out unless tended. The referee's rules are not an interruption of the game; they are what keep a game from becoming a conquest.

The point to carry forward is that the referee is not outside the game. The antitrust that keeps a market competitive and the regulation that keeps it honest are not external corrections imposed upon a natural market; they are part of the market's design, as constitutive of it as the property and the contract beneath. A market with vigorous antitrust and one without are different markets, producing different prices and different winners, though the buyers and sellers be the same — the design deciding as much as the competition it governs. But the public-interest rationale carries a silent assumption, that the referee serves the public, and it is this assumption the next plate must trouble. For the rules of competition and conduct are written by fallible officials, subject to pressure from the very firms they govern, and the body charged with maintaining the market is as liable to capture as the market is to monopoly.

the edge The case for the referee rests on a premise the next plate will trouble, but the premise is sound as far as it reaches: that competition, the market's chief discipline, is not self-sustaining, and that without rules to maintain it the market tends not toward the equilibrium of many small price-takers but toward the concentration The Power anatomized. Adam Smith, no friend of meddling, saw it plainly — that men of the same trade, left to themselves, gravitate to conspiracy against the public, and that the natural drift of commerce is toward the comfortable cartel and the protected monopoly, not the bracing competition the apologists for laissez-faire imagine. If that is right, then the competitive market is an achievement and not a default, and the antitrust and regulation that sustain it are not intrusions upon a natural order but the rules that constitute the order worth having. The difficulty, which the plate that follows takes up, is that the same logic which justifies the referee — that organized interests will bend the market to their advantage — applies to the referee himself, for the rules are written by officials subject to the same pressures, and the body charged with guarding the market is as liable to capture as the market is to monopoly. The argument for rules and the argument against trusting their authors spring from a single root, and the volume must hold both.

Plate the Fifth · The Institutions · № XVIII

The Captive

regulatory capture; who writes the rules, in whose interest

The plate asks the referee is meant to guard the public — so why does he so often end up guarding the players he was set to watch?

The public-interest theory of the last plate supposes that the referee serves the crowd in the stands. George Stigler taught economists to doubt it. In his theory of economic regulation he argued that regulation is, as a rule, acquired by the industry it governs and designed and operated chiefly for that industry's benefit — not supplied by a benevolent state to protect the public, but demanded by the regulated and bought, in the political market, by those who value it most. For regulation is useful to an industry: it can bar entry through licenses, raise prices through restrictions, hobble rivals through standards written to incumbents' specifications, and channel subsidy toward the established. The referee, meant to guard the public, is forever tempted to guard the players instead, and the name for his yielding is capture. Where the public-interest theory sees a guardian, the capture theory sees a prize — the rules a thing to be won, and won, more often than not, by the side with the most at stake and the best means to pursue it.

Why capture should be the standing tendency rather than the occasional lapse is explained by the structure Mancur Olson laid bare in his logic of collective action. The regulated industry is concentrated — a handful of firms, each with a fortune riding on the rule — and concentration is the mother of organization; such an industry can hire the lobbyists, draft the language, and fund the campaigns by which rules are made. The public is the opposite: diffuse, millions strong, each member bearing only a trifling share of the cost, and so unable to organize, defeated by the free-rider problem that The Commons describes, for why should any one consumer spend to fight a rule that costs him a dollar when the industry will spend millions to win it? The contest to write the rules is therefore tilted at its foundation, the concentrated few against the diffuse many, those who care intensely against those who scarcely know the rule exists. To this structural tilt are added the regulator's dependence on the industry for the very expertise he needs to regulate it, and the revolving door through which officials pass to lucrative industry posts and back, until the watcher and the watched come to share a worldview and a set of friends.

The contest to write the rules has a cost beyond the bad rules it produces, and Gordon Tullock named it. To compete for a favorable rule is to engage in rent-seeking — to spend on lobbyists, lawyers, and political favor in order to capture or create a rent — and the figure shows the reckoning. A rule that restricts supply creates a rent, the rectangle of profit between the competitive price and the propped-up one, and an old analysis counted only the deadweight triangle of forgone trades as the social loss, treating the rectangle as a mere transfer from buyers to the favored. Tullock saw that the rectangle is no mere transfer when it must be fought for, because the fighting consumes real resources — the talent and treasure poured into the lobby and the courtroom, which build nothing and grow nothing, existing only to move the rent from one pocket to another. In the limit the whole rent is dissipated in the contest to win it, so that a rule worth a fortune to its captor may cost society that entire fortune in the struggle, on top of the deadweight loss the restriction itself imposes. The seeking that The Rent described is, in this volume's terms, the contest over who shall write the rules, and it is dear.

The deepest power in an economy is not the power to set a price, but the power to write the rule that sets the terms of every price — and that power is won by whoever cares most and is organized best, rarely the public.

Here the volume reaches its central claim about power, and it is a claim that reorders the table. The Power studied market power, the power to set a price above cost within the existing rules; but there is a power deeper than that, the power to write the rules themselves. To set the terms on which a market operates — what may be owned, who may enter, what counts as fair competition, how a platform may treat those upon it — is to shape every price struck within it, a reach the mere monopolist never has. The firm that charges a high price has power over its customers; the interest that writes the rule under which it charges has power over the whole market, and over its rivals, and over the customers of them all. This is why the contest over the rules is the deepest contest in the economy, and why it is fought where the price-taker cannot see it — in the committee room, the regulatory docket, the standards body, the court. The plate that closes this volume returns to it; for now the dark answer to the gloss's question stands plainly: the rules are written, more often than the public-interest theory admits, in the interest of those organized enough to write them.

the edge The theory of capture corrects the naive view of regulation but can curdle into a cynicism as misleading as the naivety it replaces. The naive view holds that regulation serves the public because that is its stated purpose; Stigler showed that purpose and effect diverge, that regulation is demanded and shaped by those it governs, and that much of it raises prices and bars entry to the incumbents' benefit — an indispensable correction, explaining the occupational licenses that protect the licensed, the compliance burdens that big firms welcome because entrants cannot bear them, the revolving door that aligns the regulator's prospects with the regulated's profits. But the correction overshoots if it concludes that all regulation is capture and every rule a racket, for the safety, the clean air, and the financial stability that good regulation secures are real gains, and capture is a tendency to be resisted rather than a fate to be assumed. The truth lies in the structure Olson named: that concentrated interests organize and diffuse ones do not, so the contest to write the rules is systematically tilted toward the few who care intensely over the many who scarcely notice — a tilt, not a certainty, which strong institutions, public attention, and transparency can resist but never abolish. The right inference is neither that the referee is incorruptible nor that he is bought, but that he is perpetually pulled toward the players, and that keeping him honest is itself a contest, never finally won.

Plate the Sixth · The Institutions · № XVIII

The Engineer

market design as a science; building markets that work

The plate asks if a market is built, who are its builders — and what do they build that mere competition cannot?

If markets are built, they can be built well, and in our time a science has grown up to build them. For most of its history economics described markets — explaining how they worked, what equilibria they reached, when they failed. Market design does something else: it engineers them, taking a real problem of allocation and constructing the rules of a market to solve it, choosing the format and the algorithm and the procedures to reach a good result. Alvin Roth, who with Lloyd Shapley founded much of the field, called this the economist as engineer, and it is an apt figure, for the market designer works as an engineer does, not deducing how bridges behave in the abstract but building a particular bridge that must bear a particular load. The questions change accordingly. Not what equilibrium will arise, but what rules will produce the equilibrium we want; not how the market works, but how to make it work — for the market is no longer a natural object to be studied but an artifact to be designed.

The central discovery of the field is the one this whole volume has pressed, now proven in practice and measured in results: that the design matters, often as much as the competition it governs. The figure shows the lesson in its starkest form. Take a single market — the same buyers, the same sellers, the same goods, the same vigor of competition throughout — and run it under four different sets of rules, and the outcomes spread from half the achievable value to nearly all of it. The participants did not change; only the rules did, and the rules decided the result. A badly designed market, however fierce the competition within it, can squander value, invite gaming, congeal, or collapse; a well-designed one, with the very same participants, can realize nearly everything there is to realize. This is not a marginal refinement but the difference between a market that works and one that does not, and it puts beyond doubt the claim that the rules are no neutral frame but the very thing that determines what a market achieves.

What the good designer aims at can be stated as a set of properties the rules must secure. The market should be efficient, sending each good to whoever values it most and leaving no gain unrealized. It should be incentive-compatible, or strategy-proof, so that a participant cannot gain by misrepresenting what he wants, and honesty is safe — a direct application of the mechanism design that The Asymmetry introduced, defeating the hidden type by making truth the best policy. It should be stable, in the matching markets of the next plate, so that no pair wishes to abandon the match and pair off on their own. And it should be thick enough to draw the participants it needs, free enough of congestion to clear in time, and safe enough that people will trust it with their bids and their preferences. Roth distilled much of this into three demands — that a market be thick, uncongested, and safe — and the designer's art lies in writing rules that meet them where the unaided market would not.

The economist need not only describe the market she finds; she can build the market she wants — and the same buyers and sellers, under rules she chooses, will reach a different and better end.

The proof of the science is in the markets it has built, and the next two plates take up its triumphs — the auctions that sell the spectrum and the public debt, where the format decides whether a fortune is raised or lost, and the matching markets that pair doctors to hospitals and kidneys to patients, allocating without prices the things that prices cannot reach. These markets were not found; they were made, to specification, by economists who treated the rules as a thing to be engineered, and they work, when they work, by virtue of the design. Here is the constructive vindication of everything the volume has argued: that the market is an artifact, and that the artifact can be made to serve. Yet the science is a tool, and a tool serves whoever holds it. The same skill that builds a kidney exchange to save lives can build an auction to extract the most from its bidders, or a platform to advantage its house; and the question the field cannot answer is the one the whole volume turns on — not whether markets can be built well, but who shall decide what is built, and for whom.

the edge Market design is the most hopeful chapter in this volume and a partial answer to the pessimism of the last, yet it carries its own quiet warning. Its hope is real: where the capture theory shows the rules bent to private advantage, market design shows them deliberately shaped to public good — the kidney exchanges that have saved thousands of lives by arranging chains of donation no price could have arranged, the school-choice mechanisms that allocate seats fairly where clumsier systems invited gaming and despair, the auctions that put public resources to their best use and fill the treasury besides. These are rules written well, by skilled and public-spirited hands, and they prove that the design of markets can be a craft in the service of all. But the craft is a tool, and tools serve whoever wields them. The same expertise that designs a kidney exchange can design an advertising auction that extracts the most from advertisers, or a trading venue that advantages its owner, or a market whose rules favor the client who paid for them; and the great spectrum auctions were not serene exercises in social optimization but arenas in which every bidder pressed for the rules that would favor its own position. The engineer's skill answers whether markets can be built well; it does not answer the question the whole volume turns on, of who shall decide what is built, and for whom. A better-built market is a gain only if the builder serves the right master, and that, the science of design cannot determine.

Plate the Seventh · The Institutions · № XVIII

The Auction

auction design; the winner's curse; the design that makes the market

The plate asks when the state sells the airwaves or the Treasury sells its debt, why does the choice of auction rules decide whether a fortune is raised or lost?

The auction is the market designer's first and favorite instrument, and the clearest place to watch the rules decide the outcome. It is used wherever a thing must be sold whose price no one yet knows — the radio spectrum, the mineral lease, the public debt, the work of art, the advertising slot — for the auction does not require the seller to name a price; it elicits the price from the bidders, letting their competition discover what the thing is worth. And the rules of that competition can be drawn in several ways. In the English auction the price ascends until all but one bidder drop away; in the Dutch it descends from on high until someone accepts; in the sealed-bid first-price auction each submits one secret offer and the highest pays what he bid; in the sealed-bid second-price auction, William Vickrey's elegant invention, the highest bidder wins but pays only the second-highest bid, which makes honesty a dominant strategy, since what one pays turns on others' bids and not one's own, and there is then no reason to shade.

A famous theorem holds that, in an idealized world, these formats are equivalent in the revenue they raise: the bidders adjust their strategies to the rules — shading more, for instance, in the first-price auction where they pay their own bid — and the seller's expected take comes out the same whichever format is chosen. Revenue equivalence is a beautiful benchmark, and like most benchmarks its value lies in how it fails. The idealized world supposes that each bidder knows his own value privately and surely; the real world is otherwise, and nowhere more than where the value is common — the same for all bidders but unknown to each, like the oil beneath a tract or the worth of a band of spectrum. There the winner's curse appears, and the figure shows it.

The winner of a common-value auction is, by the very fact of winning, the bidder who estimated the value most highly — and the highest estimate, among many scattered around the truth, is the most likely to be too high. To win is therefore to discover that one was the most optimistic person in the room, which is rarely cause for joy; a bidder who naively bids his estimate will, on winning, have paid more than the thing is worth. Rational bidders must shade their bids to allow for this, and here the format ceases to be neutral, for the open ascending auction lets a bidder watch his rivals fall away and learn from their judgments, tempering his optimism, while the sealed bid shuts him up alone with it. Revenue equivalence dissolves; the design matters, and matters most where the stakes are highest.

An auction is the purest market there is — nothing but competition to set a price. And yet two auctions of the same thing, to the same bidders, under different rules, will hand the seller a fortune or a pittance.

The proof came when governments sold the radio spectrum. Here was a prize of staggering value and no known price, complicated because a bidder wanted not one license but a set of them, covering a region or a band, the worth of each depending on securing the others. Early methods — lotteries, beauty contests — gave the spectrum away and allocated it badly. Auctions replaced them, and the design proved everything: Paul Milgrom and his colleagues built for the American regulator a simultaneous multiple-round auction, selling all the licenses at once in successive rounds so that bidders could assemble their packages and the prices could find their level together. Where the design was good, fortunes were raised and the spectrum went to those who would use it best; where it was poor, bidders colluded by signaling through the trailing digits of their bids, or the winners, caught by the curse, paid sums that crippled them, as some did in the European auctions of the year 2000. Same spectrum, same firms, same competition — and the rules made the difference between a triumph and a fiasco. No clearer demonstration exists that the design of a market decides as much as the rivalry within it.

the edge The auction is sometimes held up as the market in its purest form — bare competition, stripped of all but the rivalry of bidders for a prize — and so it is, which is exactly why it makes the volume's point so sharply. For if anything were the natural, ruleless market, it would be the auction; and yet the auction is nothing but rules. Whether bids ascend or descend, whether they are open or sealed, whether the winner pays his own bid or the next bidder's, whether licenses are sold one by one or all together, whether bidders must stay active or may lurk in wait — every one of these is a design choice, and the choices decide the outcome. The revenue equivalence theorem shows that in an idealized world the format would not matter, the bidders neutralizing it by their strategies; but the idealized world does not obtain, and in the real one of common values and the winner's curse, of collusion and complementarity, the format matters enormously, as the spectrum auctions proved when good designs raised fortunes and bad ones invited gaming and ruin. The purest market turns out to be the most designed, its every outcome traceable to a rule some hand has written — and the hands that write the rules of an auction are, as ever, contested, each bidder pressing for the format that favors its position. There is no ruleless auction, and so no ruleless market, even at the very place the natural picture would have looked to find one.

Plate the Eighth · The Institutions · № XVIII

The Match

matching markets; allocation without prices; stable matching

The plate asks how do you run a market in things that must not be sold — doctors' first posts, children's schools, a stranger's kidney?

Some of the most consequential allocations a society makes cannot be left to price. We do not auction residencies to the highest-bidding doctor, or sell the seats in a good school to whoever will pay, or let a kidney go to the patient with the deepest purse — partly because we forbid it, as the next plate explores, and partly because price is simply the wrong instrument for the task. These are matching problems, in which two sides must be paired — doctors with hospitals, students with schools, patients with donors — and each side has preferences over the other that no single price can express. The market designer's response is to build a matching market, a set of rules that brings the two sides together and pairs them well, without money changing hands at all. The question is what well means here, and the answer, discovered by David Gale and Lloyd Shapley in 1962, is stability.

A matching is stable when no pair would rather have each other than the partners they were assigned — when there is no doctor and hospital, no student and school, who both prefer one another to their match and could profit by abandoning it. Stability is what makes a matching market hold together, for an unstable matching unravels: the discontented pairs strike their own bargains, offers are made earlier and earlier to lock in partners before the official market clears, and the whole arrangement collapses into the chaos that ruined many a matching market before its redesign. Gale and Shapley showed that a stable matching always exists, and gave a procedure to find it — the deferred-acceptance algorithm, in which one side proposes to its first choice, the other tentatively holds the best offer it has received and rejects the rest, the rejected propose to their next choice, and so on until the music stops. The result is always stable, and it is reached, the figure insists, with no price at any step; the allocating is done entirely by the rules.

This is not a parlor theorem but the working machinery of markets that matter. The medical residency match that places thousands of new doctors each year runs on deferred acceptance, and runs because it produces stable matchings that neither doctors nor hospitals can improve upon by defection — Alvin Roth, who diagnosed why the old match worked and redesigned it to handle married couples and other complications, made the analysis a craft. The school-choice systems of Boston and New York were rebuilt on the same foundation, replacing older mechanisms that punished honesty — that forced families to game their rankings, listing not their true favorite but the best school they thought they could get — with strategy-proof designs in which it is safe to tell the truth. And in the kidney exchange, market design reached its most humane achievement: patients with willing but incompatible donors are matched in swaps and chains, this patient's donor giving to that patient and that patient's donor giving back, so that organs which could not be sold can still be allocated, and thousands have lived who would otherwise have died. A market was built precisely where the price market is forbidden, and it saves lives by the rules alone.

A market need not run on money. Where prices are forbidden or fail, a well-made matching can still allocate — fairly, stably, and to the saving of lives — proving the market a creature of rules, not of prices.

Here the volume's thesis reaches its furthest extension, for the matching markets detach the market from the price altogether. We are inclined to think a market is essentially a place where prices clear supply and demand, so that a market without prices sounds like a contradiction; but the matching markets are markets in every sense that counts — they gather two sides, elicit their preferences, and allocate scarce things among competing claims — and they do it without money at all. What allocates, in the price's place, is the rule. The market, stripped to its essence, turns out to be not price but rule: a set of procedures for resolving competing claims to scarce things, of which the price mechanism is one species and matching another. And this enlarges both the designer's freedom and the designer's burden, for the choice is no longer how to set a price but which kind of market to build, and that choice — price or match, this priority or that, this side proposing or the other — distributes school places and life chances and even life itself.

the edge The matching markets complete the volume's case by detaching the market from the price, and in doing so reveal how much the natural picture had smuggled in. We assume a market is a place where prices clear supply and demand, so that a market without prices sounds absurd; but the matching markets are markets in every sense that matters — bringing two sides together, eliciting their preferences, allocating scarce things among competing claims — and they do it without money changing hands. What does the allocating, in the price's stead, is the rule: the deferred-acceptance procedure that finds a stable matching, the priorities that decide whose claim ranks first, the algorithm that arranges the chain of donations. This is the market reduced to its essence, which proves to be not price but rule — a set of procedures for resolving competing claims to scarce things, of which price is one species and matching another. And once the market is seen this way, the designer's freedom and responsibility both expand, for the question is no longer merely how to set a price but which kind of market to build at all, and that choice distributes life chances and even life itself. The kidney exchange saves a patient the price market would have let die; the school mechanism sends a child to one future rather than another. To design these markets is to hold a power the natural picture never imagined a market to contain, and to wield it, inescapably, on behalf of some answer to the question of who deserves what.

Plate the Ninth · The Institutions · № XVIII

The Repugnant

the moral limits of markets; what should not be bought and sold

The plate asks granted that we can design a market for almost anything — for what should we refuse to?

The matching markets of the last plate existed because a prior decision had been made: that kidneys must not be sold. This points to a question the design of markets cannot answer from within, for before one asks how to build a market one must ask whether to build it at all — and every society answers no for some things. We do not sell votes, or verdicts, or persons; we do not, in most places, sell organs, or babies, or the right to immigrate, or a place in the queue for a transplant. The boundary of the market — the line between what may be bought and sold and what may not — is as much a designed and contested thing as any rule within the market, and it is where the design of markets meets the limits of the moral. Alvin Roth, the same engineer who built the kidney exchange, gave the economist's name to the things that fall outside the line: repugnant transactions, exchanges that some wish to make and others find so objectionable that they insist on forbidding them, and which the designer must therefore treat as a fixed constraint, building around the prohibition rather than against it.

Repugnance is not a fixed fact of nature but a moving verdict of culture, which is the first sign that the boundary is chosen rather than given. Lending money at interest was once repugnant, condemned as usury for centuries, as The Rate recalled, and is now the ordinary business of every bank. Life insurance was once repugnant, a scandalous wager on a human death, and is now a pillar of prudence. Slavery, the buying and selling of persons, was for most of history accepted and is now the very emblem of what must never be a market. The line has moved in both directions and moves still — over surrogacy, over the sale of data, over the marketing of what was lately sacred — and its motion proves it a human decision, made and remade, rather than a discovery of where markets naturally stop. To ask what should not be sold is therefore to ask a question every generation must answer afresh, and answer not by consulting economics but by consulting itself.

Two objections recur whenever the market presses against a boundary, and Michael Sandel gathered them in his account of what money should not buy. The first is the objection from fairness, or coercion: an exchange is truly voluntary only between parties roughly equal in power, and in an unequal world the free sale of a kidney, a womb, an hour of intimacy may be the desperation of the poor wearing the mask of the choice of the free. Where one party acts from need and the other from surplus, the market in such things becomes a one-way traffic, from the bodies of the poor to the comfort of the rich, and its voluntariness is a fiction the inequality belies. The second objection is deeper and stranger: that a price is not a neutral measure laid over a thing but an act that can change what the thing is. Some goods are corrupted by being priced — a friendship one pays for is not friendship, an honor one buys is not an honor, a civic duty discharged for money is something other than a duty. The market does not merely allocate these goods; it degrades them, dissolving the very meaning that made them worth having.

The second objection has an economic edge sharp enough to wound the economist's faith that a market can only help, and Richard Titmuss first drew blood with it. Comparing the wholly voluntary blood-donation system of Britain with the partly paid system of America, Titmuss argued that paying for blood produced not more of it but less, and worse — for the payment crowded out the altruism that had moved people to give, converting a gift into a transaction and so destroying the civic motive on which the better and the more plentiful blood had depended, while drawing the needy to conceal the ailments that made their blood unsafe. The phenomenon, since confirmed many times, is motivational crowding out: introduce a price where a moral motive had governed, and the price can extinguish the motive and leave less of the desired thing than before. The daycare that fined parents for collecting their children late found lateness increase, not fall, because the fine had turned a moral lapse into a purchased service that parents felt newly entitled to buy. The market signal, in these cases, drives out the moral signal it was meant to reinforce, and the thing the market touched is diminished by the touch.

A price is not only a measure of a thing but an act upon it. Some goods survive the pricing; others — the gift, the duty, the sacred — are corrupted by it, and a wise society guards the difference.

So the boundary of the market is drawn where economics runs out and a society must decide what among its goods it will hold above price. And the decision is genuinely hard, with casualties on both sides of every line, which is why it is a contest and not a calculation. To marketize too freely is to invite the coercion, the corruption, and the crowding out that the objections name; but to forbid too freely has its own dead, for the prohibition on selling kidneys leaves thousands to die on the waiting list whom a regulated market might have saved, and the ban that seems to honor a good often breeds the black market that dishonors it. The economist who treats every refusal to marketize as a failure of nerve mistakes the map for the territory; the moralist who treats every market as a defilement forgets the shortage and the black market and the real gains exchange can bring. Where the line falls — around the organ, the vote, the verdict, the child — is the deepest design choice of all, for it decides not how a market shall work but what shall be a market at all, and in deciding that, a society decides what kind of society it means to be.

the edge The economist's instinct is to expand the market, and the instinct is not foolish — voluntary exchange does benefit both parties, the kidney shortage does kill people the law's squeamishness might have saved, the prohibition does breed the black market. But the repugnance he is tempted to dismiss as mere squeamishness encodes two insights he cannot afford to ignore. The first is that exchange is voluntary only among equals, so that in an unequal world the free sale of a kidney or a womb or an hour of intimacy may be the desperation of the poor dressed as the choice of the free, a one-way traffic from the bodies of the needy to the comfort of the rich. The second is that a price is not a neutral measure laid over a thing but an act that can change what the thing is — that paying for blood can poison the well of altruism that gave the better blood, that fining the latecomer can convert a moral lapse into a purchased service and so multiply it, that buying the honor or the friendship or the civic duty corrupts the very good it sought. The economist who treats every refusal to marketize as a failure of nerve mistakes the map for the territory; but the moralist who treats every market as a defilement forgets the shortage and the real gains exchange can bring. The boundary between what may be sold and what may not is a genuine contest, with casualties on both sides of every line, and a society draws it not by consulting a fact but by deciding, again and again, what among its goods it will hold above price — which is to say, what kind of society it means to be.

Plate the Tenth · The Institutions · № XVIII

The Protocol

standards, protocols, and benchmarks; the hidden rules; network lock-in

The plate asks if the deepest rules of a market are the technical ones no one votes on, who writes them — and why can a worse one win?

The rules examined so far are the visible law — the property, the contract, the antitrust, the regulation that are debated and enacted in the open. Beneath them runs another layer, technical and largely unseen, that governs exchange as powerfully and is written by scarcely anyone the public could name: the standards, the protocols, and the benchmarks. A standard fixes what a thing is and what it may join to — the meter and the kilogram, without which no quantity could be honestly traded; the gauge of a railway, the voltage of a current; the dimensions of the shipping container, a plain steel box that remade the geography and labor of world trade by making cargo interchangeable; and, most consequentially of all, the accounting standards that decide what shall count as a profit, an asset, a liability, and so shape every judgment an investor makes. A protocol fixes how parties shall communicate — the protocols of the internet, set by committees the world has never heard of, on which the whole digital economy rests. A benchmark fixes a reference against which all else is priced — the interest rate, the index, the credit rating to which a fair share of global finance is tied.

These are rules in the fullest sense, for they structure what is possible, decide who may trade with whom, and settle what words as load-bearing as profit shall mean — and yet they wear the dress of mere technique, and so escape the scrutiny the visible law attracts. To choose an accounting standard is to choose what a corporation may hide and what it must reveal, a choice fought over by the very firms it governs. To choose a measurement standard can be to favor one nation's industry over another's. To set a protocol is to govern all who would build upon it. The work is done in obscure bodies far from any ballot — the standards organizations, the protocol committees, the benchmark panels — and the rules they make bind everyone who would take part in the markets they structure.

What gives standards their peculiar power, and their peculiar danger, is the economics of adoption the figure draws. A standard is worth more to each user the more others use it — a telephone is useless if no one else owns one, a language valuable only as others speak it, a standard worth adopting chiefly because others have adopted it already. This is increasing returns to adoption, and it works a strange logic. Two standards may begin nearly even, but let one gain a slight early advantage and the advantage feeds upon itself: each new adopter makes the leader more attractive to the next, the lead compounds, and at a tipping point the market swings hard to the leader, which climbs to dominance while its rival falls to nothing. The disquieting consequence is that the standard which wins need not be the best. Because the outcome turns on early momentum rather than merit, a small accident of history — who reached the market first, who struck the lucky alliance — can fasten a market upon a standard inferior to one it might have had, and hold it there, since to switch to the better one all must move at once, and none will move first. Brian Arthur and others showed how increasing returns breed this path dependence, in which history and not efficiency decides: a failure of the market peculiar to the very rules that coordinate it.

The most powerful rules of the market are the ones no one votes on — the standard, the protocol, the benchmark — and the worse of two may win, then hold for a century, by no virtue but the luck of arriving first.

And because the standard, once locked in, is a rule that all must follow, the power to set it ranks among the deepest in the economy, and the contest to set it is fierce in proportion. Standards are set by governments, by international bodies, by industry consortia that the largest firms dominate, and by the platforms that decree the terms for all who build upon them; and whoever writes the standard can write it to favor himself — the firm whose patent the standard requires collects a toll from every user, the nation whose technology becomes the global standard lifts its own industries, the platform that sets the terms governs all the rest. The benchmark is the starkest case of all. The London rate to which hundreds of trillions of dollars of contracts were tied was set, astonishingly, by a panel of banks reporting their own estimated borrowing costs — and when it emerged that those banks had for years falsified their reports to profit their own trading positions, the world learned that a rule pricing a vast share of global finance had been quietly written in the interest of the few who reported it. The benchmark had to be torn out by the root and replaced. A rule that governed everything had belonged, in the end, to those it most enriched.

the edge The hidden rules expose a flaw in the comforting belief that markets at least select the better thing, for in the presence of network effects they may do the opposite, fastening upon the worse and holding it past all hope of dislodging. The belief is sound where each buyer chooses independently of the rest: there the superior product wins, because each buyer, weighing it alone, prefers it. But where the value of a choice depends on how many others have made it — as with every standard, protocol, and benchmark — the independence fails, and the guarantee fails with it. Now the first to gather a crowd may win regardless of merit, the crowd itself becoming the reason to join it, and a market may lock into a standard that no one, choosing freely and together, would have picked. The cost is paid quietly and for a long time, in the slightly worse keyboard, the incompatible system, the unit that confuses, the benchmark that misleads — each a small tax that an early accident levies on the present. And the remedy is hard, for to escape a bad standard the whole market must move at once, the very thing network effects forbid. The lesson is not that standards are bad, for they are indispensable and trade without them unthinkable, but that the rules which coordinate a market are exactly the rules the market is worst at choosing — so that here, more than anywhere, the design cannot be left to the market, but must be tended by some hand that looks past the moment of adoption to the century it will bind.

Plate the Eleventh · The Institutions · № XVIII

The Author

who writes the rules; the deepest economic power; in whose interest

The plate asks if the market is written, then who holds the pen — and why does it matter more than who holds the monopoly?

The volume has shown the market to be built, maintained by rules, captured, engineered, bounded by the moral, and structured by hidden standards; and through all of it has run a single question, which now demands a direct answer. Who writes the rules? The answer is not one author but many, working in many venues. The legislatures and the courts write the visible law of property, contract, and antitrust. The regulators and their agencies write the administrative rules, and sometimes, as the fifth plate showed, write them at the dictation of the regulated. The standards bodies and protocol committees and benchmark panels write the hidden rules the tenth plate uncovered. And increasingly the rules are written above the nation altogether, in the international bodies that set the terms of cross-border trade and the capital that banks must hold and the standards by which the world's accounts are kept — venues more remote from any electorate than the national ones, and harder still to see. To these are added the dominant firms that legislate for their own platforms, decreeing the terms on which all who trade upon them must trade, and the lobbyists and lawyers who draft, on behalf of whoever retains them, the language that the official authors adopt.

What these scattered authors share is not a conspiracy but a structure, and it is the structure the fifth plate named. In every venue where rules are written, the organized and the interested are present and the diffuse public is not. Those with a fortune riding on a rule will master its details, fund its drafting, and attend its making; those who will bear the rule's cost a penny at a time will never learn that it was made. No one need plot for the outcome to tilt, for it tilts of its own weight, by the asymmetry between the concentrated few who care intensely and the scattered many who cannot even afford to notice. And the tilt grows steeper as the rules ascend to the international bodies, where the public's eye reaches least and the organized interest reaches most, and steeper again through the revolving door by which the writers of rules pass to the service of those the rules govern, and back. The apparatus of rule-writing is, at every level, canted toward those equipped to work it.

Here is the volume's deepest claim about power, and it reorders the table once more. The power to set a price within the rules — the market power that The Power anatomized — is real, but it is the shallower power. The deeper power is the power to write the rules, for whoever writes them sets the terms of every price struck within them: what may be owned, who may enter, what competition shall mean, what may be sold and what forbidden, which standard shall bind. The monopolist who charges a high price has power over his customers; the interest that writes the rule under which he charges has power over the whole market, over his rivals, over the customers of them all. And the two powers differ not only in reach but in visibility, which is the most dangerous thing about the deeper one. The monopolist is visible, and resented, and watched; the rule-writer is invisible, and unaccountable, and works in rooms the price-taker never finds. The true contest of a market economy is not the visible competition for customers but the hidden competition to write the rules — and it is waged by those organized enough to enter the arena, which is rarely the public.

The monopolist who sets a high price is visible, and resented. The interest that writes the rule under which he sets it is invisible, and unaccountable — and holds the deeper power by far.

This is not a counsel of despair, for some rules are written well and in the public's interest, and the writing of rules requires a skill and an expertise that cannot be wished away; markets need drafters, and good ones. But it poses the deepest political question the series has reached. The rules of the market are made, and the making is a form of power, and that power is largely exercised beyond the sight and the control of those it binds. A society that does not see its rules as made cannot govern their making — and here the natural picture of the market does its gravest harm, for by presenting the market as found rather than written it conceals the very thing that most needs democratic attention, the authorship of the rules, and so disarms the public before the contest has even begun. To denaturalize the market is therefore not merely an analytical correction but a democratic one. So long as the market is believed to be discovered and not designed, its authorship goes unexamined, and the deepest power in the economy is wielded, by default, in the dark. The first step toward governing the making of the rules is the recognition that they are made — and that someone, somewhere, in some interest, is even now at work upon them.

the edge It is tempting to answer the question of who writes the rules with a single villain — the corporation, the lobby, the state — but the truth is at once more diffuse and more troubling. The rules are written in a thousand venues, by legislatures and courts and agencies, by standards bodies and benchmark panels and international committees, by the firms that legislate for their platforms and the lawyers who draft for whoever retains them; and what these venues share is not a plot but a structure, in which the organized and the interested attend and the diffuse public does not. No one need conspire for the outcome to tilt; it tilts of itself, because those with a fortune riding on a rule will master its details while those who bear its cost by the penny never know it was made. The remedy, such as it is, is not to find the villain but to alter the structure — to drag the rule-writing into the light, to fund the representation of the diffuse interest, to make the obscure venues accountable — and the first step toward any of it is simply to see the rules as written, which the natural picture forbids. For so long as the market is believed to be found and not made, its authorship goes unexamined, and the deepest power in the economy is exercised by default in the dark. To denaturalize the market is the precondition of governing the making of its rules: one cannot take up a contest one cannot see.

Plate the Twelfth · The Institutions · № XVIII

The Design

the market as a designed institution; the rules as the deepest verdict

The plate asks if the market itself is made, what becomes of every price struck within it — and of the argument this whole table has pressed?

The volume may now gather what its plates have shown, and in gathering it complete the argument the table has pressed from its first page. A market is not a fact of nature but a made thing — an institution, a structure of rules, built and maintained by deliberate choice, and collapsing into force the moment the rules are withdrawn. The free market is not the absence of rules but a particular set of them; there is no market prior to the law that constitutes it, and no natural baseline of exchange from which the rules are mere departures. To see this is to be cured of the deepest illusion in economic thought, the treating of one familiar arrangement of rules as nature herself, and to be returned to the only real question, which is never whether to have rules but which rules to have, and for whom.

And the rules, the volume has insisted throughout, are a verdict on a contest — not over goods or prices, but over the writing of the rules themselves. What may be owned, and who shall hold it; how competition shall be maintained, and by whom policed; what the captured agency permits and the public-spirited one forbids; where the moral boundary of the market shall fall; which hidden standard shall lock the market in — each is a choice, each distributive, each fought over by those with the means and the interest to fight, who are seldom the public. The contest to write the rules is the deepest contest in the economy, because whoever prevails in it shapes every price struck within the rules he has written.

This completes the argument. We began by denaturalizing the prices struck within markets, showing each — the wage, the rent, the rate, the rest — to be a verdict on a hidden contest rather than a fact of nature. We end by denaturalizing the market itself, the supposed natural stage on which those contests were played, and find it too a made thing, its rules a verdict on a prior contest over their authorship. So every price is doubly a verdict: a verdict struck within a market, and struck within rules that are themselves the settled outcome of a contest over who should write them. The invisible hand, where it works, works inside a structure of visible rules that some hand has written — and to ask who wrote them, and in whose interest, is to reach the deepest level at which the contests of this whole table are fought, the level beneath all the others, where the terms of every bargain are set before a single bargain is struck.

The recurring discovery, the one the gloss promised, has been that the design of a market matters as much as the competition within it. The same bidders, under different rules, hand the seller a fortune or a ruin; the same patients, under different designs, are matched to a kidney or left to die; the same market may lock into the better standard or the worse, by the accident of which arrived first. The competition is real, and its discipline precious, and nothing here has denied it. But the rules decide what the competition can achieve, and a market badly designed will fail however fierce the rivalry within it, while a market well designed accomplishes what no unaided exchange could reach. The rules are not a frame around the contest; they are a determinant of it, and often the larger one.

The volume has shown the rules in two lights and held them both. In the one, the rules are captured — written by the organized few for their own advantage, the referee pulled toward the players, the benchmark rigged, the standard locked, the deepest power exercised in the dark. In the other, the rules are engineered — written well, by skilled and public-spirited hands, to build the markets that save lives and allocate fairly what no price could. Which light prevails is fixed by nothing; it is itself the perpetual contest, the struggle to keep the writing of the rules honest and turned toward the public, a struggle never finally won. The design of markets is neither a conspiracy nor a science wholly benign, but a contested craft, and its direction is always at stake.

To its neighbors and its kin the volume hands what it has drawn. To The Power it gives the distinction between the market power that sets a price within the rules and the deeper power that writes them. To The Rent it returns the enclosure and the patent, the property conjured by rule that yields the unearned. To The Commons it offers the rules of property and regulation as the answers, well or badly drawn, to the tragedy of open access. To The Contract, its neighbor in this group, it hands the law of enforceable promise on which all exchange in time depends; to The Money, the medium the rules must constitute and sustain; to The Tax, the fiscal rules and the public revenue that the design of markets at once requires and reshapes. And to the Failures it hands the regulation by which a society answers them — the rules that correct, when they are not captured, the externality and the asymmetry and the power that the market alone would leave uncorrected.

The market, then, is the grandest of the institutions, the frame within which the firm and the contract and the money do their work, and like them it is made. Its rules are written; the writing is the deepest exercise of economic power there is; and the writing could always have gone otherwise. To design the market is to design the economy — to decide what may be owned and traded, who may enter and on what terms, what competition shall mean and what shall lie beyond all price — and the design is no more given by nature than any price within it. The hand that writes the rules is not invisible; it is only unwatched. The argument remains open, because the question of who shall write the rules of the market, and in whose interest, is never settled but re-fought wherever a rule is made — in the legislature, the agency, the standards body, the court — the perpetual labor of a people deciding, and deciding again, what kind of market, and so what kind of economy, and so what kind of common life, it means to have.
