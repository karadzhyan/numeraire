what one side cannot know

The question of the volume How does a market survive when the seller knows what the buyer cannot?

in print · twelve plates
Numéraire · Volume XII

The Argument

The classical picture of a market rests, often without saying so, on a quiet assumption about knowledge: that the two parties to a trade understand what they are trading, or that the price will tell them what they need to know. The invisible hand guides the baker and his customer to a good outcome because each can judge the loaf. But a great many of the most important trades are not like the buying of bread. The buyer of a used car cannot see what the seller knows; the insurer cannot see the health the applicant knows; the lender cannot see the borrower's true intentions, nor the employer the worker's true diligence. This volume is the study of trade conducted across a gap in knowledge — of what happens to a market when one side knows something the other cannot — and it is the work of the economics of information, the body of theory that earned its founders a Nobel and humbled the classical model at its root.

The gap comes in two forms, and the distinction organizes everything that follows. There is hidden type — a fixed characteristic one party knows and the other does not: the car is a peach or a lemon, the applicant is hale or ailing, the worker is able or not, and the type is settled before any deal is struck. And there is hidden action — a choice one party takes after the deal, unseen by the other: the insured drives carelessly, the borrower gambles the loan, the manager shirks at his desk. The first is a problem of selection, of who comes forward to trade; the second a problem of behavior, of what they do once protected. Hidden type breeds the failure called adverse selection; hidden action breeds the failure called moral hazard; and the two, between them, account for a vast territory the classical model could not map.

The deeper of the two discoveries is that hidden information does not merely inconvenience a market — it can unravel one entirely. Akerlof's parable of the lemons showed that when buyers cannot tell good from bad and will pay only for the average, the sellers of the good withdraw, the average falls, the price falls with it, and more good withdraws, in a spiral that can empty the market of everything but the worst, or empty it altogether. A market in which every possible trade would have benefited both parties can simply fail to exist, because no one can prove which trades are the good ones. This is a market failure as grave as any monopoly or any externality, and subtler than either: nothing is missing, no one has power, and yet the gains from trade go unrealized for want of a thing that cannot be bought — the buyer's knowledge of what he is buying.

Hidden action works its damage differently but no less surely. Shield a person from the consequences of his choices and leave those choices unseen, and his care will slacken in a direction one can predict: the fully insured take more risk, the rescued bank gambles, the agent paid a flat wage exerts the least effort he can. The classical remedy — write a contract — runs at once into the wall this volume keeps meeting, for a contract can reward only what can be seen, and effort cannot be seen. To motivate the agent, the principal must tie his pay to the outcome; but the outcome is buffeted by luck the agent does not control, so to load his pay with incentive is to load it with risk, and risk must be paid for. Every real contract is a compromise on this trade-off, buying as much effort as it can afford in the coin of the risk it imposes — and the compromise is never the frictionless ideal the model assumed.

Yet the market does not simply lie down before the gap; it is ingenious, and much of this volume is the record of its ingenuity. The party who knows will try to prove it, by an action the ignorant party can trust precisely because only the genuine type would find it worth taking — a signal, in Spence's sense, of which a costly education is the great example. The party who does not know will try to make the other reveal it, by offering a menu of terms so designed that each type, choosing what suits it, sorts itself — a screen, in Stiglitz's sense, of which the insurer's deductible is the everyday instance. And where neither signal nor screen will serve, the market may abandon the price as its instrument altogether and turn instead to quantity, rationing what it cannot safely sell — as the lender, unable to raise the rate without drawing the reckless, simply turns the borrower away. Signals, screens, and rationing are the devices a market builds to trade across a gap it cannot close.

Around these private devices stands a wider scaffolding, some of it grown and some of it built. Reputation disciplines the seller who must return tomorrow; certification and the intermediary supply the judgment the buyer lacks; the warranty bonds the maker's word in money; and where these private remedies fail or are too weak, the state steps in with mandated disclosure, with licensing, with the law that compels the knowing to tell. How far the market's own contrivances suffice, and where the state must supply what they cannot, is among the most contested questions the subject raises — the more so as a new technology of information, the inspection report and the rating and the public review, dissolves some asymmetries the older institutions were built to bridge.

Beneath all of it lies a reckoning that reaches past any single market to the foundations of the discipline. If the efficiency of markets depends on the symmetry of information, and information is almost never symmetric, then efficiency is the special case and failure the general one — markets with imperfect and asymmetric information, as Greenwald and Stiglitz showed, are essentially never efficient in the way the older theory promised. And information itself turns out to be the strangest of goods: costly to produce, hard to keep once sold, and impossible to value before one has it, so that the market for knowledge is itself among the failed markets, and the question of how much to know has no clean answer. The Asymmetry is the failure that recognizes the plainest truth about markets and the one the model most often forgets — that they run on knowledge, which is unequally held, costly to share, and never complete. It advances the family of The Failures, and reaches across the table to the warranty of the contract, the agent of the firm, the private types of the game, and the rationed credit of the rate. The argument remains open.

Plate the First · The Failures · № XII

The Secret

asymmetric information; hidden type and hidden action; the failure of knowing

The plate asks what goes wrong in a trade when one party knows what the other cannot?

The theorems that crown the classical model — that a competitive equilibrium wastes nothing, that the invisible hand turns private greed to public good — carry a clause so quiet it is easy to miss: that everyone knows what they need to know. The buyer can judge the good, the lender the borrower, the insurer the risk; or if they cannot, the price stands proxy for the knowledge, summing into a single number all that the market has learned. Strike out that clause, and the theorems fall. This volume studies the trades for which the clause was always false — where one party holds, privately, some fact about the bargain that the other cannot see and that changes what the bargain is worth. Such information is called asymmetric, and the asymmetry is not a minor friction to be assumed away. It is a wedge driven into the foundations.

The wedge takes two shapes, and to keep them distinct is to hold the whole subject in order. The first is hidden type: a fixed fact about a party, settled before any deal, that the other cannot observe. The used car is sound or rotten and the seller knows which; the applicant is healthy or ailing and carries the knowledge in his body; the worker is diligent or idle by disposition. The type exists at the door, and the question is who will choose to come through it. The second is hidden action: a choice a party makes after the deal is struck, which the other cannot watch. The insured, once covered, locks his bicycle or does not; the borrower, once funded, builds the prudent business or stakes it on a long shot; the manager, once hired, labors or loafs. The action comes after the signature, and the question is what the protected party will do. From hidden type grows the failure of adverse selection, a sickness in who trades; from hidden action grows the failure of moral hazard, a sickness in how they behave once trading. One root — the unobservable — two diseases, striking at different hours and yielding to different cures.

That this belongs among the failures, and not among the ordinary frictions every market carries, is worth insisting on. Here is a market that may be perfectly competitive, crowded with buyers and sellers, no monopolist in sight and no cost spilling onto a bystander — and it fails all the same. Trades that would enrich both parties do not occur, because the gains cannot be proven; or the trades that do occur are the wrong ones, the bad driving out the good. The waste is real and the cause is singular: not too few sellers, not too much power, not a missing market for some side effect, but knowledge held unequally across the bargain. It is a failure of information, and it is distinctive in one hopeful respect that the later plates will dwell on — that unlike a monopoly, which has no wish to dissolve itself, a market crippled by asymmetry strains constantly to cure itself, breeding signals and screens and reputations in its own defense.

One market, perfectly competitive, no monopolist and no spillover — and it fails regardless, because the buyer cannot tell the good loaf from the bad. The failure is not of power or of number. It is of knowing.

Economics was slow to admit the wedge, having built its rigor on a fiction of full information — the auctioneer who knew every value, the agent who knew every fact, the price that revealed all. The economics of information, arriving in a remarkable burst around nineteen-seventy, brought the gap inside the model and showed how much the older results had leaned on the assumption that abolished it: Akerlof's lemons, Spence's signals, the screening and the credit rationing that Stiglitz and his collaborators drew out of the same insight. The work earned its authors the Nobel three decades on, and remade labor economics, finance, insurance, and the economics of development in its image. The discipline had, in effect, discovered that the most interesting thing about a market is often not the price but what the price fails to say.

the edge The danger of so powerful an idea is that it explains too much. Some asymmetry attends nearly every trade — the seller always knows his wares a little better than the buyer, the worker his own effort, the borrower his own plans — so that a careless theorist can wave at hidden information to account for any market feature whatever, declaring that this institution or that exists because of asymmetric information, with no independent way to check the claim. The concept earns its keep only when disciplined by the question of when the asymmetry actually bites: it bites when the hidden fact is large beside what can be seen, when sorting the types is genuinely hard, when the stakes are high and the dealing is one-shot; it fades to nothing when the gap is small, when verification is cheap, when the parties meet again and again. And there is a corrective the bare theory tends to forget: markets knew about the gap long before economists named it, and had been building around it for centuries — the guild that vouched for its members, the merchant who guarded a reputation, the warranty older than any model of it. To measure a real market, thick with these contrivances, against a frictionless ideal of perfect information and pronounce it a failure is to indict it for falling short of a world that has never existed and was never on offer. The asymmetry is real; whether, in any given market, it is a crippling failure or a managed nuisance is a question the theory poses but cannot answer from its armchair.

Plate the Second · The Failures · № XII

The Lemon

adverse selection; the market for lemons; how hidden quality unravels trade

The plate asks if buyers cannot tell a good car from a bad one, what happens to the good cars?

Consider the market that gave the failure its homely name. A man wishes to sell his used car, and he knows, as only an owner can, whether it is sound or a wreck — a peach or, in the American idiom that became the technical term, a lemon. The buyer knows no such thing. He sees a car of a certain make and age and mileage, and behind that visible surface a quality he cannot read. What, then, will he pay? Not the price of a peach, for the car before him might be a lemon; not the price of a lemon, for it might be a peach. He will pay, if he is sensible, the price warranted by the average — by his best guess of what the typical car offered at this price is worth. It is the only price his ignorance permits.

And here the trouble begins, in a turn of reasoning that is the engine of the whole subject. At a price set by the average, the owner of a car better than average will not sell. His peach is worth more to him than the averaged price on offer; he keeps it, or asks more and finds no taker. So the cars that remain on the market are the below-average ones — and the average quality of what is actually offered falls. The buyer, no fool, anticipates this and lowers what he will pay; which drives out the next tier of cars, those just above the new and lower average; which lowers the average again; which lowers the price again. The market does not settle. It unravels, each turn of the screw expelling the best of what remains, until in the limit only the worst is left to trade — the lemons, alone, as the name foretold — or until no price will support any trade at all and the market simply vanishes.

The result is more startling than any deadweight triangle. It is not that a few trades at the margin go unmade; it is that an entire market, in which every conceivable trade would have left both parties better off, can fail to exist. The buyer values each car above what its owner asks — gains from trade lie everywhere — and yet, because no one can prove which cars are which, none of the gains are reaped. Hidden quality has done what no monopolist and no tax could do: it has destroyed the market outright. And the mechanism is general. It is George Akerlof's lemons of nineteen-seventy, but its shadow falls across every market where one side knows a quality the other cannot: the insurance pool the healthy abandon, the credit market the safe borrowers flee, the labor market the able workers quit when all are paid the same averaged wage. Wherever a hidden type sorts the eager from the reluctant, the lemons logic is at work.

The good is driven out by the bad — not at the margin, but altogether. A market in which every trade would have enriched both parties can simply cease to exist, for want of a thing no one can buy: the buyer's knowledge of what he is buying.

It is worth marking exactly what does the damage, because the cure must be aimed at it. The damage is not done by low quality; a market of nothing but lemons, openly known to be lemons, would function perfectly well, clearing at a lemon's price. The damage is done by hidden quality — by the buyer's inability to distinguish, which forces him to price by the average and so to punish the good along with the bad. It follows that every remedy this volume will examine is, at bottom, an attempt to make quality visible: to let the peach prove itself a peach, by a warranty its owner can afford and a lemon's owner cannot, by a reputation built over many sales, by an inspection the buyer trusts. The lemons market is not a market without hope. It is a market under pressure to invent the institutions that will save it.

the edge Read as a prophecy, the lemons model is plainly too gloomy, and the proof is all around us: the markets it condemns to collapse — used cars above all — are vast, thronged, and durable. The full unraveling is the extreme case, reached only under assumptions the bare parable makes and the world does not honor. Buyers are not wholly blind; they kick the tires, hire a mechanic, pull the vehicle's history from a database that did not exist when Akerlof wrote. Sellers are not anonymous; the dealer has a name to protect and a franchise to lose. And the very devices the previous paragraph anticipated — the warranty, the certified-pre-owned label, the right of return — are woven through every real market in the goods whose quality is hard to see. The honest reading is not that markets with hidden quality collapse, but that adverse selection is a force, real and directional, pushing toward collapse, against which markets array a counter-force of institutions; the observed market is the resultant. How strong the lemons force actually is — how large the discount on a car the moment it is driven off the lot, how much the average quality of what is offered really falls — is an empirical question with mixed and market-specific answers, not a theorem to be asserted. The model identifies the disease. It does not establish that the patient dies.

The Pool

adverse selection in insurance; the death spiral; the mandate and the risk class

The plate asks if chiefly the sick want health insurance, what must it cost to insure anyone?

Insurance is the native soil of adverse selection — the term is the insurers' own, older than the economics that formalized it — and the mechanism there wears its plainest face. To insure is to pool risk: the many who pay premiums cover the few who suffer losses, and each buys, for a small certain cost, escape from a large uncertain one. The arrangement works beautifully when the insurer can see the risk he is taking on. It begins to fail the moment the applicant knows his own risk better than the insurer does — which, for health, for life, for the soundness of a venture, is nearly always.

For then the eagerness to buy is itself a signal, and a damning one. The person who most wants health insurance is, on average, the person who most expects to use it — the one who feels the symptom the insurer cannot see. So the pool that actually forms is sicker than the population at large: selection has worked adversely, drawing in the bad risks and leaving out the good. The insurer, finding his pool more expensive than the average citizen, must raise the premium to cover it. But at the higher premium, the healthiest of his customers — those whose expected claims fall well below what they are now charged — conclude that the policy is no longer worth its price, and leave. Their departure makes the remaining pool sicker still; the premium must rise again; the next tier of the relatively healthy departs; and the market spirals, in the insurers' own grim phrase, toward death. At the end of the spiral sits a small pool of the very sickest, paying premiums near their own high expected losses, while the great middle of the population — moderate risks who would gladly have bought insurance at a fair price — go uncovered for want of a pool they could afford to join.

The diagram makes precise what the spiral does in time. The peculiarity of insurance, which no ordinary market shares, is that the cost of serving a customer depends on which customer it is — and the price, by determining who chooses to buy, determines the cost. So the cost curves slope downward: widen coverage by lowering the premium, and the people you draw in are progressively healthier, pulling the cost of the marginal customer down, and the average of the pool down behind it. Competition drives the premium to the average cost of whoever has chosen to insure; but the efficient extent of coverage is set by the marginal cost — by whether the next person values insurance above what it costs to cover him, not above the average dragged up by the sick. Because average cost sits above marginal cost, the premium sits above the efficient price, and a wedge of people who would have been worth insuring are priced out. That wedge is the signature of the failure: not collapse, usually, but chronic under-insurance.

The eagerness to buy is itself the bad news. The one who most wants the policy is the one who most expects to claim — so the pool that forms is always sicker than the crowd it formed from, and the premium that covers it drives the healthy out.

Knowing the disease, one can read the institutions of insurance as so many treatments for it. The mandate — the requirement that everyone hold coverage — abolishes the selection at a stroke by denying the healthy their exit; it is the logic behind compulsory auto insurance and the contested individual mandate of health reform. Risk classification — sorting applicants by observable markers of risk, age and history and occupation, and pricing each class on its own — shrinks the asymmetry within each pool by making more of the hidden type visible. The group policy sold through an employer pools people selected by their jobs rather than their health. Waiting periods, exclusions, and medical underwriting all gnaw at the same problem from different sides. The architecture is elaborate because the force it resists is strong; an insurance market, like the lemons market, survives by inventing the devices that hold its pool together.

the edge Each treatment carries a cost the diagram does not show, and the deepest of them cuts to the purpose of insurance itself. The mandate forces the low-risk to buy a policy worth less to them than its price, which is to say it makes them subsidize the high-risk — a redistribution that may be humane but should be named, not smuggled in under the banner of efficiency, and a compulsion that has its own price in liberty. Risk classification is stranger still, for pursued to its limit it destroys the thing it was meant to save: an insurer who could read every applicant's risk perfectly would charge each his own expected loss, and a premium equal to your expected loss is not insurance at all — it pools nothing, shares nothing, and leaves the genuinely high-risk, the chronically ill and the genetically unlucky, facing ruinous prices or no offer at all. Perfect information would end insurance as a form of solidarity. This is why we deliberately forbid some classification — barring the use of genetic tests, requiring that pre-existing conditions be covered, mandating community rating — choosing to tolerate a measure of adverse selection in order to preserve access, and accepting the spiral's pressure as the price of the pool's reach. And the empirical ground is less firm than the theory: in some markets the selection runs the other way, the cautious souls who buy the most insurance being also the ones who take the most care and claim the least, so that the pool is better than average and the spiral never starts. Whether a given market suffers adverse selection, advantageous selection, or neither is a question for the data, not the blackboard.

The Hazard

moral hazard; the hidden action; the deductible, the co-pay, the monitor

The plate asks once a loss is someone else's to bear, how carefully will you trouble to avoid it?

The lemons and the pool were failures of hidden type — of a fact fixed before the deal. We turn now to the second great failure, which springs from hidden action — a choice made after the deal, and unseen. Its name, borrowed like adverse selection from the insurance trade, is moral hazard, and its principle is one of the most reliable in all of economics: shield a person from the consequences of his actions, leave those actions unobserved, and his care will slacken in a direction one can name in advance. The insured against theft grows careless with his locks. The bank that knows it will be rescued reaches for riskier bets. The employee paid a flat salary, his effort unwatched, exerts rather less of it. In each case the party who bears the loss is not the party who controls the risk, and the wedge between them — the gap the contract cannot close, because effort cannot be seen — is where the waste accumulates.

The hazard comes in two tenses. Before the loss, it dulls prevention: the covered party takes fewer pains to keep the bad event from happening, since its cost will fall on another. After the loss, it swells consumption: the party whose bills are paid by the insurer uses more of whatever the insurer is buying, since it is free to him at the point of use. The second tense is the easier to draw, and the clinic supplies the example. A patient whose insurance covers the whole cost of care faces, at the moment of decision, a price of nothing — and a rational person facing a price of nothing consumes until the very last increment is worth nothing to him, far past the point where the care was worth what it cost to provide.

The remedy, the diagram suggests, is to put some of the price back. If the insured bears a part of the cost, the price he faces is no longer zero, and his consumption retreats toward the efficient quantity. So the deductible makes him pay the first slice of any loss; the co-payment and the co-insurance leave a fraction of every bill on his side of the ledger; the policy limit caps what the insurer will cover; experience rating raises tomorrow's premium after today's claim, restoring over time the cost of carelessness. Where consumption cannot be priced, it can sometimes be watched: the insurer requires the alarm, inspects the wiring, audits the claim. Every one of these devices works by the same logic — returning to the actor some share of the consequence his action sets in motion. And every one of them, in doing so, hands him back a piece of the very risk that insurance existed to take away. That tension — between disciplining the action and bearing the risk — is the knot the next plate must untie.

The party who bears the loss is not the party who controls the risk. Insurance, in lifting the fear of the bad event, quietly lifts the incentive to prevent it — and the fuller the cover, the fainter the signal that once kept care in line.

What gives moral hazard its grip on the public imagination is its sheer reach. The bailed-out bank is its grandest instance — the institution too large to fail, gambling on the rescue it expects, is moral hazard written across the whole financial system. But the same shape recurs wherever protection meets an unwatched choice: the shareholder shielded by limited liability, the tenant who lets the maintenance slide, the contractor on a cost-plus deal with no reason to economize, the borrower who, once the loan is his, stakes it on the long shot. The phrase has escaped economics into ordinary speech precisely because the thing it names is everywhere — anywhere, that is, that someone has been insulated from a risk that remains his to influence.

the edge The concept is as over-used as it is real, and three cautions keep it honest. First, not every rise in consumption under insurance is waste. The triangle assumes the uninsured quantity was the efficient one — but the uninsured often under-consume, skipping the surgery or the check-up they cannot face the cost of, so that insurance, in freeing them to seek care they genuinely need, moves them toward efficiency rather than away from it. The man who finally treats a long-dreaded condition once it is covered is not abusing his policy; he is doing what the policy was bought to let him do. Telling true moral-hazard waste apart from the welcome lifting of a cruel constraint is hard, and the harder for being so often skipped. Second, the word moralizes. To call a rational response to incentives moral hazard is to dress a pricing problem as a character flaw, as though the insured were cheating — when he is merely doing what anyone would, and what the insurer, having written the incentive into the contract, ought to have foreseen. That framing has done real work as a cudgel, wielded against insurance and against rescue on a quasi-ethical ground the economics never supplied. Third, the cures are not free. Deductibles and co-pays push risk back onto the sick and the unlucky — the very people insurance is for — and the co-payment that deters a frivolous visit also deters a poor patient from filling a needed prescription. A policy that fears moral hazard above all else will under-insure; the right amount of moral hazard, as the next plate insists, is not zero.

Plate the Fifth · The Failures · № XII

The Incentive

the principal and the agent; pay for performance; insurance traded for effort

The plate asks how do you pay a man for an effort you cannot see?

Moral hazard, formalized, becomes the problem of the principal and the agent — the most worked-over relationship in modern microeconomics, and the one that joins this volume to the institutions that follow it. A principal engages an agent to act on his behalf: the owner hires a manager, the shareholder a chief executive, the landlord a tenant, the insurer takes on a policyholder. Two facts make the engagement fraught. The agent's interests are not the principal's — the manager would rather work less, the tenant tend the land less — and the agent's effort cannot be observed. The principal would dearly like to pay for effort and be done with it; but effort is precisely the thing he cannot see. He is forced to contract instead on what he can see, which is almost always the outcome — the profit, the harvest, the sale — and the outcome is a treacherous proxy for the effort behind it.

Treacherous because the outcome is the joint product of two things: the agent's effort, which the contract wants to reward, and luck, which it does not. The harvest reflects the tenant's labor and the season's rain; the firm's profit reflects the manager's diligence and the market's mood. To tie the agent's pay tightly to the outcome — to give him high-powered incentives — is to spur his effort, for now he keeps much of what his effort produces. But it is also to expose him to all the luck the outcome carries, to make his income lurch with rain and market in ways he cannot control. A risk-averse agent loathes that exposure and will not accept it without a premium; high-powered incentives, in other words, are expensive, purchased in the coin of the risk they dump on the agent and the compensation he demands for bearing it. Here is the trade-off that organizes the entire theory: incentive on one side, insurance on the other. The contract can motivate the agent, or it can shield him from risk, but it cannot fully do both, and every real contract is a settlement of the quarrel between them.

The outcome is effort and luck, mixed past separating. Reward it strongly and you spur the effort — but you also make the agent bear the luck, and a man who cannot abide that risk must be paid to swallow it. Incentive is bought with risk.

Where the settlement falls depends on a few intelligible forces. The more responsive the agent's effort is to reward, the more incentive is worth its cost; the more risk-averse the agent, the more the contract leans toward insuring him; the noisier the outcome — the more luck drowns out effort — the flatter the optimal contract, since a pay tied to a signal that is mostly noise buys little effort while imposing much risk. In the limit, when the outcome is almost all luck or the agent almost wholly unable to bear risk, the best contract is nearly flat: a near-wage that insures the agent and accepts that he will shirk somewhat, because the alternative — loading him with risk to buy an effort the noise would swamp anyway — is worse. This is why the agent is so rarely made the full owner of the outcome, the residual claimant who keeps every dollar his effort earns. That arrangement would give flawless incentives, but only by handing the agent every scrap of risk, which suits him only if he is risk-neutral or the outcome nearly certain. The optimal contract, Holmström showed, also reaches for any further signal that carries news of the agent's effort, however imperfect — judging the manager against his peers to strain out the shocks they share, so that he is rewarded for beating the field rather than for a rising tide that lifts them all.

The theory's reach is best seen in the institution it rescued from contempt. Sharecropping — the landlord taking a fixed share, classically a half, of what the tenant grows — was long dismissed as a feudal inefficiency, a drag on agriculture that gave the tenant only partial claim to his own labor. The principal-agent lens reads it instead as an intelligent compromise. A fixed rent would give the tenant perfect incentive — he keeps every extra bushel — but would saddle him, a poor and risk-averse man, with the whole risk of the harvest. A wage would insure him against the weather but leave him no reason to exert himself in a field no one is watching. The share splits the difference: the tenant bears some risk and keeps some incentive, the landlord bears some risk and supplies some insurance, and the fraction is the dial that tunes the one against the other. The same dial sets the salesman's commission, the executive's bundle of salary and stock, the franchisee's claim on his outlet's profit, and the firm's recurring choice between the employee it pays a steady wage and the contractor it makes a residual claimant. Each is the incentive-insurance trade-off, turned to a different setting — which is why this plate is the bridge from the failure of information to the firm and the contract that the institutions of the table are built from.

the edge The elegance of the contract on the blackboard curdles, often, into trouble on the ground, and the troubles are instructive. When the agent does many things and the principal can measure only some, paying for the measurable warps effort away from the rest: the teacher rewarded for test scores starves everything the test ignores, the surgeon judged on survival rates turns away the patients most likely to die, the manager paid on this quarter's earnings cuts the research that would have fed the next decade. On a partial measure, high-powered incentives can do more harm than a flat wage — which is why so many jobs are deliberately low-powered, paying a steady salary precisely to avoid distorting the unmeasured majority of the work. Worse, agents do not merely respond to the metric; they game it, hitting the sales target by stuffing the channel, making the number by manipulating the books, manufacturing the accounts the bonus depends on. Enron and Wells Fargo are monuments to incentives that worked exactly as designed and corrupted exactly what they touched. And money is not the only motive: pay people to do what they once did from duty or pride, and the payment can crowd the duty out — the famous fine that, charged for collecting children late, increased lateness by reframing a shared obligation as a purchased service, a lesson that belongs as much to the norm as to the contract. The grandest application has arguably failed outright: executive pay, meant to align the chief executive with the shareholders, has too often become pay for luck, rewarding bosses for oil prices and bull markets, designed by boards the bosses themselves had captured. The theory says incentive is bought with risk. It does not say that the buyer always gets what he paid for, or that what he buys does only what he intended.

Plate the Sixth · The Failures · № XII

The Signal

Spence and the signal; education as proof; the costly sign that separates

The plate asks how can the able prove that they are able, when anyone at all can claim it?

The party who holds the good news has every reason to share it — the able worker, the sound borrower, the maker of the reliable machine would all profit if only the other side believed them. The trouble is that belief cannot be bought with words. Anyone can say he is able; the lemon's owner will swear as readily as the peach's that his car is sound. Talk is cheap, in the precise sense that it costs the liar no more than the honest man, and a claim that costs nothing to make carries no information, because everyone makes it. To convey his type, the informed party must do something the other type would not — take an action whose cost is bearable to him but prohibitive to a pretender. Such an action is a signal, and Michael Spence's discovery, in nineteen seventy-three, was how it works and how strange its workings are.

His example was education, and his assumption, made deliberately to shock, was that education might teach nothing whatever — add not one jot to a worker's productivity — and still command a wage. For suppose only that schooling is easier for the able than for the less-able: that the able sit through it with less effort, less expense, less pain. Then the able can afford a level of education the less-able cannot, and an employer who pays more to the educated is not being foolish. He is reading a signal that, by its very cost, none but the able would trouble to send. The educated worker earns his premium not because school made him productive but because reaching that rung of schooling proved he was the sort who could — a proof written in the currency of cost, conveying information without creating any. The diploma sorts the talent; it need not improve it.

The single crossing the figure turns on is the whole condition of a working signal. A signal separates the types only if it costs them differently — if the marginal pain of another year of school is lighter for the able than for the rest — for that is what makes the able willing to climb to a rung the less-able will not. A signal equally costly to all would be sent by all or by none, and would tell the employer nothing; the signal must be a handicap, and a handicap the good type can carry more easily. When the condition holds, the market can settle into a separating equilibrium, the able schooling themselves to the threshold and the less-able forgoing it, each paid the wage their revealed type commands. It may instead settle into a pooling equilibrium, all choosing the same level and all paid the average, the signal conveying nothing — and the theory, awkwardly, permits many of each, with no firm word on which will obtain. The multiplicity is a genuine embarrassment, patched by refinements that ask which beliefs are reasonable, never wholly cured.

A claim that costs the liar no more than the honest man tells you nothing, for both will make it. Only a handicap separates — an expense the good type can bear and the pretender cannot. The signal must hurt, and hurt the right people more.

Once the shape is in hand it appears everywhere, for the logic is indifferent to its setting. The long warranty is a signal: ruinous for the maker of junk, who will be called to honor it, and cheap for the maker of quality, who will not — so the warranty's length proves the product's soundness, and the warranty of the contract turns out to be a cousin of the diploma. The dividend is a signal: a firm confident of its earnings can afford to commit to paying one, a shaky firm cannot, so the dividend speaks for the balance sheet. The lavish, content-free advertisement signals a firm's own faith that buyers will return, for only a seller expecting repeat business would spend so freely to acquire them. And nature, as so often, was there first: the peacock's preposterous tail, useless and costly and borne only by birds fit enough to afford the burden, is a signal of fitness — Zahavi's handicap principle, which is Spence's theorem in feathers. A signal, wherever it is found, is a cost arranged so that only the genuine article would willingly pay it.

the edge The pure-signaling story, for all its elegance, is too cynical about the schoolroom, and the honest position is that we do not know the mix. Education plainly does more than label — it teaches, trains, forms habits of mind, and raises what a worker can do, which is human capital and not mere signal. The long quarrel in labor economics is not whether education signals or builds, but in what proportion: how much of the wage premium reflects skills genuinely acquired, how much ability merely revealed. The sheepskin effect — the extra jump in wages from finishing the degree, beyond the value of the years spent — is real evidence that some of the return is signaling, but it leaves the larger share plausibly with human capital. And on that share the whole charge of waste depends. If schooling builds productive skill, the resources poured into it are not burned but invested, and Spence's startling waste shrinks to a corner of a system that is mostly doing useful work. Even the purely signaling corner is not pure waste, for a signal that sorts the able into the work that uses them best improves the match between talent and task, and a better allocation of talent is worth something even when the act of signaling produces nothing directly. Add the models' embarrassment of equilibria and their reliance on everyone solving the inference just so, and the signaling theory emerges as a profound lens and a fragile prediction: indispensable for seeing why the diploma commands a premium, unreliable for saying how much of that premium is waste.

Plate the Seventh · The Failures · № XII

The Screen

Stiglitz and the screen; the menu that sorts; self-selection and its distortion

The plate asks if the informed party will not reveal his type, can the other design a deal that makes him?

Signaling put the burden of proof on the party who knew — the able worker schooling himself to advertise his ability. Screening turns the arrangement inside out. Here the uninformed party moves first, and instead of waiting for the informed to disclose, he lays a trap of his own devising: a menu of contracts so constructed that each type, reaching for the one that suits it best, betrays which type it is. The customer is not asked to confess; he is offered a choice, and the choice confesses for him. Joseph Stiglitz and Michael Rothschild worked the idea out for insurance, and the device they described is the one every insurer now uses without naming it.

Suppose the insurer cannot tell the high-risk applicant from the low. Rather than post a single policy — which would draw the high-risk and repel the low, in the death spiral of an earlier plate — he posts two. The first offers full coverage at a steep premium; the second, partial coverage with a large deductible, at a low one. Now watch the applicants sort themselves. The high-risk, who fully expect to suffer the loss, will pay dearly to be wholly covered, and they take the full policy. The low-risk, who think the loss unlikely, balk at the steep premium and prefer to carry some risk themselves in exchange for the cheaper, thinner policy — and they take the partial one. No applicant was ever asked his risk; each revealed it in the act of choosing, and the menu accomplished what no inspection could. The insurer has screened his customers through their own preferences.

The diagram shows both the mechanism and its cost. The two types differ in how steeply they value coverage — the high-risk, certain of the loss, will pay much more premium for each added point of protection than the low-risk will — and their indifference curves accordingly cross but once, the same single crossing that made signaling work, here turned to the insurer's advantage. He chooses the two contracts so that the high-risk strictly prefer the full policy and the low-risk the partial one, and the sorting follows. But notice where the partial policy sits. To keep the high-risk from taking it — from posing as low-risk to grab cheap coverage — the insurer must make it thin enough that the high-risk disdain it, which means giving the low-risk less coverage than they would freely buy at a fair price. The low-risk are under-insured, and not for any fault or risk of their own: they are under-insured to keep the high-risk honest. This is the distortion that screening exacts, and it falls on the innocent — the careful, the healthy, the safe, made to carry risk they would rather shed, as the price of being told apart from those they are nothing like.

The customer is not asked to confess. He is offered a choice, and the choice confesses for him. But to make the bad type choose truthfully, the menu must spoil the good type's contract — the honest pay for the temptation of the rest.

The same construction, once seen, is everywhere a seller faces buyers he cannot read. The firm that sells a crippled budget version beside its full-priced flagship has built the defects of the cheap one on purpose, to keep the wealthy from buying down — sorting the price-sensitive from the quality-sensitive exactly as the insurer sorts his risks. The airline's tangle of fares, with their Saturday-night stays and advance-purchase rules and non-refundable penalties, is a screen that separates the business traveler, who will not stay the weekend and will pay almost anything, from the holidaymaker, who will do both. The quantity discount, the product line, the menu of deductibles — all are screens, and they share their mathematics with the price discrimination of an earlier volume, for to sort hidden types by a menu of terms and to charge each type what it will bear are the same problem in two costumes. The Screen and the monopolist's graduated prices are one theory, wearing the mask of information here and of power there.

the edge The construction is beautiful and badly behaved. Rothschild and Stiglitz proved a result that still unsettles the field: a competitive market screening hidden types may possess no equilibrium at all — for whenever the firms settle into a separating pair, a clever pooling offer can lure everyone away, after which the low-risk defect again, and the chase never comes to rest. A market can be left by asymmetric information not merely inefficient but without any stable outcome to analyze, and the several repairs proposed — changing what firms expect their rivals to do — restore existence only by assumptions none can claim as canonical. Even where an equilibrium exists, its distortion is regressive in a way worth naming: it worsens the terms of the good risks to discipline the bad, handing the careful and the healthy a thinner deal to thwart the reckless, so that whether the outcome counts as efficient depends entirely on whose welfare one is adding up. In the competitive scramble the same logic turns predatory, firms racing to skim the low-risk and shed the high, until the worst risks find no one willing to write them at any price — the screen, run to its conclusion, manufactures the uninsurable. And the device leans on knowledge of its own: to design a menu that just separates the types, the screener must know the distribution of those types and trust that the types are sharp and stable — a second-order information problem the elegant diagram quietly assumes away. Real menus, coarser and cruder than the theory's knife-edge contracts, betray how much it leaves out. Screening names a true force — menus do sort us — without delivering the tidy market the blackboard draws.

Plate the Eighth · The Failures · № XII

The Rationing

credit rationing; why raising the rate backfires; the market that will not clear

The plate asks if more borrowers want loans than the bank will make, why does the bank not simply raise the rate?

The market for credit is where the two failures of this volume meet and compound, for the lender suffers both at once: he cannot see, before he lends, which borrower carries the sound project and which the rash one — a hidden type — and he cannot govern, after he lends, what the borrower does with the money — a hidden action. It is also the market in which asymmetric information produces its most subversive result, one that strikes at a presumption the classical model holds almost sacred. That presumption is simple. If more people want loans than there are loans to be had, the interest rate should rise until the two are equal; a bank confronted with a queue of eager borrowers should be glad to charge them more, and the rate, like any price, should climb until the market clears. No one, on this view, who is willing to pay the going rate should ever be turned away.

Joseph Stiglitz and Andrew Weiss showed, in nineteen eighty-one, that the presumption fails — and fails because the interest rate is not merely a price but, like the insurer's premium, a thing that sorts and a thing that tempts. Raise it, and two effects work against the lender. The first is selection: the borrowers with safe, modest projects, who repay in nearly every outcome, find that a high rate eats their thin returns, and they withdraw — while the borrowers with wild, high-stakes ventures, who repay only if the gamble comes off and default otherwise, are scarcely deterred, since a higher rate only bites in the lucky state they are betting on. So the pool of borrowers, as the rate climbs, grows steadily more reckless. The second is incentive: a borrower who owes a great deal of interest has every reason to swing for the fences, for the safe project can no longer cover his obligation and only the long shot can — so the high rate pushes even the borrowers who remain toward riskier conduct. Between them, the two effects mean that beyond some point a higher rate brings the lender not more income but more default.

The picture follows at once, and it is strange. The lender's expected return, plotted against the rate he charges, does not climb without end; it rises, crests at a rate one may call r-star, and then falls away. The lender, being no fool, charges r-star — the rate that maximizes what he earns — and he will not be coaxed above it, because above it he earns less. But r-star may sit below the rate that would clear the market, the rate at which the throng of would-be borrowers just matches the supply of loans. At r-star, then, there is excess demand: more sound applicants want to borrow than the lender chooses to fund. The classical script says he should raise the rate and pocket the difference; the analysis says that raising the rate would lower his return, by spoiling his pool and inciting his borrowers. So he does neither. He keeps the rate at r-star and rations the credit — granting loans to some applicants and refusing others, who are no different and who would happily pay more, yet cannot buy a loan at any price. Identical borrowers, and some are financed and some are sent away.

The interest rate is not only a price; it is a filter and a goad. Raise it, and the safe depart while the gamblers stay, and those who remain are tempted to gamble harder — so beyond a point the lender earns less by charging more.

What rationing overturns is among the load-bearing beliefs of the older economics: that markets clear, and that any excess demand is a fleeting thing the price will swiftly competed away. Here the excess demand is permanent, and the price will not budge it, because the price has work to do beyond balancing the books — it selects the borrowers and shapes their risk, and to move it for the sake of clearing would wreck those other functions. The equilibrium is one of quantity rationing, not price adjustment, and the lesson reaches far past the loan office. It tells us why credit can dry up in a downturn without any fall in the rate, why monetary policy works partly by loosening and tightening the spigot of available credit rather than only by moving its price, why the marginal borrower in a slump is rejected rather than merely charged more. The market for the price of money, studied in an earlier volume, turns out not to behave like a market for an ordinary good at all — and the reason is the asymmetry at its heart.

the edge Whether credit rationing is a pervasive fact or a theoretical curiosity has been argued for forty years, and the skeptics have a strong hand. Lenders are not confined, as the bare model confines them, to a single instrument; they wield collateral, covenants, credit scores, and the long memory of a banking relationship, and with these they sort borrowers rather than ration them. Collateral in particular cuts against the result twice over: a borrower willing to pledge his house signals confidence in his project, and a borrower with his house at stake has reason to choose the safe path — so the security that the model omits can screen the type and tame the action together, dissolving the very rationing the model predicts. Rationing, on this reading, is what happens when screening fails, not a general law of credit; given the lender's full toolkit, it may be the exception. And even where it appears, it is not obviously a failure to be remedied. The lender rations because lending more would lose him money — which is to say he is responding correctly to the information he lacks, not committing some additional sin. To brand the rationing itself a market failure is to confuse the symptom with the disease: the disease is the asymmetry, and the rationing is the market's sober answer to it. The grander claims, that credit rationing drives the business cycle, are influential and unsettled, their magnitude a matter the data have not closed.

Plate the Ninth · The Failures · № XII

The Witness

reputation, certification, the warranty; the devices the market breeds to cope

The plate asks what does a market build, of its own accord, to bridge a gap it cannot close?

The signal and the screen were the parties' own devices, deployed within a single transaction. But a market does not face the asymmetry one deal at a time and unaided; it grows, over time and without any designer, a whole apparatus for coping with the gap — institutions that supply the trust the bare transaction lacks. This plate gathers them, for they are the answer to the question the volume opened with: how a market survives when one side knows what the other cannot. The short answer is that it builds witnesses — third things, standing outside the deal, that vouch for what the buyer cannot see.

The oldest and the strongest of these is reputation. A seller who must return to the market tomorrow has a reason to deal honestly today that a one-time stranger does not: he holds an asset, built slowly out of satisfied customers and destroyed quickly by a single betrayal, and the fear of losing it disciplines his conduct now. The mechanism is the repeated game of an earlier volume turned to commercial use. In a single encounter the seller gains by cheating and is gone; in an ongoing relationship the value of all the honest dealing still to come can exceed the one-time prize of a swindle, so that honesty enforces itself, held in place not by conscience or by law but by arithmetic. A reputation, on this account, is a bond — the discounted stream of future custom that the seller forfeits the moment he is caught cheating — and it explains why the branded good commands a premium over the anonymous one, why firms pour fortunes into names that confer no function, why the regular customer is treated better than the passing trade. The brand is a hostage the seller gives to his own future good behavior.

Where the parties cannot supply the trust between them, a third party can sell it. The inspector who certifies the building, the auditor who attests the accounts, the agency that rates the bond, the laboratory whose mark declares the wiring safe — each is a specialist in a particular kind of knowing, and each stakes its own reputation on the judgments it renders, so that its certification carries weight precisely because its name would suffer if the certification proved false. Near kin to the certifier is the intermediary, the middleman who makes a business of bridging the gap: the used-car dealer who inspects and reconditions and warrants, turning Akerlof's anonymous lemon lot into a branded forecourt with a name to protect; the broker, the market-maker, the platform. And the platform is the certifier and the intermediary reborn for an age of strangers — the marketplace that lets a buyer in one country trust a seller in another by staking its own reputation on the exchange, and by aggregating into a single star rating the remembered experience of ten thousand past customers, so that the reputation which once required personal acquaintance is now manufactured at scale and handed to a stranger for free.

A market threatened by the gap does not lie down and fail. It breeds witnesses — the reputation it can ruin, the certifier it can shame, the warranty it can call — and turns the anonymous bazaar into a place where strangers can deal.

The remaining devices put money where the word is. The warranty, met before as a signal, is also a bond: in promising to bear the cost of any failure, the seller stakes his own purse on the quality he claims, and the longer and fuller the warranty the larger the stake. The money-back guarantee, the service contract, the maker's pledge to repair — all convert a claim about quality into a financial hostage. On the borrower's side the corresponding device is collateral: the security he forfeits if he defaults, which at once signals his confidence in his own project and binds him to the prudent path, since it is now his house and not merely the bank's money at risk. Bonds, deposits, escrow, the contractor's surety — each is a pledge that aligns the pledger's interest with the truth of his representations. Taken together, reputation and certification and intermediation and bonding compose a remarkable fact, and the hopeful counterweight to the gloom of the lemons: a market crippled by asymmetric information does not passively accept its fate but spontaneously evolves the institutions that hold it together, so that the failure which the blackboard pronounces fatal is, in any market that actually works, already half-cured by contrivances the blackboard never drew.

the edge The witnesses are real and indispensable, and not one of them is reliable. Reputation, the strongest, works only where the dealing repeats and the cheating shows: it disciplines the corner grocer who will see you next week, and does nothing to the roadside stand that will never see you again, nothing in the tourist trap, nothing where the defect surfaces too late or too faintly to fix the blame — the same patience and frequency and visibility that the folk theorem requires, and the same collapse when they are absent. Certification fails in its own way, through capture: the certifier paid by the party he certifies has every reason to flatter, and the spectacle of the rating agencies stamping their highest grade on the subprime securities that detonated in two thousand eight, paid all the while by the issuers whose paper they were grading, is the textbook instance of a watchman bought by those he was set to watch — as the auditors of more than one collapsed firm had been bought before them. The intermediary, meant to close the gap, opens a fresh one, for now there is a party who knows more than you about whether he is serving your interest or his own commission — the broker who pushes the quick sale, the adviser who steers you to the product that pays him most, the agent whose agency is itself the problem this volume began with. And reputation, that great solvent of asymmetry, congeals into a barrier: the established name keeps out the better newcomer who has not had time to build one, and the rating that vouches for quality is gamed by fabricated reviews and laundered testimonials. The market's own cures mitigate the disease. They do not cure it, and they bring new ailments of their own.

Plate the Tenth · The Failures · № XII

The Disclosure

the state's hand; mandated disclosure; statistical discrimination and its harm

The plate asks where the market's own remedies fall short, what should the state compel?

The witnesses of the last plate were the market's own, grown without a designer and answerable to no one but the custom they hoped to keep. Where they fail, or fall short, or arrive too late, the state may step in — supplying by command the information or the assurance the market could not supply itself. The interventions form a ladder of increasing force, and the lightest rung is also the favorite, for it neither bans a trade nor fixes a price but merely drags a hidden fact into the light: mandated disclosure. The seller of a used car must declare its known defects; the lender must state the true annual rate; the label must list the ingredients, the calories, the side effects, the energy the appliance will burn. The grandest disclosure regime of all governs the issue of securities, where the market in a company's shares is a lemons problem of vast scale — the insiders knowing the firm's true worth, the public guessing — and the law's answer, since the reforms of the nineteen-thirties, has been to compel the firm to open its books: the audited statement, the prospectus, the continuous filing. Disclosure is the economist's preferred remedy because it is the least distorting, leaving the market to work but furnishing it the facts.

Above disclosure stand the heavier rungs. Where quality is too hard for any buyer to judge and the cost of error too grave — in medicine, in aviation, in the safety of food and drugs — the state licenses the provider, its certificate standing as a public warrant of competence where private reputation would form too slowly or too late. And where even a licensed and disclosed market cannot be trusted to protect the buyer, the state regulates the quality directly, decreeing that the drug be proven effective before it is sold, that the building meet the code, that the food be safe — mandating the thing itself, and not merely its disclosure. Each rung trades a measure of the market's freedom for a measure of the buyer's protection, and the choice of rung is the substance of a long argument.

That argument has three corners, and an honest umpire will not award it outright to any. In the first corner stands the interventionist: asymmetric information is a failure, and the state should mandate, license, and regulate to mend it. In the second stands the skeptic, who points to the previous plate — the market already breeds reputation and certification and the warranty, and the state that intervenes often merely duplicates these, or crowds them out, while adding the failures peculiar to regulators, who are captured by the industries they oversee and who know less than the market they correct. In the third, and lately the loudest, stands the technologist, who observes that a new machinery of information is dissolving the very asymmetries that justified the old interventions: the database that yields a car's whole history for a few dollars, the reviews that pool ten thousand buyers' experience, the rating and the comparison site and the device in every pocket, all making hidden facts cheaply visible — so that the market reveals what the law once had to compel. The truthful verdict is that it depends: on whether the private remedies actually work in this market, on whether buyers can read and act on what is disclosed, on the stakes and the cost of being wrong, and on whether the state can really do better than the market it proposes to fix. There is no rule, only the case at hand.

Disclosure is the lightest hand — it bans nothing and fixes no price, but drags the hidden fact into the light and lets the market work with it. Whether the light is read, and acted on, is another question entirely.

Asymmetric information has, besides its failures and its remedies, a dark and stubborn consequence that no disclosure cures, and it deserves its own paragraph. When a party cannot observe the trait he cares about in an individual — the worker's productivity, the borrower's reliability, the applicant's risk — he is tempted to predict it from a trait he can observe: the group the individual belongs to. The employer who cannot see this applicant's diligence judges him by the average diligence of his kind; the lender prices the loan by the neighborhood; the insurer by the sex or the age. Kenneth Arrow and Edmund Phelps named this statistical discrimination, and its disturbing feature is that it need spring from no hatred at all. It is the cold, profit-seeking use of a group's average as a proxy for the person, rational given the gap in knowledge — and unjust precisely because it is rational, penalizing the able member of a disfavored group not for anything he is or does but for the company the statistics keep him in. Worse, it can fulfill itself: a group that knows it will be judged by its average, whatever its members achieve, has less reason to invest in the achievements that would distinguish them, and the slackened investment confirms the average that justified the discrimination — a trap that locks itself. It is the mirror image of screening turned cruel: where the screen lets the individual reveal himself, statistical discrimination assigns him his group's mean, and the weaker the individual signal, the stronger the pull toward the group proxy. The law's response has been to forbid the use of certain traits — race, sex — as proxies, even where they predict, choosing to bar the rational use of some information rather than tolerate the injustice it works, the same hard choice met in the banning of genetic tests from the insurer's hands. The choice has only grown sharper as the proxy passes from the human prejudice to the trained algorithm, which will find in a postal code or a purchase history a serviceable stand-in for the very traits the law forbids.

the edge The state's remedies are no more reliable than the market's, and the favorite of them may be the feeblest. Mandated disclosure is beloved of legislators because it is easy to pass and seems to cost nothing and offend no one — and it largely does not work, because the disclosed is not read. No one studies the click-through privacy policy, the dozens of pages at the mortgage closing, the fine print of the card agreement, the dense leaflet folded into the medicine; and what is not read cannot inform. Ben-Shahar and others have pressed the indictment hard: disclosure mandates accumulate because they are the path of least resistance, furnishing the appearance of a remedy while the asymmetry survives untouched beneath it — and sometimes doing harm, as the warning repeated on everything is heeded on nothing, and the flood of mandated facts drowns the few that mattered. Licensing fares no better when it is captured, dressed as consumer protection but functioning as a wall against competitors, demanded for trades whose risks could not justify it. And the fairness-accuracy choice at the heart of statistical discrimination is genuinely, irreducibly hard: forbid the predictive trait, and insurers and lenders price worse and may abandon the market or ration it; permit it, and the injustice rolls on — a dilemma with no clean resolution, and one the algorithm has made acute, for to forbid a model the postal code that proxies for race is to forbid it information that genuinely predicts default, at a cost someone must bear. Behind every call for the state to supply what the market cannot lies the question the skeptic keeps asking: whether the state, knowing less than the market and prone to capture, will supply it any better — or merely smother the private remedies that were, imperfectly, already at work.

Plate the Eleventh · The Failures · № XII

The Paradox

information as a peculiar good; Arrow's paradox; the knowledge a market cannot price

The plate asks if information is the cure, why can the market not simply produce and sell it?

Through every plate of this volume a single remedy has hovered, unspoken: if only the ignorant party knew. If the buyer could see the car's quality, the lender the borrower's risk, the employer the worker's ability, the failures would dissolve and the market would clear as the classical model promised. The obvious thought, then, is that the market should supply the missing knowledge itself — that where information is scarce and valuable, someone should produce it and sell it, and a flourishing market for information should furnish the asymmetric markets the very thing they lack. The thought is sound, and it fails, for the most instructive of reasons: information is the strangest commodity in the whole of economics, and the market for it is itself among the markets that fail. The cure, it turns out, is made of the same stuff as the disease.

The strangeness begins with a paradox that Kenneth Arrow set down and that bears his name. To know what a piece of information is worth to you, you must know the information — for only then can you judge how it bears on your decisions. But once you know it, you have no reason to pay, for you already possess it. The seller of information thus confronts a buyer who cannot value the goods until he has been shown them, and who, having been shown them, no longer needs to buy. A fact cannot be inspected before purchase like a melon, then bought or declined; to inspect it is to acquire it. This is why information is so awkward to sell in any ordinary transaction, and why those who deal in it resort to such contrivances — selling reputations rather than facts, bundling the knowledge into a service, guarding it as a secret disclosed only under contract — none of which quite escapes the paradox at the root.

The paradox is only the first of information's peculiarities, and the others compound it. Information is non-rival: my knowing a fact does not consume it or keep you from knowing it too, so that once produced it can serve everyone at no further cost — which means its efficient price is nothing, and a price of nothing pays no one to produce it. It leaks: revealed once, or sold once, it escapes into the world, copied and passed on and reverse-engineered, so that its producer captures only a fraction of the value it ultimately yields. And it is costly to make — the research, the search, the experiment all consume real resources before any fact is in hand. Put these together and the verdict is the figure's: the market under-produces information, because the producer, able to capture only the private sliver of a value that is mostly social, will stop far short of the amount worth making. It is the under-provision of the public good, met before with lighthouses and basic science, here visited upon knowledge itself — and the devices that answer it, the patent that grants a temporary monopoly so the producer can appropriate his return, the public funding of the research no firm would undertake, are the same imperfect contrivances, trading one distortion for another in a good the market cannot handle cleanly.

From this peculiar good follows the deepest result of the whole subject, and the one that fixes the Asymmetry's place among the failures past any doubt. Bruce Greenwald and Joseph Stiglitz proved that an economy with imperfect and asymmetric information — which is to say every real economy — is essentially never efficient in the way the classical theorem promised: not efficient, and not at a single point of failure to be patched, but generically, pervasively, as a matter of course. The famous efficiency of the invisible hand turns out to be the knife-edge special case, requiring information complete and symmetric and markets entire; the ordinary case, the actual case, is inefficiency. This inverts the inherited presumption at its foundation. The textbook had treated efficiency as the rule and market failures as a short list of exceptions — the monopoly, the externality, the public good. Information economics reveals that the list was the world: once knowledge is admitted to be unequal and costly, as it always is, failure is the rule and efficiency the exception, and the question is never whether a market falls short of the frictionless ideal but only by how much and what, if anything, can be done.

The cure is made of the same stuff as the disease. You cannot price a fact until you know it, and once you know it you need not buy it — so the market for the knowledge that would mend the other markets is itself among the markets that fail.

One question the subject raises it cannot answer, and the honest theorist says so. If information is costly, then the right amount to gather is finite — perfect knowledge is not the goal, for the last increments cost more than they are worth. But how much, exactly, should a buyer learn before he trades, a firm research before it invests, a society know before it acts? The question has no clean answer, and Arrow's paradox is the reason: to weigh a piece of information against its cost, one must already have it, so the calculation that would tell us the optimal amount of knowledge requires the very knowledge it is meant to ration. The economics of information ends, fittingly, at a fact about itself — that the value of knowing is the one thing knowing cannot settle.

the edge Under-provision is the headline, and the sign is not always negative. Information is in places over-produced, and wastefully: firms racing to patent the same discovery duplicate the same costly research, and the winner takes a prize the loser's effort merely inflated; traders spend fortunes to learn a fact a moment before their rivals, in an arms race that — as Jack Hirshleifer observed — can be privately lucrative and socially worthless, since the early knowledge mostly redistributes a gain rather than creating one; and the modern firm over-collects the personal data it can monetize while foisting the privacy cost on others, an over-provision dressed with a negative externality. The patent that answers under-provision routinely overshoots into a distortion of its own — the thicket, the troll, the evergreened monopoly whose deadweight cost outruns the innovation it was meant to buy. And the grand Greenwald-Stiglitz verdict, that markets are generically inefficient, must be read with the discipline its authors themselves urged: it humbles the market without crowning the state. That an outcome falls short of the constrained ideal does not mean any government could reach the ideal, for the government labors under the same asymmetries — knowing less than the dispersed market it would correct — plus failures of its own. The theorem dethrones the invisible hand; it does not enthrone the visible one. And since the optimal amount of knowledge is itself underdetermined, the case for any particular intervention to fix the under-provision rests in the end on judgment, not on the theorem — which tells us that the market errs, but not by how much, nor reliably in which direction.

Plate the Twelfth · The Failures · № XII

The Knowledge

what the failure teaches; markets run on knowledge, unequally held

The plate asks what does the failure of knowing teach about the markets that run on it?

We began with a clause the classical model kept quiet — that the parties to a trade know what they need to, or that the price will tell them — and we have spent eleven plates watching what happens when the clause is false. The secret, in the first plate, split into two: the hidden type, fixed before the deal, and the hidden action, taken after it; and from the two grew the two great failures of the subject. From hidden type came adverse selection — the lemons that drive the good cars from the market, the insurance pool the healthy abandon to the sick — a sickness in who comes forward to trade. From hidden action came moral hazard — the careless insured, the gambling bank, the shirking agent — a sickness in how the protected behave once trading. We watched the failures, and then we watched the market refuse to accept them.

For the responses were the larger half of the story. The party who knew his type tried to prove it, by a signal too costly for a pretender to send — the schooling that separates the able even if it teaches them nothing. The party who did not know tried to extract it, by a screen of self-sorting terms — the menu of policies through which the high-risk and the low confess themselves in the act of choosing. Where price could neither signal nor screen, the market abandoned price and turned to quantity, the lender rationing credit rather than raising a rate that would draw the reckless and tempt the rest. And around these contrivances the market grew a standing apparatus — the reputation it could ruin, the certifier it could shame, the warranty it could call, the collateral it could seize — witnesses that let strangers deal across a gap no single transaction could close. Where even these fell short, the state added its hand, compelling the disclosure the seller would not volunteer, licensing what the buyer could not judge, and confronting the darkest issue of all, the use of a group's average as a proxy for a person it could not read. And beneath everything lay the final paradox: that the knowledge which would cure the asymmetric markets is itself a good the market cannot make or price, so that information is at once the remedy and another instance of the disease.

What the whole accumulates to is a correction of the model at its root, and the correction is simple to state. Markets run on knowledge. They run on the buyer knowing what he buys and the lender whom he lends to and the employer whom he hires, and this knowledge is unequally held, costly to gather, impossible to complete — which the classical picture, for the sake of its theorems, had assumed away. The economics of information put the knowledge back, and found that almost everything turned on it: that a market could vanish for want of it, that the most ordinary institutions — the diploma, the deductible, the brand, the credit limit — were elaborate machinery for coping with its absence, and that the efficiency the older theory had taken for the rule was in truth the rarest of special cases. The Asymmetry is the name for the failure of knowing, and the failure of knowing, it turns out, is not one market's misfortune but the condition of them all.

Seen so, the volume sends its lines out across the whole table. The warranty that signaled quality here is the warranty that bonds a promise in the volume of the contract; the agent whose hidden effort the principal could not see is the agent at the heart of the firm, which is itself a knot of incentive contracts tied against moral hazard. The private types that the signal revealed and the screen sorted are the private types of the games of incomplete information; the screen that sorted insurance risks shares its mathematics with the graduated prices of the monopolist, so that the power to discriminate and the asymmetry of information are one theory in two masks. The credit that rationed rather than cleared is the rate that an earlier volume took for an ordinary price and that this one finds will not behave like one; the pool that adverse selection unravelled is the premium's pool, and the moral hazard that swells a claim is the premium's hazard too. The information that the market under-produces is the public good of the commons, non-rival and unowned and therefore too little made. Even the reputation that disciplines the seller is the norm enforced by repeated dealing, and the incentive that crowds out a craftsman's pride is a warning the norm will repeat. The failure of knowing touches every element it neighbors, because knowing underlies them all.

Within its own family the Asymmetry takes the second place. The Failures are the ways a market can betray us with no villain in sight — no monopolist, no thief, no fool — and each member names a different treachery. The Power was the failure of number, of too few sellers; the Externality, still to come, is the failure of the missing market, of the cost that falls on a bystander no price counts; the Commons, also ahead, is the failure of the resource that all may use and none will tend. The Asymmetry is the failure of knowledge, of the fact held by one side and hidden from the other. With it the group stands two of four complete, and the two that remain — the spillover and the shared resource — wait their volumes. What unites the four is the lesson that a market may be perfectly competitive and perfectly lawful and fail all the same, for reasons written into its structure rather than charged to anyone's account.

The achievement deserves its full due, for it is among the largest in the economics of the last century. Information economics humbled a model that had grown complacent, showing that its central promise held only under conditions the world does not meet; and in humbling it the new theory built rather than merely tore down, explaining as the rational fruit of the informational problem a vast catalogue of institutions that the older economics had to treat as given or as folly — the credential, the warranty, the deductible, the rationed loan, the brand, the rating, the disclosure law. It reshaped the study of labor and of finance, of insurance and of development and of regulation; it gave its founders, Akerlof and Spence and Stiglitz, the field's highest honor, and drew into the work the deepest minds of a generation. Above all it taught economics to look past the price to the knowledge the price conceals — to ask, of any market, not merely what things cost but who knows what, and what the not-knowing does.

And yet the volume has insisted, plate by plate, on the limits of its own instrument, and the insistence is part of the achievement. The elegant models breed equilibria they cannot choose among and lean on a rationality no one quite possesses, so that they identify forces more surely than they predict outcomes. The remedies, private and public alike, are partial and corruptible — reputations that fail the one-shot deal, certifiers bought by those they rate, disclosures no one reads, mandates that redistribute under the name of efficiency, screens that punish the innocent to discipline the guilty. Markets prove more resourceful than the gloomy theorems allow, breeding cures and harnessing a technology of information that dissolves asymmetries the models held fatal — even as that same technology hands the discriminating proxy to an algorithm. The policy question stays genuinely open, the case for the state's hand always shadowed by the state's own ignorance and capture; and the deepest question of all, how much it is worth knowing, the subject confesses it cannot answer, since the value of knowledge is the one thing knowledge will not reveal. The Asymmetry is the failure that recognizes the plainest truth about markets and the one the model most often forgets — that they run on knowledge, unequally held, costly to share, and never complete. It humbles the model, it explains the institutions, and it leaves to judgment, market by market, whether the gap is a crippling failure or a managed friction, and whether its cure is best left to the market's ingenuity or supplied by the hand of the state. The argument remains open.
