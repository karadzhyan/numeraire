On reading across

Reading Across

There are two ways to read a table. Down a column you hold the group fixed and vary the element; across a row you hold the period fixed and vary the group. The chemist learns to do both, and to watch the diagonals besides, where elements unrelated by row or column turn out to share a property all the same. This work has been built, until now, for reading down: each of the twenty-six volumes takes a single element and follows it through twelve plates, from its first definition to its final doubt. That is the vertical read, and it is the spine of the thing. But it is not the only read the preface promised.

The preface spoke of diagonal reads — of following one idea as it crosses the table, surfacing in the third plate of one volume, the seventh of another, the fifth of a third. Until now that was only a suggestion; the reader was left to find the diagonals alone. This companion draws them. Each throughline below is an ordered path of individual plates, gathered from across the twenty-six volumes and set in a reading order, so that a single concern — what a price conceals, what cannot be measured, what one side cannot know — can be followed as a continuous argument through the whole work rather than glimpsed twelve times in twelve separate places. Every step names its plate, says why it belongs on the thread, and links to its exact place in the text.

This is the first companion to work below the level of the element. The atlas, the constellation, the properties, and the compounds all move among the twenty-six volumes; the diagonals move among the three hundred and twelve plates, the actual chapters where the argument is made. Choose a throughline and watch where it goes — the small map shows which volumes it touches and how often — then walk it plate by plate. Read down for the depth of one element; read across for the life of one idea. The grid holds both, and the second read is the one that shows how few ideas the whole vast table is really made of.

Each step opens its plate in the volume it belongs to. Read them in order, or leap to the one that calls.

What the diagonals reveal

Down and Across

Walk a few of these threads and the table changes character. Read down, the work looks like twenty-six separate subjects, each with its own twelve-chapter logic; the volumes sit side by side like strangers who happen to share a shelf. Read across, the strangers turn out to be relatives. The trouble with measurement is one trouble, whether it surfaces in the utility, the share, or the price level; power is one force, whether it wears the mask of the monopolist, the monopsonist, the rent-seeker, or the captured regulator; the hidden contest is one contest, fought over the wage and the rent and the externality and the share by turns. The diagonals show that beneath a hundred models there are only a handful of recurring concerns, stated again and again in different vocabularies.

And that is the quiet argument of the whole apparatus. A periodic table is not a list of substances; it is a claim that the substances are organized, that knowing where a thing sits tells you what it is like and what it will do. To read this table down is to learn the elements; to read it across is to learn the order — to see that the same few ideas recur at fixed intervals, in lawful places, because the subject has a structure and not merely a contents. The volumes are the depth of the work; the diagonals are its unity. Both are true at once, which is the only honest thing a table can ever be: a way of holding many things apart so that the few things they share can finally be seen.

Return to the atlas for the table of elements, the constellation for the bonds among them, the properties to weigh them by their traits, the compounds for what they form together, the laboratory to set their models moving, the preface for the argument entire, the guided tour for a curated first path, the index to find any plate by name, the objections where the book answers its critics, the chronology for the order in which it was found, the measures for the figures themselves, the cases to see the elements explain the world, the priceless for the goods that resist, the gaps for the cells it lacks, the first person for how they are lived, the schools for how each tradition reads them, the sources for the books behind them, or the unsettled for the verdicts not yet in. Read down, read across; the argument remains open whichever way you cut it.
