the quantity held at a moment — capital and wealth, the accumulation of the past and the value of the future

The question of the volume What is the difference between what you own and what you earn — and why does almost everything in economics depend on never confusing the two?

in print · twelve plates
Numéraire · Volume IX

The Argument

There is a distinction so basic that the whole of economic accounting rests upon it, and so easily missed that confusing its two sides is the commonest serious error in the subject: the distinction between a stock and a flow. A stock is a quantity measured at a single instant — what one has at a moment, with no reference to time in its very unit: a sum of wealth, a quantity of capital, a holding of money, a level of inventory. A flow is a quantity measured over an interval — what passes in a span of time, its unit carrying a "per year" or "per month" within it: an income, an investment, a rate of depreciation, a deficit. The stock is a photograph; the flow is the film. What you own is a stock; what you earn is a flow; and to mistake the one for the other — to confuse a fortune with an income, a debt with a deficit — is to misunderstand, at the root, who has what and how it came to be. This volume is about the quantity held, and it is the last of the atlas.

Stock and flow are bound together by time, and the binding is the calculus of accumulation. A stock is the accumulation of all the past flows that have fed and drained it: the level in a bathtub rises whenever the inflow exceeds the outflow, and stands as the running sum of their difference. The capital of a nation is the accumulation of its past net investment; the public debt is the accumulation of its past deficits; a person's wealth is the accumulation of a lifetime's saving. The stock is the past made present — history congealed into a quantity one can hold at an instant. In the language of the calculus, the stock is the integral of the flow, and the flow the rate of change of the stock; and almost every dynamic question in economics is, at bottom, a question about how a flow fills or empties a stock over time.

The central economic stock is capital — the produced means of production, the tools and plant and structures accumulated by past investment and set to work making more. And capital raises at once the deepest question about any stock: what is it worth? The answer, which Irving Fisher made the cornerstone of the theory, is that the value of any durable asset is the present value of the future flow of income or service it will yield. A machine is worth the discounted sum of the outputs it will produce; a bond, the discounted sum of its coupons; a parcel of land, the discounted sum of its rents; a firm, the discounted sum of its profits. So the stock looks in two directions at once: accumulated out of the past, it is valued by the future, brought back to the present by the rate of interest of an earlier volume. The stock is the past made present in its accumulation and the future made present in its worth.

Two flows govern whether a stock grows, holds, or shrinks. Investment is the flow that builds the capital stock — undertaken, at the margin, up to the point where the marginal product of capital equals its cost, and made strikingly volatile by the accelerator, which ties investment not to the level of output but to its change. Depreciation is the flow that wears the stock away, the silent consumption of capital as it ages and is used. Net investment is their difference, the actual change in the stock, and the steady state is the rest point at which they balance and the stock stands still. But to speak of "the capital stock" as a single number is to invite the hardest question in the theory of capital — for capital is heterogeneous, a jumble of machines and buildings and tools that can be summed only in value, and value requires prices, which depend on the very rate of return one is trying to explain. The Cambridge controversy over whether aggregate capital can be coherently measured at all has never been fully settled.

The stock appears in many guises beyond capital, and a few recur with special force. The holding of a stock as a buffer against uncertainty is one: inventories are held to absorb the mismatch between the flow of production and the flow of sales, and their swings drive the business cycle; money is the most liquid stock of all, held — as Keynes saw — not only to spend but as a reserve against an uncertain future, at the cost of the interest it might have earned. And wealth is the great stock whose distribution shapes a society, for when the return on wealth outruns the growth of income, as Thomas Piketty has pressed, the stock of past accumulation comes to loom ever larger over the flow of present effort. Examine any quantity in economics closely enough and it resolves into a stock or a flow, and the relation between the two.

The discipline that all of this imposes is stock-flow consistency — the simple, constantly violated requirement that the stocks and the flows in any account fit together exactly, that the change in every stock equal the net of its flows. Its most famous casualty is the confusion of the deficit with the debt: the deficit is a flow, the debt the stock it accumulates, and a falling deficit still adds to a rising debt, so that "the deficit is shrinking" and "the debt is growing" are both true at once and forever muddled in public argument. The thesis of the volume is that to think clearly in economics is, above all, to know at every moment whether one is looking at a stock or a flow, and never to mistake the photograph for the film. The quantity, here as everywhere in the atlas, is a verdict on a hidden contest: between the stock and the flow, between the past accumulated and the future redeemed, between what is held and what is earned, between the moment and the time that gives it worth. And with this, the last of the elements, the periodic table of economic life is complete. The argument remains open.

Plate the First · The Quantities · № IX

The Snapshot

stock against flow — a quantity at an instant versus a quantity through time, and why everything depends on the difference

The plate asks what is the difference between a thing held and a thing earned?

A stock is a quantity measured at a single instant; a flow is a quantity measured over an interval of time. The difference is in the very unit. A stock's unit carries no reference to time — a sum of dollars, a number of machines, a tonnage of grain, a holding of money: what one has, frozen at a moment. A flow's unit carries a span of time within it — dollars per year, machines built per quarter, grain harvested per season: what passes, reckoned over an interval. The stock is a photograph, capturing what stands at an instant; the flow is the film, recording what moves through time. What you own is a stock; what you earn is a flow. The two are as different as a quantity and its rate of change, and keeping them distinct is the beginning of all clear economic thought.

The distinction sounds elementary, and confusing its two sides is nonetheless the commonest serious error in the subject. Wealth is a stock and income a flow, and a person may hold great wealth while earning little, or earn a large salary while holding nothing, so that to judge a person's standing by their income alone is to mistake the flow for the stock. The public debt is a stock and the deficit a flow, and to speak as though reducing the deficit reduced the debt is to confuse the level of a reservoir with the rate at which it fills. Money is a stock and spending a flow; capital is a stock and investment a flow. In each pair the same trap waits: to read a quantity at a moment as though it were a quantity through time, or the reverse — and to misunderstand, at the root, both who has what and how it came to be.

What you own is a stock; what you earn is a flow — and to confuse a fortune with an income is to misread, at the root, who has what.

Yet stock and flow, though distinct, are inseparably bound, and the bond is time. The flow accumulates into the stock: every stock is the running sum of the flows that have fed and drained it, the integral of its own history. And the stock generates flows: capital yields the flow of output, wealth the flow of income, a reservoir the flow drawn from it. A quantity at a moment and a quantity through time are the two faces of a single thing seen in two ways — the level and the motion, the standing and the passing. The plates that follow examine the great stocks of economic life — capital above all, and wealth, and money, and inventory — tracing how each is accumulated by the flows that fill it, how each is valued by the flows it will yield, how each wears away, and what discipline it takes to keep the stocks and the flows from ever being confused.

the edge The distinction is sharp in principle and blurs, instructively, at its edges. Some quantities are ambiguous or depend on the period over which they are reckoned, and a stock measured at year-end is a snapshot of something that was flowing all year and will flow again. More deeply, the connection between a stock and its flows runs through time and the rate of interest in ways that make a stock's value depend on a forecast of flows not yet arrived — so that the worth of a stock, though a single number at an instant, secretly encodes an entire imagined future. The clean separation of a quantity at a moment from a quantity over an interval is the start of clarity; the intricate ways the two are bound together, by accumulation behind and by valuation ahead, are much of the rest of this volume, and the last lesson the atlas has to teach.

Plate the Second · The Quantities · № IX

The Reservoir

accumulation — the stock as the integral of past flows, rising when inflow exceeds outflow; the past made present

The plate asks how does a stock come to be — and what fills or empties it?

A stock comes to be by accumulation — the piling up, over time, of the flows that feed and drain it. The image that holds the whole idea is the reservoir, or the humble bathtub: the level of water in it is a stock, a quantity at a moment; the tap pouring in and the drain running out are flows, quantities through time; and the level rises whenever the inflow exceeds the outflow, falls whenever the outflow exceeds the inflow, and holds steady when the two are matched. The figure draws it. The stock standing in the tub at any instant is nothing other than the running sum of every inflow it has received, less every outflow it has lost — the net flow, accumulated over all past time.

Every economic stock is such a reservoir. The capital of a nation is the accumulation of its past net investment, filled by the building of new plant and drained by the wearing-out of the old. The public debt is the accumulation of its past deficits, filled each year that spending exceeds revenue. A household's wealth is the accumulation of a lifetime's saving, filled by what is set aside and drained by what is drawn down. In every case the stock is the past made present — the whole history of the flows, congealed into a single quantity that can be held and counted at an instant. To know the level is to hold a summary of everything that has flowed; the reservoir remembers what the flows themselves, passing, forget.

A stock is the past made present — the running sum of every flow that has filled or drained it, history congealed into a quantity.

The relation is, in exact terms, that of the integral and the derivative. The stock is the integral of the flow — its accumulation over time — and the flow is the derivative of the stock — its rate of change at each moment. This is why a stock can be vast though its current flow is a trickle, a great reservoir filled drop by drop across ages; and why a stock can be falling even as a positive flow continues, if the drain exceeds the tap. The level tells the history; the flow tells the present motion. To know a stock fully, one must know the whole past that accumulated it; to foresee its path, one must know the flows that will move it. The calculus of accumulation is the grammar that binds every stock to its flows.

the edge The reservoir reveals a deep asymmetry between stocks and flows: their speed. Because a stock changes only by the slow accumulation of its flows, stocks have inertia — a sudden rise in the flow of saving lifts the stock of wealth only gradually, and a collapse in the flow of investment shrinks the stock of capital only over years, not months. Stocks are slow and flows are quick, and much of the drama of macroeconomics is the interplay between the two: the flow of investment swinging violently from year to year while the stock of capital it feeds scarcely stirs, the level in the great reservoir barely moving though the tap gushes and chokes. The stock remembers and the flow forgets, and the difference in their tempos is itself one of the most important facts about how an economy moves through time.

Plate the Third · The Quantities · № IX

The Capital

capital — the accumulated stock of produced means of production, and the desired stock where its marginal product meets its cost

The plate asks what is capital, and how much of it should be held?

Capital is the central economic stock — the produced means of production, the tools and machines and buildings and structures accumulated by past investment and set to work making more. What sets it apart from the other factors of production, from the labor of the wage and the land of the rent, is that capital is itself produced: it is at once an output of past production and an input to future production, a stock that the economy makes in order to make with. Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk caught its essence in a phrase — capital is roundaboutness, the productive detour through time. We forgo consuming now in order to build the tools that will let us consume more later; the deferral, embodied in a stock of equipment, is what capital is.

How much capital should be held? The figure gives the marginal answer. Capital, like any factor, is subject to diminishing returns: the marginal product of capital — the extra output won from one more unit of it — falls as the stock grows, the curve sloping downward. Against this stands the cost of capital — the price of using a unit of it for a period, which is the interest forgone on the funds it ties up plus the depreciation it suffers, together its user cost, drawn here as a horizontal line. The desired capital stock is where the two meet: hold capital up to the point where the marginal product of the last unit just equals what it costs to hold, and no further. Beyond that point each added unit costs more than it yields; short of it, another unit would more than pay its way.

Capital is the productive detour through time — present consumption forgone to build the tools that yield more consumption later.

The concept has grown far beyond machines, and the growth is one of the great facts of modern economics. Human capital — the skills and knowledge embodied in people, accumulated through education and experience, the subject of the wage — is now reckoned the largest stock of all in advanced economies, dwarfing the physical. Intangible capital — software, knowledge, brands, patents, the organizational capacity of firms — has come to rival and surpass plant and equipment in value, and is far harder to see, let alone to count. Natural capital — the stock of environmental resources of the commons — is increasingly entered in the accounts. In every case the logic of the stock is the same: an asset accumulated by past investment, yielding a flow of services across time. But the further the concept stretches from the visible machine, the harder the stock becomes to measure — which is the trouble the next plates pursue.

the edge The clean rule — hold capital until its marginal product equals its cost — assumes that one can speak of "the marginal product of capital" as a definite quantity, which is exactly what the plate on the Cambridge controversy will throw into doubt once capital is heterogeneous. And a circularity lurks beneath the diagram: the cost of capital includes the rate of interest, but the rate of interest is itself in part the return to capital, so the picture that determines the capital stock from the interest rate and the picture that determines the interest rate from the capital stock chase one another in a circle. The desired stock set where marginal product meets cost is a powerful and indispensable first approximation — but it rests on foundations that the theory of capital, examined closely, finds it cannot quite secure.

Plate the Fourth · The Quantities · № IX

The Asset

valuation — the worth of any durable asset as the present value of the future flow it yields — Fisher

The plate asks what is a thing held worth, when its worth lies in the future?

The deepest question about any stock is what it is worth, and Irving Fisher gave the answer that organizes the whole of asset valuation: the value of a durable asset — any stock that yields a flow of income or service over time — is the present value of that future flow. The asset is worth the sum of all the yields it will deliver, each one discounted back to the present by the rate of interest, so that a dollar promised next year counts for somewhat less than a dollar in hand, and a dollar promised in a distant year for much less. A stock's value, on this principle, is simply its future, brought back to now and added up.

The principle is universal, the single key to the price of everything durable. A machine is worth the discounted stream of the outputs it will produce; a bond, the discounted stream of its coupons and its repayment; a parcel of land, the discounted stream of its rents, which is the capitalization of the rent volume; a share, the discounted stream of its dividends; a whole firm, the discounted stream of its profits; even the human capital embodied in a person, the discounted stream of their future earnings. In every case the price of the stock today is a forecast of flows tomorrow, collapsed into a single number by the act of discounting. The figure shows why the horizon matters so: one dollar received far in the future is worth very little today, the discount factor falling away toward nothing as the years recede.

A stock's value is its future brought to the present — the discounted sum of every flow it will yield, an entire imagined future collapsed into one number.

This is the exact mirror of accumulation, and together the two complete the stock's relation to time. The reservoir showed the stock as the past made present — the integral of the flows that have filled it. Valuation shows the stock as the future made present — the discounted sum of the flows it will yield. The rate of interest of an earlier volume is the hinge between them, the rate at which the future is converted into the present and the present compounded into the future. A stock thus stands at the meeting of two times, accumulated out of the past and valued by the future, and its physical quantity — so many machines — and its money value — so many dollars — are bridged only through the interest rate and a forecast of what is to come. The stock is a knot tied in time.

the edge Valuation by present value rests on two foundations, each treacherous, and their treachery is why the serenest identity in economics governs its most volatile quantity. It requires a forecast of the future flows — and the future is unknown, so every asset price embeds a guess about outputs or rents or profits or dividends that may never arrive. And it requires a discount rate — and a small change in the rate swings the present value enormously, most of all for flows in the distant future, so that the worth of a long-lived asset is exquisitely sensitive to the rate at which time itself is priced. Because both the forecast and the rate are uncertain and forever shifting, asset prices can wander far from any settled fundamental for years at a stretch — the bubbles and crashes of the spread and the premium — and the calm proposition that a stock is worth its discounted future turns out, in the living markets, to be the source of their wildest motion.

Plate the Fifth · The Quantities · № IX

The Sum

the measurement of capital — heterogeneity, aggregation, and the Cambridge capital controversy — Robinson, Sraffa

The plate asks can capital be measured as a single quantity?

To speak of "the capital stock" of a firm or a nation as a single number — the quantity of capital that enters a production function and earns its marginal product — one must add the capital up. And here the most basic of the stocks meets its most basic difficulty: capital is heterogeneous. Lathes and looms, locomotives and office towers, drills and warehouses cannot be summed in physical units, for there is no common physical measure of a press and a building. They can be added only in value — in dollars, by their prices. The capital stock, unlike a tonnage of grain or a count of workers, exists as a single quantity only when reckoned in money. And in that necessity lies the trouble.

For to value the capital goods, one needs their prices; and the price of a capital good is, by the valuation of the last plate, the present value of the future income it will yield — which depends on the rate of interest. But the rate of interest is itself the rate of return on capital, the very thing the quantity of capital was supposed to help determine. So the measurement of the capital stock presupposes the rate of interest, and the rate of interest presupposes the capital stock: the aggregate quantity of capital cannot be measured independently of the distribution of income between capital and labor that it was invoked to explain. The measuring rod is made of the very substance it is meant to measure, and the circle will not open.

Capital can be summed only in value, and its value depends on the interest rate — so the measure of capital presupposes the very return it was meant to explain.

This circularity erupted into one of the great disputes of twentieth-century economics, the Cambridge capital controversy — Cambridge in England, where Joan Robinson and Piero Sraffa pressed the attack, against Cambridge in Massachusetts, where Paul Samuelson and Robert Solow defended the aggregate. The English Cambridge showed that the textbook production function, with its single quantity of capital earning a well-defined marginal product, rests on assumptions that need not hold. The phenomenon of reswitching settled it in logic: the same technique of production can be the most profitable one at both a low and a high rate of interest and yet not at the rates between, so that there is no orderly, one-directional relation between the "quantity of capital" and the rate of return — and hence no coherent aggregate capital that behaves like a factor with a definite marginal product. The Massachusetts Cambridge largely conceded the logic, and largely went on using aggregate capital all the same.

the edge The Cambridge controversy rests in an uncomfortable limbo, and what to make of it is disputed still. On the strict logic the critics were right: there is no general, theoretically clean measure of aggregate capital, and the textbook production function with its quantity of capital and its marginal product is, at the level of the whole economy, not rigorously founded. Yet aggregate capital is used everywhere — in growth accounting, in macroeconomics, in the national statistics — because some measure is needed and the approximation serves well enough in practice. The honest position is that the aggregate capital stock is a useful fiction whose foundations will not bear close inspection, a number that economics can neither do without nor fully justify — so that the measurement of the most basic of all economic stocks remains, at the deepest level, unsolved, and the discipline proceeds by a convenience it knows to be unsound.

Plate the Sixth · The Quantities · № IX

The Accelerator

investment — the flow that builds the stock, its determinants, and why it swings with the change in output

The plate asks what determines investment, and why does it swing so violently?

Investment is the flow that builds the capital stock — the construction of new plant, the purchase of new equipment, the raising of new structures. Gross investment is the whole of it; net investment is gross investment less depreciation, the actual addition to the stock once the wearing-out of the old is allowed for. What determines how much is undertaken? At the margin, the answer is the desired-stock logic of the capital plate: a firm invests up to the point where the return on the marginal unit of new capital equals its cost. James Tobin gave the same condition a financial face in the ratio that bears his name — invest while the market value of installed capital exceeds the cost of replacing it, while his q stands above one — for then the market is saying that a dollar of new capital is worth more than a dollar.

But the most striking fact about investment is its violence, and the accelerator explains it. Suppose firms hold a capital stock proportional to their output — so much plant for so much production. Then investment, which is the change in the capital stock, depends not on the level of output but on its change. The figure draws the consequence, and it is dramatic. When output grows steadily, investment is positive but steady; when output's growth merely slows — not output falling, only its rise flattening — investment drops sharply; and when growth resumes, investment surges. A small wobble in the rate at which output grows becomes a large swing in investment. Investment is, in effect, the derivative of a desired stock that tracks output, and the operation of differentiation magnifies every change.

Investment tracks not output but the change in output — so a mere slowing of growth can collapse it, and a mere quickening can make it surge.

This is why investment is the most volatile component of output and a principal engine of the business cycle of a later volume. Because investment responds to the change in output, and its own swings in turn change output, the accelerator and the multiplier can drive each other into booms and slumps: rising output spurs investment, which lifts output further, which spurs investment more — until growth slows, investment collapses, and the spiral runs in reverse. The flow that builds the patient, slow-moving stock of capital is itself the most impatient and restless quantity in the whole economy, and its restlessness, amplified by the accelerator and broadcast through the multiplier, is much of what makes the aggregate economy lurch from expansion to contraction and back.

the edge The accelerator in its crude form is too mechanical to be quite right, and its refinements matter. Firms do not adjust their capital stock to the desired level instantly: adjustment is costly, capital is ordered and built with long lags, and it is expectations of future output, not merely the latest change, that drive the decision to invest — so the simple proportionality of investment to the change in output is softened in practice into something more sluggish and more forward-looking. Tobin's q, which folds the whole relevant future into the market's valuation of capital, is in principle the completer account, though it founders on the same gap between market value and fundamental value that troubles all asset pricing. That investment is volatile and bound to the change in output is robust and important; the exact mechanism that links them remains a matter of continuing refinement.

Plate the Seventh · The Quantities · № IX

The Decay

depreciation — the stock wears out, gross against net investment, and the steady state where they balance

The plate asks if a stock wears out, what does it take merely to stand still?

Every capital stock wears out. Machines age, break, and fall obsolete; buildings decay; equipment is used up in the using. Depreciation is the flow of this wearing-away — the consumption of capital, the rate at which the stock would shrink were nothing done to replace it. It is a flow, so much capital consumed per year, and it is the dark twin of investment: where investment is the inflow that builds the stock, depreciation is the outflow that drains it, the second pipe on the reservoir. No stock of capital simply endures; left untended, it runs down, and standing still requires effort.

On this turns the distinction between gross and net investment. Gross investment is all the new capital built; net investment is gross investment minus depreciation — the actual change in the stock once replacement of the worn-out is subtracted. A nation may invest heavily in gross terms and yet add almost nothing to its capital, if most of that building merely makes good what has decayed. The difference is large in the measurement of an economy: gross product counts the building of replacement capital as output, and rightly, since the work is really done — but a society that builds only enough to replace what wears out is, in net terms, standing perfectly still, running hard to stay in place. The flow can be furious while the stock holds constant, because the inflow is exactly swallowed by the drain.

Gross investment that merely replaces what has worn out adds nothing to the stock — a society can build furiously and, in net terms, stand perfectly still.

This defines the rest point of all accumulation, the steady state. The steady state is where net investment is zero — where gross investment exactly equals depreciation, and the capital stock holds constant, neither growing nor shrinking. It is the level toward which a stock tends once the forces that build it and the forces that wear it come into balance, and it is the organizing idea of the theory of growth in a later volume: an economy left to its own devices approaches a steady-state capital stock at which just enough is invested to offset depreciation and to equip its new workers. The steady state is the stock at rest — the equilibrium of the reservoir, inflow matched to outflow, the level standing still though both pipes run full. It is where the bathtub settles when the tap and the drain are reconciled.

the edge The steady state is an illuminating abstraction the actual world never quite occupies, and its idealizations deserve naming. Depreciation itself is far harder to measure than the tidy constant rate of the models suggests — capital wears out at rates that depend on how hard it is used, how well it is maintained, and the unpredictable pace of obsolescence, and a machine rendered worthless overnight by a better one has fully depreciated though it still runs sweetly. And the very notion of a single steady-state capital stock inherits all the troubles of aggregating capital from the plate on the Cambridge controversy. The steady state is the resting point the system is forever approaching and never reaching, a center of gravity rather than a destination — useful precisely because it names where the flows would carry the stock if they ever stopped changing, which, in a living economy, they never do.

Plate the Eighth · The Quantities · № IX

The Buffer

inventories — why a stock is held against uncertainty, the optimal level, and the inventory cycle

The plate asks why hold a stock at all, when holding is costly?

Holding a stock is costly. It ties up resources that might have earned a return, occupies space, courts spoilage and obsolescence, and must be financed and guarded. So why hold one? Because a stock buffers the mismatch between flows. Inventory is the clearest case: production and sales are two flows that rarely match moment to moment, and inventory is the reservoir set between them, absorbing the difference — filling when production runs ahead of sales, drawing down when sales run ahead of production. The stock is held as insurance against the unevenness and the unpredictability of the flows it stands between, a cushion that lets production proceed steadily though sales arrive in fits, and lets sales be met though production comes in batches.

How much to hold balances the cost of holding against the cost of running out. Too thin a stock courts stockouts — lost sales, idled production lines, customers turned away; too thick a stock wastes the carrying cost. The optimal inventory minimizes the sum of the two, and the classic figure of inventory management — the sawtooth shown here — captures its rhythm. The stock is drawn down steadily as sales deplete it, then replenished all at once when it falls to a reorder point, then drawn down again, tracing a sawtooth across time; the average stock held works out to about half the size of each order. The economic order quantity strikes the balance between the fixed cost incurred each time one orders and the running cost of carrying what one holds.

A stock is held because flows do not match — inventory is the reservoir between production and sales, insurance against the unevenness of both.

Small as inventories are in the total stock of a nation's wealth, they play an outsized part in the business cycle of a later volume. Because firms hold inventory in proportion to their expected sales, a change in sales — or even in expected sales — triggers a magnified change in inventory investment, the accelerator over again, now applied to stocks of goods. When demand softens, firms not only produce less but run their inventories down, so production falls by more than sales did; when demand recovers, they rebuild their stocks, so production rises by more than sales. This inventory cycle — the swing from destocking to restocking — is among the sharpest and most regular of all short-run fluctuations, a small stock whose adjustments amplify the motion of the whole economy far beyond their own modest size.

the edge The tidy logic of the optimal buffer has been pressed hard by practice in ways that reveal its limits. The just-in-time revolution showed that much inventory once thought necessary was simply waste, and that stocks could be driven far lower by tightening the coordination of the flows — trading the buffer of held inventory for the buffer of fast, reliable resupply. But the same revolution exposed the cost of thin buffers when the flows are disrupted: a supply shock that idles an entire production line for want of a single missing part, as recent disruptions made vivid to all. The right size of a buffer depends entirely on the cost and the reliability of the flows it cushions, and as those shift — as resupply grows cheaper or shocks grow likelier — the optimal stock swings with them, with no settled answer. The stock held against uncertainty is itself hostage to a deeper uncertainty: how much uncertainty to expect.

Plate the Ninth · The Quantities · № IX

The Wealth

wealth as a stock — its distribution, and the divergence when returns outrun growth — Piketty

The plate asks what changes when wealth, not work, becomes the measure of a society?

Wealth is the stock of which income is the flow — the accumulated value of all that a person or a society owns, the capital and land and housing and financial claims, net of debts. It stands apart from income at every turn, as the snapshot plate insisted: one may hold a great fortune and earn little from it, an idle estate; or earn a large salary and hold nothing, a high flow spent as fast as it comes. Wealth is accumulated saving, the deposit the past has made in the present, and the way it is distributed across a society is among the most consequential of all economic facts — more consequential, in some respects, than the distribution of the income flow, because it determines not what people currently receive but what they securely command.

Wealth is almost everywhere far more concentrated than income — the stock more unequal than the flow. The reason is partly arithmetic and partly historical. Wealth accumulates, and small differences in the rate of saving and the rate of return compound over a lifetime, and across generations, into vast differences in the stock; and inheritance passes the accumulated stock forward intact, so that the distribution of wealth carries the inequalities of the past into the present in a way the distribution of income, earned afresh each year, does not. The concentration of the stock confers not merely consumption but position — security, independence, power, freedom from the discipline of the wage — so that the shape of the wealth distribution structures a society as deeply as the shape of the income distribution rewards its current effort, the theme the volume on the share will take up directly.

Wealth is the past's deposit in the present — and because it accumulates and is inherited, the stock carries forward inequalities the flow of income alone never could.

Thomas Piketty has pressed a relation between this stock and its flow with large consequences. When the rate of return on wealth exceeds the rate of growth of income — when r runs ahead of g — the stock of accumulated wealth tends to grow faster than the flow of income against which it is measured, and so to loom ever larger relative to it. Past accumulation then outpaces present effort: the wealth amassed and inherited from earlier times grows more important than the income earned in the present, the ratio of wealth to income climbs, and wealth concentrates further still, since the largest fortunes are the ones that compound the fastest. Whether the return reliably exceeds the growth rate, and what follows if it does, is much disputed — but the kernel is a pure piece of stock-and-flow logic: when a stock grows faster than the flow it is measured against, the stock comes in time to dominate, and a society of inherited position quietly displaces a society of earned reward.

the edge The wealth-and-growth thesis has been among the most contested in recent economics, and the disputes turn on exactly the stock-flow subtleties of this volume. Critics ask whether the rate of return holds up as wealth accumulates — for the diminishing returns to capital of the capital plate should pull the return down as the stock grows — whether the measured rise in wealth-to-income ratios reflects genuine accumulation or merely the rising price of existing assets like land and housing, and whether human capital, the great unmeasured stock embodied in skills, belongs in the reckoning, which would change the picture entirely. What is not in doubt is that the distinction between the stock of wealth and the flow of income, and the dynamics by which a stock can grow faster or slower than its flow, lie at the heart of the deepest questions of distribution — and that these are, at bottom, stock-flow questions, asked of the largest stock there is.

Plate the Tenth · The Quantities · № IX

The Hoard

money as a stock — the demand to hold it, liquidity preference, and the opportunity cost of the interest rate — Keynes

The plate asks why hold money, which earns nothing?

Money is a stock — a quantity held at a moment — and spending is the flow that money finances. The money stock of an economy, the quantity of money in being at an instant, is distinct from the flow of spending it supports, the two related by the speed at which money changes hands, the subject of the volume on money. But the sharpest puzzle is at the level of the single holder. Money in its pure form earns no interest, while the bonds and assets one might hold instead do; to hold a stock of money is therefore to forgo a return. Why, then, hold any — why keep a hoard of barren cash when wealth could be held in forms that grow?

John Maynard Keynes gave the answer in his theory of liquidity preference: money is held for its liquidity — its immediate and certain usability, its readiness to be spent at any moment without conversion, delay, or loss. People hold money for transactions, to bridge the gap between income received at intervals and spending made continuously; for precaution, as a reserve against unforeseen needs and shocks; and, Keynes argued, for speculation, to wait in safety for a better moment to buy other assets. Money is the buffer stock of the previous plate in its most liquid form, a reserve held against the unevenness and uncertainty of the flows of receipt and payment. The figure shows the demand to hold it: the higher the rate of interest, the greater the return given up by holding money rather than bonds, and so the less money is held — the demand for money slopes downward against the interest rate.

Money is the most liquid stock — held not only to spend but as a reserve against an uncertain future, at the price of the interest it forgoes.

The rate of interest is thus the price of holding money — the opportunity cost of liquidity, the yield surrendered for the convenience of keeping wealth in instantly spendable form. This makes the demand for money the bridge between the money stock and the interest rate, and so a hinge of monetary policy and of the determination of the interest rate itself, in the volumes on the level and on money. When money is made more plentiful, its holders, finding they now hold more liquidity than they wish at the going rate, bid for other assets, and that bidding drives the interest rate down until they are content to hold the larger stock. The willingness to hold the money stock, at each rate of interest, is the lever that monetary policy works upon. Liquidity is a service the stock of money renders, and the interest rate is the price the holder pays for it.

the edge Liquidity preference is illuminating, but its foundations have shifted, and the shifting matters. The sharp line between non-interest-bearing money and interest-bearing assets has blurred as money itself came to pay interest and near-monies multiplied, so that the opportunity cost of holding money is now far smaller and far less clear than Keynes assumed. The speculative motive — holding cash to await a fall in asset prices — has been questioned as a basis for the demand, since one might wait instead in short-term interest-bearing assets. And at the zero lower bound on interest rates, where money and bonds yield nearly alike, the demand for money goes flat and the whole apparatus changes character — the liquidity trap, where adding to the money stock accomplishes nothing because there is no longer any return being forgone. The demand to hold money is real, but exactly what drives it, and what it costs, depends on a financial landscape that keeps changing beneath the theory.

Plate the Eleventh · The Quantities · № IX

The Accounting

stock-flow consistency — the deficit is not the debt, and the discipline of never confusing them

The plate asks why is a falling deficit still a growing debt?

The discipline the whole volume has been building toward is stock-flow consistency: the requirement, simple to state and ceaselessly violated, that the stocks and the flows in any account fit together exactly — that the change in every stock equal the net of its flows, with nothing created or lost in the gap between them. Every flow comes from somewhere and goes somewhere; every stock is the accumulation of the flows that have moved it. A consistent account leaves no flow without a stock it changes and no change in a stock without a flow that caused it. This is mere bookkeeping — and it is, all the same, the most reliably violated principle in public argument about the economy.

The gravest and most familiar violation is the confusion of the deficit with the debt. The deficit is a flow — the amount by which spending exceeds revenue in a single year. The debt is a stock — the accumulated total owed, the running sum of all past deficits net of all past surpluses. The figure sets the two side by side: the deficit is the annual flow into the debt, and the debt is the integral of the deficits, the reservoir they fill. And here is the implication so constantly missed: as long as the deficit is positive, the debt grows, even when the deficit is shrinking. A falling deficit is still a rising debt — the inflow into the reservoir is slowing, but the level is still climbing. "The deficit fell this year" and "the debt has never been higher" are both true, at the same moment and forever, and the failure to hold the stock and the flow apart muddles very nearly every public argument about the public finances.

A falling deficit is still a rising debt — the inflow is slowing, but the level is still climbing, and both statements are true at once.

Stock-flow consistency is not a fact about governments alone but a universal accounting discipline that binds the whole of macroeconomics together. One sector's deficit is another's surplus; one agent's borrowing is another's lending; the flow of saving accumulates into the stock of wealth, the flow of investment into the stock of capital, the flow of the current account into the stock of foreign assets. The modern macroeconomic models that take this seriously — the stock-flow consistent models — insist that every flow be traced to the stock it changes and every stock to the flows that move it, so that the accounts close and nothing leaks. The discipline is humble and powerful at once: a great deal of economic confusion, and not a little bad policy, comes from losing track of whether a quantity is a stock or a flow, and from accounts that fail to balance because a flow was forgotten or a stock was counted twice. To keep the books consistent is, in the end, simply to think clearly.

the edge Stock-flow consistency is an accounting truth, not an economic theory, and its very rigor marks the boundary of what accounting can do. It guarantees that the quantities fit together; it says nothing whatever about whether they are good or sustainable. A consistent account can describe a debt growing without bound as faithfully as one shrinking to nothing — consistency constrains the arithmetic, not the wisdom. The harder questions the books cannot answer — when a debt is too large, when a deficit is prudent or reckless, whether a rising wealth-to-income ratio is healthy or ominous — demand judgment, theory, and value, which no bookkeeping supplies. But the accounting is the indispensable floor: an argument that violates stock-flow consistency is simply wrong, whatever its conclusion, and getting the stocks and the flows to fit is the minimum price of admission to a serious economic claim. Consistency is necessary, and it is never sufficient.

Plate the Twelfth · The Quantities · № IX

The Bequest

the stock made whole — the past accumulated and the future redeemed, and the close of the atlas

The plate asks what, in the end, is a stock — and now that the table is full, what has the atlas been?

A stock is the quantity held at a moment, and this volume has followed it through its forms: the snapshot set against the film; the reservoir of accumulation; capital and its valuation; the sum that cannot quite be taken; the flow that builds and the flow that wears away; the buffer held against uncertainty; wealth, and money, and the accounting that keeps them straight. It remains to ask what a stock is, taken whole — and, this being the last plate of the last volume, what the atlas of which it is the end has been.

A stock, first, is the past made present. It is the integral of bygone flows: capital the sum of past investment, wealth the sum of past saving, debt the sum of past deficits, every reservoir the running total of all that has flowed into it less all that has drained away. To hold a stock is to hold the accumulated history of the flows that made it — the present quantity is the past, congealed and counted. Stocks have inertia and memory; they move slowly where flows move quickly, and they carry forward, into every present moment, the whole record of what has been.

A stock, second, is the future made present. Its value is the discounted sum of the flows it will yield — the asset worth its coming outputs, the bond its coupons, the land its rents, the firm its profits, each pulled back to now by the rate of interest. A stock thus stands at the meeting of two times, accumulated out of the past and valued by the future, a knot tied in time and bound by the rate at which time is priced. To weigh a stock is to weigh, at a single instant, an entire history behind it and an entire imagined future before it — which is why the calmest identity in economics, that a stock is worth its discounted future, governs in practice its most restless quantity.

From this the volume's practical lesson follows, the plainest the atlas has to give: to think clearly in economics is, above all, to know whether one is looking at a stock or a flow, and never to confuse the photograph with the film. Wealth is not income; the debt is not the deficit; capital is not investment; the money stock is not spending. The commonest and most consequential errors in the subject — in public argument most of all — are confusions of a quantity at a moment with a quantity through time, and the simple discipline of asking which one is before us dissolves a remarkable quantity of muddle.

What, then, is known of the stock, and what is not? The framework is a triumph of clarity: accumulation understood as integration, valuation as discounting, consistency as bookkeeping, and from these few clean ideas the whole of capital theory, asset pricing, growth, and macroeconomic accounting is organized. And the humility is just as real: the most basic stock, capital, cannot be coherently aggregated, as the Cambridge controversy showed; the value of any stock rests on an unknowable future and a volatile discount rate, as the bubbles of the asset plate attest; and the largest stocks of the modern world, the human and intangible capital embodied in skills and knowledge, are the hardest of all to measure. The framework is lucid; the quantities it organizes remain elusive.

The Stock is the fourth and last of The Quantities, and the one most deeply bound to time. It completes the group: it stands beneath The Margin, where capital is hired to its marginal product and the stock is optimized at its edge; beside The Surplus, the value a market realizes from its trades; beneath The Utility, where the discounting of a future satisfaction is the very operation that values a stock. And it reaches across the whole atlas — to The Rate, the interest that prices time and bridges every stock to its flows; to The Wage, where labor's own stock is its human capital; to The Growth, where accumulation is the engine of all advance; to The Share, where the distribution of the stock of wealth is the deepest question of justice; to The Money and The Cycle, where the money stock and the inventory stock move the whole. The stock is the quantity in which all the times of economics meet.

What is a stock? The quantity held at a moment — and, like every quantity this atlas has weighed, a verdict on a hidden contest: between the stock and the flow, between the past accumulated and the future redeemed, between what is held and what is earned, between the moment and the time that gives it worth. To hold a stock is to hold a knot of time, the whole past poured into it and the whole future discounted back, reckoned at a single instant. The argument, here as in every volume before it, remains open.

And here a word is owed to the whole, for this is the last plate of the last volume. Twenty-six elements, ranged in six groups — the prices and the quantities, the failures and the institutions, the aggregates and the behaviors — have been set out as a periodic table of economic life: the wage and the rent and the rate, the premium and the spread; the margin, the surplus, the utility, the stock; the powers and frictions that break the markets, and the firms and contracts and monies and rules that build them; the cycles and growth and levels and shares of the whole; and the choices, biases, games, and norms of the minds within it. Each was a quantity that some price or number is supposed to measure, and each, examined closely, was found to be not a simple fact read off the world but a verdict — the settled-for-now outcome of forces held in tension. What the atlas has tried to show, element by element, is that the numbers economics reports are not neutral readings but the results of contests, and that to take them for bare facts is to miss the argument folded inside each one. The table is now full. It is offered not as a set of answers but as a way of seeing: a claim that beneath every economic quantity there lies a question, and that the question stays open. Here the atlas ends. The argument does not.
