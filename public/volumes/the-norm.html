the unwritten rules

The question of the volume Which of the market's rules are written, and which merely understood?

in print · twelve plates
Numéraire · Volume XXVI

The Argument

The volume on the contract ended with a confession: that most of what holds the economy together is not written down. The contracts sit in their drawers, the statutes in their books, and yet the daily business of a market — the deliveries made on a word, the bills paid without a threat, the quality given when no one is watching, the stranger trusted on the strength of nothing but the expectation that he will deal as people deal — runs on something the law never wrote and the contract never specified. That something is the subject of this last volume, and it is the most pervasive and the least visible thing in all of economic life: the norm, the unwritten rule, the done thing, the shared expectation held quietly in common. We have spent the table denaturalizing the written rules — the prices, the institutions, the laws that build the market. We end at the rules that were never written at all, and discover that they are the ground on which the rest of it stands.

A norm is a rule of behavior that a community enforces upon itself without the apparatus of the state. It is not a law, for no legislature passed it and no court will hear a suit upon it; it is not a contract, for no one signed it and it binds those who never agreed. It is, rather, a shared understanding of how one is to act — to keep one's word, to take one's turn, to repay a kindness, to deal honestly, to refrain from the thing that is not done — sustained not by the sheriff but by the approval and disapproval of others, by the fear of a name spoiled and a face lost, and by the voice within that calls a thing shameful before any other voice is raised. Douglass North taught economists to see that the rules of the game come in two kinds: the formal institutions written on the page, which The Rule examined, and the informal institutions held in common, which are the norms. A society runs on both, and where the formal rules are weak or silent — which, as the contract showed, is most of the time — it runs very largely on the informal.

A norm is a rule no legislature passed and no court will enforce, binding those who never agreed to it — sustained by the approval of others and the voice within that calls a thing shameful before any other voice is raised.

That people follow such rules at all is the puzzle the volume must answer, for many a norm asks us to act against our narrow interest — to tell the truth when a lie would pay, to repay when we might default, to contribute when we might free-ride. The answer is threefold. Some of the enforcement is internal: norms are taken in, made part of the self, so that to violate them is to feel guilt or shame regardless of whether anyone sees — the conscience as the enforcer that never sleeps. Some of it is external: the watching eyes of others, the gossip and the reputation and the ostracism that fall on the one who breaks the code, the shadow of the future that the contract already showed disciplines the present. And some of it is stranger and more remarkable than either — the willingness of people to punish violators even at a cost to themselves, to reject an unfair offer though refusing it leaves them poorer, to spend their own resources to see a cheat brought low. This costly, seemingly irrational enforcement, which the laboratory has documented again and again, turns out to be among the deepest facts about our species, and one of the foundations on which cooperation rests.

Where the norms come from is a question with a surprising answer: mostly, from no one. A few are designed and promulgated, but the great body of them emerges spontaneously, the way a path is worn across a field by feet that never agreed to make it — each person doing what the others do because the others do it, until a regularity hardens into a rule and the rule acquires the force of an obligation. David Hume saw this of the conventions of justice and property; Friedrich Hayek made it the center of his thought; and the students of cultural evolution have shown how norms of cooperation can spread and stabilize across generations without any mind having intended them. Norms are grown, not made — which is why they are so hard to design and so hard to abolish, and why a society's deepest rules are precisely the ones no one can quite remember adopting.

What the norms do, when they are good, is everything the contract could not. They make trust possible, and trust, as Kenneth Arrow observed, is the lubricant of every economic system — the thing that lets exchange occur without the ruinous cost of verifying and enforcing every term. Where the norms of honesty and fair dealing are strong, transactions are cheap and cooperation is wide; where they are weak, every deal must be policed, and the policing eats the gains. Norms govern the commons that neither market nor state governs well, as Elinor Ostrom showed of the communities that have managed forests and fisheries for centuries by rules of their own. They are, in the aggregate, what some have called social capital, and their presence or absence may explain as much about why some societies prosper as any factor the earlier volumes named. But norms are not always good, and the volume will not pretend they are. The same machinery that sustains honesty can sustain corruption; the same conformity that enforces fair dealing can enforce discrimination, silence, and cruelty; and a bad norm, once established, can be as self-enforcing and as stubborn as a good one, trapping a society in an arrangement that everyone privately deplores and no one will be the first to break.

The thesis of this final volume, and through it of the table entire, is that beneath all the visible apparatus of the economy — the prices and quantities, the institutions and aggregates this work has anatomized — there lies an invisible substructure of shared expectation without which none of it would function. The money is accepted because we all expect it to be; the contract is kept because honesty is the done thing; the property is respected, the queue is honored, the market clears, because a dense and mostly unspoken web of norms tells each of us how the others will behave and what will befall us if we behave otherwise. The invisible hand, it turns out, rests on an invisible substructure, and that substructure is the norm. And the numéraire of honesty that this whole work has sought — the standard of true reckoning against which the verdicts hidden in prices might at last be read — is itself, at bottom, a norm: the keystone norm of honest dealing and honest thought, the unwritten rule that we will not cheat the count. The table ends, as it must, at the rules that were never written, which like every element within it were not given by nature but grown by us, and which therefore could be, and may yet be made, otherwise. The argument remains open.

Plate the First · The Behaviors · № XXVI

The Unspoken

what a norm is; the unwritten rule; informal versus formal institutions

The plate asks if a rule is in no book and no court will hear it, in what sense is it a rule at all?

Begin with what a norm is, for it sits between two things we have already named and is exactly neither. It is not a law. No legislature passed it, no text records it, no court will hear a suit upon its breach; the state that stands behind the contract and the statute does not stand behind the norm. And it is not a contract. No one signed it, no consideration was exchanged for it, and it binds people who never agreed to it and never could have — for one does not opt into the expectation that one will keep one's word or take one's turn or deal honestly; one is simply held to it by being a member of the community that holds it. A norm is a third kind of rule: a shared understanding of how a person is to act, enforced not by the law's coercion but by the softer and stranger powers of approval and disapproval, reputation, and the conscience that has taken the rule inside.

Douglass North gave economists the frame that makes sense of this. The rules of the game, he said, come in two kinds. There are the formal institutions — the constitutions, the statutes, the property rights, the enforceable contracts — that The Rule examined, written down and backed by the organized force of the state. And there are the informal institutions — the norms, conventions, customs, codes of conduct, and taboos — held in common and enforced by society itself. A functioning economy runs on both, intertwined; but the proportion between them is not what a lawyer might guess. The formal rules are the visible tip. The informal rules are the great submerged mass, governing the vast reach of conduct that no statute touches and no contract specifies — which is to say, as the volume on the contract showed, nearly all of it.

The reason norms are so easily overlooked is precisely that they work so well. We notice a law, for it is written and enforced in the open, with penalties and officers and courts. We do not notice a norm at all, until it is broken. The queue we join without being told, the tip we leave without being billed, the greeting we return, the appointment we keep, the honest weight the grocer gives, the change the stranger hands back — these are the water we swim in, felt not as rules at all but as the way things simply are. And that feeling, that a norm is not a rule but a fact of the world, is the deepest naturalization this table has had to confront. The whole work has labored to show that prices are not facts of nature but verdicts of contests; here, at the foundation, we meet the rules that feel most natural of all, the done things that present themselves as obvious and eternal — and that are, no less than the prices, human constructions, grown and enforced and alterable.

We notice laws, for they are written and enforced in the open. The norms we do not notice at all — they are the water we swim in, the done thing that feels like the way things simply are. Which is why they are the hardest of all to see as made.

What makes a norm a rule, and not a mere suggestion, is that it is enforced — not by the law, but enforced all the same. A regularity that carries no sanction is a habit or a fashion; a norm carries a sanction, and the sanction is what gives it its grip. The plates that follow will anatomize the enforcement — the conscience within, the watching community without, and the remarkable willingness of people to punish a violator even at a cost to themselves. But the point to fix here is that the bindingness of a norm comes from this enforcement and not from any writing, so that an unwritten rule, socially enforced, can be every bit as real and every bit as constraining as a written one. The norm is not the law's soft shadow. It is an institution in its own right — a structure of shared expectation that shapes what we do as surely as any code, and over a far wider field.

the edge The line between the norm and the law is not as firm as the contrast suggests, and the traffic between them runs both ways, which is worth seeing at the outset. Much of what is now law began as norm — the customary practices of merchants hardened into commercial law, the conventions of decent dealing codified into statute, the moral expectation written at last into a code. And much of what is now norm is the residue of old law, or the shadow the law casts, the formal rule internalized until it is followed from habit and conscience rather than fear of penalty. The two are less separate institutions than two states of the same thing — a rule of conduct, sometimes written and coercively enforced, sometimes unwritten and socially enforced, often both at once, and forever passing from the one condition to the other. This matters because it warns against two opposite errors. The first is to imagine that the formal rules are what really govern us, the norms a soft supplement — when in truth the norms do the greater part of the work, and the law floats upon them as the written contract floats upon trust. The second is to imagine that because norms are unwritten they are weak — when a strong norm, enforced by shame and the watching community, can bind more tightly than any statute, reaching into the unobserved corners of conduct that no law could ever police. The unwritten rule is not the lesser kind of rule; it is, for most of what we do with one another, the only kind there is.

Plate the Second · The Behaviors · № XXVI

The Convention

coordination norms; the self-enforcing rule; which side of the road

The plate asks if it does not matter which rule we follow, only that we follow the same one, where does the rule come from?

The simplest norms are the conventions, and they are the place to begin because their workings lie open to view. A convention is the solution to a coordination problem — a situation in which each of us wants above all to do the same as the others, and it scarcely matters what that is, only that we agree. The cleanest case is the side of the road. It is of no importance whether a nation drives on the left or the right; what matters, enormously, is that everyone in it drives on the same side. The figure draws the choice. Two drivers approaching each other each pick a side; if they pick the same, they pass in safety; if they pick differently, they collide. There are two ways to get it right and two ways to get it catastrophically wrong, and the whole problem is to land together on one of the two right ones.

Two features of the convention follow from the figure, and they are the signature of this whole class of norm. The first is that it is arbitrary: nothing in the nature of things favored the right side over the left, and a nation that drives on one is in no way wiser than a nation that drives on the other. The second is that, arbitrary in origin, it is absolute in force: once a nation has settled on a side, the rule binds every driver with a completeness no statute could surpass, and binds him not because the law commands it — though the law may also command it — but because everyone else is there, and to depart is to crash. The convention enforces itself. This is what sets it apart from the harder norms the later plates will treat, the norms of honesty and cooperation that run against self-interest and so require a punisher; a convention runs with self-interest once it is established, and asks no enforcement beyond the mutual expectation that sustains it.

Where does such a rule come from, if no one need impose it? Mostly it grows. David Hume gave the classic account, and chose his examples from the heart of the economy: the value of money, the institution of property, the keeping of the rules of justice — none of these, he argued, rests on a contract or a command, for all arise as conventions do, gradually and without agreement, from the repeated experience that it serves each to act as the others act. Two men who row a boat, he wrote, keep time by a convention though they never promised it to each other. Money is accepted because each expects the next to accept it, in the self-fulfilling circle the volume on money called faith. Where no precedent yet exists, Thomas Schelling showed, people coordinate on whatever option is salient — the focal point, the obvious place to meet, the round number, the natural Schelling point on which expectations converge without a word. By precedent and salience and the slow sediment of repeated dealing, conventions crystallize, and once crystallized they are as binding as anything in the social world.

Drive on the right, and you would be mad to drive on the left — not because the right is right, but because everyone else is there. A convention is arbitrary in its origin and absolute in its grip.

The economy is built through and through on such conventions. Money is one, as we have seen; so are the units in which we reckon — the meter, the measures, the standards and protocols that The Rule traced — and the meanings of the words in which we deal, and the trading hours and customary terms and settled practices of every market and trade. These are the coordination infrastructure of exchange, the shared expectations that let strangers transact without renegotiating from nothing each time, and they are mostly the work of no one, grown rather than made. They show, against the picture of the market as a thing that must be wholly designed and enforced, that a great part of its order arises spontaneously, self-organizing out of mutual expectation. And they carry the table's lesson into its strangest corner: that what feels most fixed and necessary — the medium, the measure, the settled way things are done — is often a mere convention, an arbitrary historical settlement frozen into a rule, which could have set otherwise and feels inevitable only because we have forgotten that it was ever open.

the edge The convention is the gentlest of norms and the most revealing, for it shows that a rule can govern absolutely without anyone willing it, enforcing it, or even being able to say why it is the rule. No one decided which side of the road a nation would drive on; the convention grew, hardened, and now binds every driver with a force no statute could improve upon — and binds them not because anyone commands it but because each expects all the others to follow it and would be a fool to do otherwise. This is order without an orderer, the spontaneous coordination that Hume saw in the rowers who keep time without a word and Hayek made the cornerstone of his account of society: a vast portion of the structure within which we act was authored by no one, emerging from the simple fact that it pays each of us to do what the rest are already doing. The lesson cuts two ways. It humbles the designer, for much of the market's order needs no design, arising of itself from mutual expectation as a path is worn across a common. But it unsettles the fatalist, for it shows that what feels most fixed — the measure, the medium, the settled way of doing things — is often merely a convention, arbitrary in its origin however absolute in its grip, a historical accident frozen into a rule, which could have crystallized otherwise and might, with enough of us moving together, crystallize otherwise yet.

Plate the Third · The Behaviors · № XXVI

The Reciprocity

the deepest norm; the gift and the return; tit for tat

The plate asks why do we return a kindness, and how does the return find its way back when the giver and the repayer are not the same?

If conventions are the gentlest norms, reciprocity is the deepest, and it may be the oldest social rule our species keeps. Return a kindness; repay a gift; answer help with help and, often enough, harm with harm. The anthropologists find some version of this in every human society, and Marcel Mauss, studying the gift economies that precede and underlie the market, showed that the gift is never truly free: to give is to oblige, to receive is to be bound, and to fail to return is to be shamed. The gift creates a debt that no contract records but that everyone feels, and the cycle of giving and returning weaves the web of obligation on which a society of exchange is built. Long before there was a contract, there was the gift and its return, governed by a rule older than any law.

In its simplest form reciprocity is direct: I help you, and you, in time, help me. This is the logic the contract met under the name of the shadow of the future, and Robert Axelrod gave it its sharpest expression when he set strategies to compete in a repeated prisoner's dilemma and found that the winner was the simplest reciprocator of all. Tit-for-tat — cooperate on the first move, then do whatever the other did last time — beat every cleverer scheme, and it did so by being nice, never defecting first; retaliatory, always answering a defection; forgiving, returning to cooperation the moment the other did; and clear, so that any partner could see at once that cheating did not pay. Reciprocity, formalized as tit-for-tat, sustains cooperation in any relationship expected to last, by making each party's future treatment depend on its present conduct.

But reciprocity reaches further than the bilateral pair, and its longer reach is what makes it the foundation of a whole community's cooperation. The figure shows the extension. A helps B; but the help that returns to A comes not from B at all — it comes from C, who saw or heard of A's good conduct and so is willing to help A in turn. The return travels round the circle rather than straight back, and what carries it is reputation: by helping, A earns a good name, and a good name draws help from others who were never helped themselves. This is indirect reciprocity, and the biologists Nowak and Sigmund, building on Richard Alexander, showed how it can sustain cooperation across an entire community, each person helping those of good repute and withholding from those of bad.

This indirect form is of the first importance, for it is the bridge from the cooperation of pairs to the cooperation of whole societies, and it is, very nearly, what we mean by a moral system. The injunction to do good carries the promise that good will come back — not, as the child supposes, from the very person one helped, but from a world that keeps a rough account of one's conduct and metes out its help and its withholding accordingly. The reputation systems of commerce, the credit ratings and the reviews and the references the contract relied upon, are indirect reciprocity made explicit; and the older, vaguer reputation that attaches to a name in a community is the same mechanism running on gossip and memory. Reciprocity, in its direct and indirect forms together, is the engine that turns the prudence of self-interested parties into the cooperation of a society.

Before there was a contract there was the gift, and the gift bound by a rule older than any law: that a kindness must be returned. The market is this ancient norm formalized — reciprocity made immediate, balanced, and explicit.

And here we reach the deepest thing reciprocity has to teach, which is its relation to exchange itself. The market transaction is reciprocity made immediate and explicit: I give you the goods, you give me the money, the kindness and its return collapsed into a single balanced act and the debt discharged on the spot. What the gift economy did slowly and diffusely, through obligations that hung in the air and were repaid over years, the market does instantly and precisely, through a price. Exchange is the ancient norm of reciprocity, formalized, accelerated, and stripped of its personal residue — which is why the market could grow out of the gift, and why, beneath the impersonal machinery of price and contract, the old rule still beats: that what is given must be returned, and that the one who takes without returning has broken something the whole order depends upon.

the edge Reciprocity sits at the hinge between self-interest and morality, and that is what makes it the most important norm of all and the hardest to classify. Seen from one side, it is barely a norm at all but a strategy, the cold logic of tit-for-tat that Axelrod found triumphant — be nice, then repay in kind, cooperating with cooperators and retaliating against defectors, and you will prosper and your kind will spread. On this reading reciprocity needs no morality, only the shadow of the future and a memory for faces; it is what a sufficiently far-sighted egoist would do. Seen from the other side, reciprocity is the very root of the moral sense, the principle behind the golden rule and the gift that binds, the disposition to return good for good that anthropologists find in every society. And the truth seems to be that it is both at once, and that the two cannot be cleanly separated — that the norm of reciprocity grew up where evolved self-interest and felt obligation reinforce each other, where doing right and doing well so often coincide that we can no longer tell which motive moves us. This is uncomfortable for those who would keep the economic and the moral in separate rooms, but it is the deepest thing the study of norms has to teach: that the rule which makes markets possible and the rule which makes us good may be, at bottom, the same rule, and that the line between the calculating and the decent is far blurrier than either the cynic or the moralist would like.

Plate the Fourth · The Behaviors · № XXVI

The Conscience

internalization; guilt and shame; the rule obeyed unobserved

The plate asks why do we keep a rule when no one is watching and no one will ever know?

Reputation and the watching community explain why we keep a norm when we are seen. They do not explain the harder and more telling case: why we keep it when we are not. The traveler returns the overpaid change to a shop he will never enter again; the finder mails back the wallet dropped on an empty road; the diner leaves the tip in a town to which he will not return, for a server who will not remember him. In none of these does reputation hang in the balance, for no one will ever know; the shadow of the future falls nowhere. And yet people do these things, reliably, and feel a wrongness when they fail to. The enforcement that reaches into the unobserved cannot be external. It must be within.

It is within, and we call it conscience. Norms, when a society does its work of raising the young, are not merely obeyed but internalized — taken inside, made part of the self, so that to violate them is to suffer not only the disapproval of others but a punishment one administers to oneself. Jon Elster located this enforcement in the moral emotions, and the two that matter most are shame and guilt. Shame is the pain of others' disapproval, real or imagined — the burning face, the wish to disappear, the eyes of the community felt even when the community is absent. Guilt is the pain of one's own judgment — the inward verdict that one has done wrong, which needs no audience and survives in perfect solitude. Both make the violation of a norm costly to the self regardless of detection, and so both reach where reputation cannot: into the dark, where most of conduct actually lives.

How conscience comes to be is no mystery, though it is easy to mistake. The norms a child meets are at first wholly external — the parent's frown, the teacher's reproof, the playmate's scorn — and through the long apprenticeship of growing up these external sanctions are taken in, until the parent's disapproving voice becomes the inner voice that disapproves when the parent is gone. The social becomes psychic; the rule enforced from without becomes the rule enforced from within. And there is reason to think the very capacity for this internalization was favored by evolution, for Robert Frank argued that the moral emotions function as commitment devices: the person who genuinely feels guilt at cheating is, for that reason, a person who can be trusted not to cheat, and so a person others will deal with, cooperate with, and prefer — so that a conscience, far from a handicap in the struggle of life, is an asset that draws the cooperation on which thriving depends. The honest man's honesty advertises itself, and is repaid.

The deepest enforcer is the one we cannot escape: the voice within that calls a thing wrong before any other voice is raised. It reaches where no watcher can — into the unobserved, where most of conduct lives.

The economic significance of this is hard to overstate, though it appears in no ledger. A market runs, to a degree we scarcely notice, on internalized honesty — on the countless dealings in which a party could cheat undetected and simply does not, because cheating is not the kind of thing he does. Strip this away, and imagine a world in which everyone cheated whenever cheating would pay and go unseen, and the result is the lemons market everywhere at once: every transaction policed, every quality verified, every promise distrusted, the costs of suspicion eating the gains of exchange until little exchange is left. The conscience is, in cold economic terms, the cheapest enforcement mechanism ever devised, for it requires no watchers, no courts, no surveillance; it works in the dark, for free, in the only place that the expensive machinery of external enforcement can never reach. A society blessed with widely internalized norms of honesty is rich before it produces anything, for it can transact at a fraction of the cost of a society that must watch its every member.

the edge That conscience is largely internalized from society, rather than welling up from an innate moral core, is a claim that troubles our self-image and repays the trouble. We experience the pang of guilt as the voice of our own deepest self, the shame at a mean act as something felt from within, and so we imagine our morality as ours, original, a private possession. But the content of conscience is, to a striking degree, the content of our community's norms, taken in during the long apprenticeship of childhood and worn so smooth by use that we forget it was ever given to us. The person raised among one set of norms feels guilt at violating them; the person raised among another feels guilt at violating those; and each experiences his particular conscience as the universal voice of right. This does not make conscience an illusion or morality a fraud — the internalized norm is real, does real work, and a person without it is a danger to everyone near him. But it does mean the moral sense is not the bedrock beneath the norms but the norms themselves, lodged within; that what we take for the most personal thing about us is in large part the most social; and that a society shapes its members' very consciences in shaping its norms — a power more intimate, and more consequential, than any the law commands. The voice within is real. It is also, mostly, the voice of those who raised us, speaking on after they have gone.

Plate the Fifth · The Behaviors · № XXVI

The Sanction

external enforcement; approval, reputation, and ostracism

The plate asks when the law does not punish a violation, who does — and how?

Conscience reaches the unobserved, but much of conduct is observed, and there a second layer of enforcement operates — external, social, and wielded by the community without any help from the state. Its instruments are humble and familiar. The mildest is approval and its withdrawal: the warm word and the cold one, the nod and the snub, the esteem we extend to those who keep the norms and withhold from those who break them. Because we are a species that craves the regard of others and shrinks from their contempt, this costs the community almost nothing to administer and yet bites deep. Above it stands reputation: the violator's name is marked, and others grow wary of trusting, dealing with, or standing beside him — the loss of all the future cooperation that a good name would have drawn, which the volumes on the contract and on reciprocity showed to be a genuine and heavy cost.

Between the snub and the spoiled name runs the mechanism that makes reputation possible at all, and it is one the respectable affect to despise: gossip. Gossip is the community's information system, the means by which a violation in one corner becomes known in all the others, so that reputations can be assigned and the watching eyes can see even what they did not witness. Anthropologists who have studied it closely find it anything but idle — it is the registry of the unwritten law, the distributed record of who has dealt well and who has cheated, without which no reputational sanction could function, for one cannot withhold trust from a cheat one has never heard of. What the court record and the credit bureau are to formal enforcement, gossip is to informal: the memory of the community, kept in common and consulted before every dealing.

At the summit of the social sanctions stands the heaviest of all: exclusion. To be shunned, boycotted, cast out — denied the cooperation, the company, and the standing of the group — is for a social animal a kind of death, and the threat of it has disciplined human conduct more thoroughly than any prison. The merchant guilds that Avner Greif studied enforced honest dealing across continents by the threat of collective boycott; the close community enforces its norms by the threat of the shut door; and the lesser exclusions of daily life, the un-invitation and the quiet dropping, enforce the smaller norms. These sanctions are graded, from the flicker of disapproval to total banishment, and they are decentralized — administered by no authority but by the diffuse, spontaneous reactions of everyone at once, which is what makes them at once so cheap and, under the right conditions, so overwhelming.

The community needs no court to punish. A frown, a cold word, a name passed quietly along, and at the last the shut door — these are the sanctions of the unwritten law, cheap to wield and, among people who know each other, devastating to suffer.

The phrase "under the right conditions" carries the weight, for the social sanctions have a precise domain in which they are mighty and outside which they fail. They work where conduct is observable, where dealings are repeated, and where the community is dense enough that gossip reaches everywhere and no one can escape his name. They are the enforcement of the village, the guild, the trade, the close community — and within such a world they govern nearly everything, leaving the law little to do. But they depend utterly on being seen and remembered, and so they reinforce the three-layered structure of enforcement this volume has been assembling: the conscience within, reaching the unobserved; the community without, reaching the observed and remembered; and, beyond both, the formal law of The Rule, reaching where neither conscience nor community can — among the strangers of the anonymous society, where the watching eyes are blind and the spoiled name does not follow. A society leans on all three, in proportions that shift with its scale, and the next plates will show what happens when the work of enforcement grows too great even for the costly punishers who give the social sanctions their final teeth.

the edge The dependence of social sanction on observability draws the sharp historical line between the world of the village and the world of the city, and explains why the law had to grow as it did. Among people who know one another, who deal repeatedly and cannot escape the talk that travels between them, the informal sanctions are formidable: every act is seen or soon heard of, every reputation known, and the violator cannot outrun his name or find a fresh community that has not heard. In such a world norms govern almost everything and law need govern little, for the gossip and the cold shoulder and the threat of the shut door do the work. But scale the society up, mix it, make it mobile and anonymous — pour its members into cities where one deals daily with strangers one will never see again, where no gossip reaches and no reputation follows — and the informal sanctions lose their grip. The cheat can simply move on; the watching eyes are blind; the community that would have shunned him does not know him. It is precisely here, in the society of strangers, that the impersonal enforcement of law becomes indispensable, taking over the work that reputation can no longer do, as the contract foresaw. The deep pattern is that informal and formal enforcement are substitutes that scale in opposite directions: norms govern the small and the known, law the large and the anonymous, and the long movement of economic history — from village to city, from the guild to the global market — is in large part the story of the second slowly relieving the first of a burden it could no longer bear.

Plate the Sixth · The Behaviors · № XXVI

The Punisher

the cost of enforcing; altruistic punishment and the ultimatum game

The plate asks if punishing a cheat costs the punisher and benefits everyone, why does anyone do it?

The social sanctions of the last plate all depend on someone willing to administer them — to shun the cheat, to spread the word, to refuse the unfair deal, to spend the effort that punishment requires. And here a puzzle opens that threatens the whole structure. Punishing a violator is costly to the punisher: it takes effort, invites retaliation, and yields him no private gain, since the benefit of a well-enforced norm — a more honest community — flows to everyone alike. The rational self-interested party should therefore decline to punish and hope that others will, free-riding on their costly enforcement exactly as the free-rider rode on others' contributions in the first place. But if everyone reasons so, no one punishes, the cheats go unanswered, and the norm collapses. This is the second-order free-rider problem, and on paper it should unravel cooperation as surely as the first.

On paper. In the laboratory, something else happens, and its discovery is among the most consequential in modern economics. Ernst Fehr and Simon Gächter set people to play a public-goods game over many rounds, each deciding how much to contribute to a common pool that benefited all. The figure shows what they found. Without any means of punishment, contributions begin at a moderate level and then decay, round by round, toward nothing, as the cooperators tire of being exploited by the free-riders and stop contributing themselves — cooperation unraveling just as the model predicts. But when the players are given the option to punish, at a cost to themselves, those who contributed little, everything changes: contributions begin at the same moderate level and then climb, round by round, toward near-complete cooperation.

The starkest demonstration of the same disposition is the ultimatum game, and it is worth stating in full because it refutes so cleanly the picture of the purely self-interested agent. One player is given a sum and proposes how to split it with a second; the second either accepts, and both keep their shares, or rejects, and both get nothing. Self-interest is unambiguous here: the responder should accept any positive offer, since any amount beats nothing, and the proposer, foreseeing this, should offer the smallest possible crumb. What happens is the opposite. Responders routinely reject offers they find insulting — a fifth of the pot, a tenth — choosing nothing over a pittance, paying with their own loss to punish a proposer who tried to take too much. And proposers, knowing this, mostly offer something close to half. People enforce a norm of fair division at a cost to themselves, and the enforcement is so reliable that it reshapes the offers made in its shadow.

Offered a fifth of the pot or nothing, people choose nothing — paying with their own loss to punish a stingy split. We are a species that will spend to see unfairness answered, and on that willingness cooperation quietly rests.

Why people punish at a cost is a question with answers at several depths. The proximate answer is emotion: anger and indignation at the cheat and the chiseler, which make punishing feel satisfying enough to be worth its price — the moral emotions, once again, doing the work that calculation will not. The ultimate answer, more contested, is evolutionary: Samuel Bowles and Herbert Gintis argued that a disposition to costly punishment — strong reciprocity, they called it — could have spread by cultural group selection, since groups well supplied with such punishers sustained more cooperation and prevailed over groups without them, even though within any group the punishers bore a cost the free-riders escaped. However it arose, its presence is not in doubt, for it appears across cultures, survives when the stakes are raised, and resists every attempt to explain it away. And its significance for the table is profound: it means that the cooperation on which every economy rests cannot be derived from self-interest alone, however far-sighted, but depends at its foundation on a willingness to enforce fairness that is not itself self-interested — the punisher in us, on whom the whole order of norms, and the cooperation it sustains, finally leans.

the edge The willingness to punish at a cost is the keystone of the whole edifice of norms, and it is also the fact about human beings that the standard economic model most flatly denies, which is why the laboratory findings landed with such force. The model's agent is a rational maximizer of his own payoff; offered any positive sum in the ultimatum game he takes it, for something beats nothing; asked to punish a cheat at his own expense he declines, for why pay to harm another when the benefit flows to everyone but himself. The agent of the experiments does neither. He rejects the insulting offer though refusal costs him, because he would rather have nothing than let unfairness stand; he pays to punish the free-rider though he gains nothing by it, out of an indignation the model has no place for. These are not the errors of confused subjects, for they appear across cultures, persist when the stakes are raised, and survive every attempt to explain them away; they are a stable feature of the species. And their implication runs deep, for they show that the cooperation on which every economy rests cannot be wholly derived from self-interest, however far-sighted — that it requires, at its foundation, a disposition to enforce fairness that is not itself self-interested, a willingness to spend oneself to see the cheat answered. Homo economicus, the lone calculator of his own advantage, would live in a world of unraveling cooperation and unpunished defection; that we do not is owing to the punisher in us, who is angrier, and more generous, than the model can understand.

Plate the Seventh · The Behaviors · № XXVI

The Origin

where norms come from; spontaneous emergence and evolution

The plate asks if no one designs a norm, how does it come to be — and why do some catch while others never take?

We have seen what norms are, how they bind, and how they are enforced; the question that remains is where they come from, and the answer is the strangest in the volume. A few norms are made — proclaimed by a leader, instilled by a campaign, left behind as the residue of a law. But the great body of them is made by no one. They emerge, the way a path is worn across a field by feet that never agreed to make it: each walker takes the way the others have taken because it is easier and because the others are taking it, and every passage wears the path a little deeper, until what began as the chance track of a few has hardened into the only way across. No surveyor laid it; no authority decreed it; and yet it governs every crossing. So it is with the conventions of property and dealing that David Hume watched arise without contract or command, with the spontaneous order Friedrich Hayek placed at the center of his thought, and with the self-enforcing regularities of cooperation and exchange that Robert Sugden showed could grow up out of nothing but repeated interaction. Norms are the result of human action but not of human design.

That emergence is not guaranteed, however, and the reason is the tipping dynamic the figure draws. Because a norm is self-reinforcing — the more who keep it, the stronger the pressure on each to keep it too, partly because one wishes to do as others do and partly because the sanctions fall on the deviant — its fate hangs on a threshold. The line below maps the fraction of a community that follows the norm, from none to all, and marks the three points that matter. At either end sits a stable resting place: a world where no one follows, and a world where all do, each self-perpetuating. Between them lies an unstable point, the tipping point, and it divides the line into two countries. Begin to the left of it, with too few adherents, and the norm decays toward extinction, each defection making the next easier until it fades back into the grass. Begin to the right of it, with enough, and the norm spreads toward universality, each new adherent drawing in the next until it governs all.

The tipping line explains what the simple image of the worn path cannot: why some norms catch and spread to every foot while others, walked by too few, fade away. A norm must reach its critical mass to survive, and below it the most sensible rule will die unobserved, for there are too few keeping it to make keeping it worthwhile. This is the mathematics of the fad and the riot and the bank run as much as of the norm — Thomas Schelling's tipping and Mark Granovetter's thresholds, the cascade that waits on a critical mass — and it governs the birth of the unwritten rules as surely as it governs their death.

Over the long run, the norms that emerge are sifted by something like selection. Those that serve their communities — that sustain cooperation, coordinate behavior, solve the recurring problems of living together — tend to spread and endure; those that do not tend to die out, taking their groups down with them or being abandoned for something better. The students of cultural evolution, Robert Boyd and Peter Richerson foremost among them, have shown how norms are transmitted and selected across generations like a second inheritance, faster and more flexible than the genetic one. And Samuel Bowles and Herbert Gintis argued that the cooperative norms in particular may have spread by selection among groups, the well-normed prevailing over the ill-normed across the long contest of human history. On this reading the norms we keep are evolved solutions to the games our ancestors played without end — the coordination games that left us conventions, the cooperation games that left us reciprocity and fairness — the sediment of what worked, carried forward in the customs of the living.

A norm is a path worn by use: no one laid it, yet everyone walks it, and to walk it is to wear it deeper. Some paths catch and spread to every foot; others, walked by too few, fade back into the grass.

Two consequences follow for the table, and they pull against each other in a way the volume will not resolve. The first is humbling: because norms are grown and selected rather than designed, they may carry a wisdom no designer possesses, encoding in their wordless way the lessons of more trials than any planner could run — which is Hayek's case for treating the inherited rules with a certain reverence, and for doubting the reformer who would sweep them away in the name of a reason that sees less than it thinks. The second is unsettling, and it is the table's own: because norms are grown and not given, they are contingent, the frozen accidents of which paths happened to be walked and which groups happened to prevail. A different norm could have caught; the tipping could have gone the other way; the done thing that feels eternal is the survivor of a contest that might have ended otherwise. The deepest rules of economic life, no less than the prices the table began with, were not handed down by nature. They were worn into us by use, and what use wore in, use can wear away.

the edge The tipping dynamic that governs how norms emerge is also the key to how they change, and it holds both a warning and a hope. The warning is that norms, once established above their threshold, are powerfully self-reinforcing and resist piecemeal reform: the lone reformer who abandons a bad norm pays the price of deviance while the norm rolls on unmoved, so that everyone may privately wish the norm gone and yet no one dares be first to leave it — the trap the eleventh plate will examine. But the hope lies in the same mathematics. Because the norm is sustained by mutual expectation, it is vulnerable at the tipping point; and a change that moves enough people at once — a coordinated refusal, a shift in what is openly said, a vivid example that makes the private wish public — can carry the fraction past the threshold, after which the norm flips not slowly but in a cascade, each new defector lowering the cost to the next until the old rule collapses and a new one rushes in to take its place. This is why norm change so often looks impossible right up until it looks inevitable, why the practice that seemed eternal can vanish in a decade, and why those who would change a bad norm do better to coordinate a crossing of the threshold than to preach to individuals one by one. The same self-reinforcement that locks a norm in place is what makes its eventual overthrow, once begun, so swift.

Plate the Eighth · The Behaviors · № XXVI

The Lubricant

trust as social capital; the economic value of the unwritten

The plate asks what is trust worth to an economy — and where does a society get it?

We have anatomized the norms; now we must price them, or at least show why they are beyond price. The volume on the contract found trust filling the gaps that no clause could close, in the particular relationship between two parties who deal again and again. Raise that finding from the pair to the society, and it becomes one of the largest facts in all of economics. Kenneth Arrow stated it plainly: trust, he said, is an important lubricant of a social system, extremely efficient, saving a great deal of the trouble it would take to verify everything one is told. Where people can rely on one another's word, exchange flows; where they cannot, it seizes. And what people rely on, when they trust, is precisely the dense web of norms this volume has described — the expectation that others will deal honestly, keep their promises, and return a kindness rather than exploit it.

The figure shows the mechanism by which trust does its economic work. As the trust in a society rises along the horizontal, two things move together. The cost of transacting falls, for where one can take a word on faith there is less need to verify, monitor, guard, and litigate — the whole apparatus of suspicion that the volumes on asymmetry and the contract showed to be so expensive grows lighter as trust grows. And the volume of beneficial exchange rises, for as the cost of each dealing falls, dealings that were not worth their friction become worth doing, and cooperation widens to fill the space that distrust had kept empty. The two curves cross the page in opposite directions, and the gap that opens between them — low cost, high exchange — is the dividend that trust pays.

This asset has a name. Following James Coleman and made famous by Robert Putnam, economists and sociologists call it social capital — the networks, norms of reciprocity, and trust that let a community cooperate, and that raise its productivity as surely as a stock of machines or a fund of skills. Social capital behaves like capital in that it is productive and it accumulates; it is unlike the capital of the earlier volumes in that no one owns it, for it lives in the relations between people rather than in any of them, and it is built not by saving and investing but by the slow practice of dealing well and keeping faith. A society well supplied with it can do cheaply what a society lacking it cannot do at all: trust a stranger, enforce a bargain by reputation, build an institution larger than a family.

That last difference is the one Francis Fukuyama placed at the center of his study of trust, and it carries the argument to the scale of nations. Societies differ enormously in the radius of their trust — in how far the expectation of honest dealing reaches beyond the circle of kin. Where trust extends widely, to strangers and not merely to family, a society can build the large impersonal organizations that modern production requires, can staff them with the unrelated, and can transact across the distances that wealth demands. Where trust is confined to the family or the clan, the firm struggles to grow beyond the household, the costly scaffolding of formal enforcement must do what trust will not, and the gains of wide cooperation go unreaped. The wealth of nations, on this view, rests in no small part on a thing that no nation can purchase and few can quickly build: the diffuse, generalized trust that is the dividend of good norms widely kept.

Trust is the lubricant of every exchange, and it is never on the balance sheet. A society's stock of it — built slowly, betrayed quickly, bought never — may be worth more than all its priced capital combined.

So the unwritten rules, which the early plates showed binding and constraining us, show here their other and kindlier face. They are not only the chains of conformity; they are the foundation of cooperation, the social capital that makes wide and cheap exchange possible, the lubricant without which the gleaming machinery of prices and contracts would grind to a halt. Beneath all the priced wealth the table has anatomized lies this unpriced wealth, the stock of trust and honest dealing that never appears on a balance sheet and may be worth more than everything that does. And it returns us, by a long road, to the thing this whole work has sought — for the numéraire of honesty is not merely a standard against which to read the verdicts hidden in prices; it is itself the most valuable asset a society can hold, the keystone norm of honest dealing on which the dividend of trust is paid.

the edge That trust is a form of capital — productive, valuable, accumulable — is an illuminating idea that turns treacherous if pressed too far, and the danger is worth marking. It illuminates because it captures something real: a society rich in trust transacts cheaply and cooperates widely, and the advantage compounds over time exactly as a stock of capital does, so that to speak of social capital is to name a genuine asset the conventional accounts of wealth leave out. But trust differs from capital in ways the metaphor can hide, and each difference matters. It cannot be bought, for to purchase trust is already to lack it; it cannot be built by a single owner, for it lives between people and not within them; it is not depleted by use but, unlike a machine, deepened by it, growing with the dealing it enables; and it can be destroyed far faster than it was made, a reputation for honest dealing that took a generation to build undone by a single betrayal that travels at the speed of gossip. The treachery comes when the metaphor tempts a society to think it can invest in trust as it invests in roads, or that whatever raises measured trust must therefore be done — for the things that build trust are mostly the slow, unpurchasable practices of keeping faith, and the surest ways to destroy it, as the next plate will show, include some of the very market incentives a society reaching for more cooperation might be tempted to deploy. Trust is capital enough to be worth husbanding, and unlike capital enough that husbanding it means something other than spending on it.

Plate the Ninth · The Behaviors · № XXVI

The Community

governing the commons by norm; the third way between market and state

The plate asks between the market that prices everything and the state that commands it, is there a third way to govern what we share?

The volume on the commons posed a tragedy: a resource held by all and owned by none — the pasture, the fishery, the forest, the air — is used by each to exhaustion, since each takes the whole of his private gain while the cost of depletion falls on all. And it offered the two remedies that economic thought has long allowed. One may privatize the commons, carving it into owned parcels so that prices and the owner's self-interest do the conserving, which is the market's answer. Or one may regulate it, setting a distant authority over it to ration and prohibit, which is the state's. The whole debate over the things we share has run, for a century, between these two poles: make it private, or make it public; give it to the market, or give it to the state.

Elinor Ostrom won a Nobel Prize for showing that this exhausts neither the possibilities nor the facts. Going out into the world to look, she found commons that had been governed sustainably for centuries by neither market nor state, but by the communities that depended on them — the high pastures of the Swiss Alps, the irrigation networks of Spain, the village forests of Japan, the inshore fisheries of Maine — each managed not by private owners and not by government officials but by the users themselves, under rules of their own making, monitored and enforced among themselves. The tragedy of the commons, she demonstrated, is not a law of nature but a possibility that communities have repeatedly escaped, and the means of their escape was neither the price nor the command but the norm.

She did more than find the cases; she distilled what made them work, and the distillation reads as a catalogue of everything this volume has described. The enduring commons had boundaries clear enough that everyone knew who might use them and who might not; rules fitted to local conditions rather than imposed from afar; the participation of the users in making the rules they would have to keep, so that the rules were theirs and were felt as legitimate; monitoring, often by the users watching one another; and — the punisher's signature — graduated sanctions, mild for a first transgression and mounting for the stubborn, applied by the community to its own. These are the norms and the social sanctions of the earlier plates, organized into a working government of the commons. Self-governance by norm is not a sentimental hope; it is a documented and reproducible institution, with conditions that can be stated and met.

What Ostrom's discovery restores to economics is a third pillar of governance that the discipline had let fall. Beside the market, which governs by exchange and price, and the state, which governs by law and command, stands the community, which governs by norm and mutual obligation — the realm of cooperation that is neither bought nor commanded, which Samuel Bowles and Herbert Gintis insisted belongs in the picture and which Raghuram Rajan named the third pillar. An economy is held up by all three, and leans too far when it forgets any one: the society that knows only the market and the state will dissolve its working communities in the name of efficiency or order, and discover too late that it has destroyed the cheapest and most legitimate governor it had.

The endless quarrel between the market and the state forgets a third party older than both: the community, governing what it shares by rules it made itself, monitored and enforced by the people who must live with them.

Yet the third pillar is a pillar and not the whole building, and Ostrom was the first to mark its limits. Community governance works under the same conditions that make any norm strong — a bounded membership, a stable population, the capacity to watch and to sanction, the long shadow of repeated dealing — and it falters where those conditions fail: for the large, the diffuse, and above all the global commons. The atmosphere has no bounded community to govern it, no users who watch one another, no shared norms reaching across the whole of humanity; and so the greatest commons of all remains the hardest case, beyond the reach of the village solution and waiting still on the cooperation of strangers who do not know one another's names. The community can govern what it can see and sanction. Past that horizon, the old problems return, and the work of governing what we share passes back to the market, the state, and whatever fragile cooperation the species can contrive among those it cannot watch.

the edge The recovery of the community as a third mode of governance corrects a deep bias in economic thought and then, if taken too far, threatens to install a fresh romance in place of the old one. The bias is real and consequential: trained to see only the market and the state, economists long treated every commons as a tragedy awaiting one of two rescues, privatization or regulation, and overlooked the thousands of communities that had quietly governed their pastures and forests and fisheries for centuries by neither — a blindness Ostrom's patient fieldwork at last corrected, and that cost the world dearly wherever it led reformers to dissolve a working commons in the name of a theory that said it could not work. But the correction breeds its own temptation, the romance of the community as a gentle and sufficient alternative to the cold market and the heavy state, and this the same fieldwork rebukes. Community governance succeeds only under conditions — a bounded membership, a stable population, the capacity to monitor and to sanction, the long shadow of repeated dealing — that are exactly the conditions for norms in general, and that fail for the large, the diffuse, and the global. And the community that governs its commons well often does so by excluding the outsider and binding the insider, so that the warmth of mutual obligation and the coldness of the closed door are two faces of one thing. The third pillar is real, and was wrongly forgotten. It is also bounded, and would be wrongly romanced. An economy needs all three — market, state, and community — and the art of governing it well is the art of knowing which the matter at hand requires.

Plate the Tenth · The Behaviors · № XXVI

The Crowding

markets and norms; when the price drives out the principle

The plate asks if you want more of a good thing, will paying for it always get you more?

The last plate but one showed markets resting upon norms — trust the lubricant, honesty the foundation. This plate shows the darker half of the relation, in which the market does not rest upon the norm but dissolves it. The intuition to be overturned is the most natural in economics: that incentives work in one direction, so that to want more of a behavior is to pay for it, and the more one pays the more one gets. Against this stands one of the most instructive findings in the modern literature, and it begins with a daycare.

Uri Gneezy and Aldo Rustichini studied a set of Israeli daycare centers troubled by parents who came late to collect their children, leaving the staff to wait. The centers introduced a fine for lateness, expecting it to deter. Lateness rose. It rose because the fine had changed the meaning of being late: what had been a small moral failing — an imposition on the staff, a thing one felt ashamed to do — became a service with a posted price, which a parent was now entitled to purchase. The norm that had restrained lateness was a sense of obligation; the fine replaced it with a fee, and once lateness could be paid for, there was no longer anything wrong with it. Worse still, when the centers later removed the fine, lateness did not return to its old level: the norm, once dissolved, did not reassemble. The price had not added a deterrent to the norm; it had driven the norm out and taken its place, and proved a weaker master than the shame it had banished.

The figure generalizes the lesson, and it is the shape of the curve that matters. With no payment at all, the behavior stands high, sustained by the norm or the intrinsic motive that moves people to do a thing for its own sake. Introduce a small payment, and the behavior does not rise but falls, for the payment dissolves the norm — reframing the act as a transaction — while being far too small to call forth as a transaction what the norm had called forth as a duty. Only when the payment grows large does the behavior climb again, the price at last buying what the principle used to give. The relation between incentive and effort is not the rising line of intuition but a valley: a small reward is worse than none, and the dashed line marks how far below the unpaid level the lightly paid behavior sinks. Gneezy and Rustichini drew the moral in four words — pay enough, or don't pay at all — but the deeper moral is that the middle case, the small price laid upon a norm, can be the worst of every world.

Why a price should dissolve a norm rather than reinforce it is now fairly understood, and the mechanisms all turn on meaning. A payment reframes an act, moving it from the domain of duty, gift, or honor into the domain of the market, where the norms of duty, gift, and honor no longer apply and only the price speaks. It can undercut the intrinsic satisfaction of doing a thing freely, as the students of motivation have shown, so that what was done for its own sake is now done only for the money or not at all. And it can signal distrust — the fine that says we expect you to shirk, the monitor that says we do not take your word — corroding the very goodwill on which cooperation depended. In each case the market does not layer an incentive atop the norm; it changes what kind of thing the act is, and in changing it, destroys the norm that governed it.

Fine the latecomers, and lateness rises: the price meant to deter it has dissolved the shame that did. A market set upon a norm can devour the norm — and leave the price a poorer master than the principle it drove out.

Set beside the earlier plate, this one completes the account of how markets and norms entangle, and the account has three strands rather than one. Markets depend on norms, as the lubricant of trust made plain. Markets can erode norms, as the crowding-out makes plain here. And markets can, in their gentler moods, create norms — the punctuality, the fair dealing, the tolerance that commerce among strangers is sometimes said to teach. The market is not a separate sphere walled off from the moral order, as the model's clean machinery suggests, but is embedded in that order — the word is Mark Granovetter's, and the warning behind it Karl Polanyi's — drawing on its norms, sometimes nourishing them, and sometimes consuming them. Michael Sandel pressed the consequence furthest: that there are goods whose nature is altered by being priced and relations the market degrades by entering, so that the question of what may be bought and sold is not only a question of efficiency but of what each thing is and how it ought to be held.

the edge The crowding-out of norms by prices sets a boundary on the reach of the market that is not a matter of efficiency but of meaning, and locating it is among the most delicate tasks an economy faces. The market's genius is its reach: the price can coordinate strangers, allocate the scarce, and call forth effort across a scale no other institution matches, and the impulse to extend that reach into every corner of life — to price the things now governed by duty, affection, or honor — has the force of a great success behind it. But this plate has shown that the extension is not always gain, for a price laid upon an act can change its meaning, converting a gift into a sale, a duty into a fee, a favor into a transaction, and dissolving in the act the very norm that made the behavior reliable. Where this happens the market does not merely allocate the good; it corrodes the principle that governed it, and may leave behind a thing that functions worse under the price than it did under the norm — the latecomers more numerous, the blood scarcer, the citizens more grudging. Michael Sandel and Samuel Bowles, from their different directions, converge on the warning: that there are goods whose nature is altered by being priced, relations the market degrades by entering, and norms that good incentives do not reinforce but replace. The boundary between what should be left to the price and what kept to the norm is therefore not a technical line drawn by the calculus of efficiency but a moral one, drawn by a society's judgment of what each thing is — which means that the scope of the price system, the very object this whole table has anatomized, is itself set by norms, and that the question of how far the market should reach is, at the last, a question the market cannot answer.

Plate the Eleventh · The Behaviors · № XXVI

The Trap

the dark side; bad norms, sticky norms, and the tyranny of conformity

The plate asks if everyone would be better off under a different rule, why does the bad one hold?

The volume has so far shown the kindly face of the norm — cooperation, trust, the social capital that lubricates exchange and the community that governs the commons — and it would be a sentimental and dangerous account that stopped there. For the machinery this volume has described is neutral as to what it enforces. The shared expectation, the watching community, the graduated sanction, the internalized conscience — these sustain a norm of honesty exactly as they sustain a norm of corruption, a norm of fair dealing exactly as a norm of discrimination, a norm of courage exactly as a code of silence that shields the wrongdoer. A norm is only a rule the community keeps and enforces upon itself; whether the rule is good or vile, the keeping and the enforcing work the same. And so the very strength that makes a good norm a blessing makes a bad norm a curse that is exceptionally hard to lift.

The figure shows why, and it is the dark twin of the tipping line drawn earlier. Along the horizontal runs the fraction of a community that keeps some good norm — honest dealing, say, in a place where corruption is otherwise the rule — and up the vertical runs the payoff each person receives. There are two stable resting places. At the right sits the good equilibrium, where nearly all keep the norm and all are prosperous, the high payoff of a society that has solved its problem of cooperation. At the left sits the trap: a low fraction keeping the norm, a low payoff for everyone, a world of mutual cheating in which the honest are fleeced and the corrupt are merely normal. Between them, the open circle marks the unstable tipping point that must be crossed to get from the one to the other. And the cruelty of the picture is that a society can be locked at the left-hand point — everyone worse off than they would be at the right — with no individual able to move it.

The mechanism of the lock is the same self-enforcement that made good norms durable, now working for ill. Each person keeps to the bad norm because everyone else does and the deviant is punished: the official who refuses the bribe is frozen out, the merchant who alone deals honestly is undercut and ruined, the one who breaks the code of silence is destroyed by those it protects. No one will be the first to leave, for the first to leave bears the whole cost of deviance while the norm rolls on unmoved — the collective-action problem of the contract and the commons, returned in its bleakest form. And to this is often added a subtler trap still, which Timur Kuran named preference falsification: under the pressure of the norm, people misrepresent what they truly think, professing in public a support they do not feel in private, so that each comes to believe — falsely — that all the others endorse the very rule that all of them privately curse. The norm then stands not on real conviction but on a shared illusion of conviction, a pluralistic ignorance in which everyone conforms to please a majority that does not exist.

A bad norm holds for the same reason a good one does: no one will be the first to leave it. Everyone may privately curse the rule and publicly keep it, each kept in line by the false belief that all the rest believe in it.

This dark side also revises the kindly accounts that preceded it. The social capital of the eighth plate, it turns out, can be dark capital: the dense networks and fierce norms that let a community cooperate are the same that let a mafia, a cartel, or a closed and bigoted guild cooperate, and a society can be rich in a trust that binds its members the more tightly to a collective harm. The conformity that coordinates can also oppress, and John Stuart Mill thought this social tyranny more penetrating than the political kind, since it reaches past the public acts that law can touch into the private life that only opinion governs, punishing not the crime but the difference, and silencing not the dangerous but the merely deviant — including the dissenter and the innovator on whom all improvement depends. Norms stabilize the good and the bad with equal force, and suppress, along with the harmful deviation, the beneficial one.

the edge The norm trap is where the study of the unwritten rules turns from description to critique, and where it earns its place as the last word of a table devoted to denaturalizing. For the whole danger of a norm is bound up with its greatest strength — that it presents itself not as a rule that might be otherwise but as the way things simply are, the done thing, felt as natural and eternal and therefore beyond question. A bad law announces itself as a law, and so invites repeal; a bad norm hides as a fact of the world, and so escapes it, enforced not only by the watching community but by the conviction of its own victims that no alternative is thinkable. This is why the trap can hold a society against the private wishes of nearly everyone in it, each conforming to a rule he despises because he believes, falsely, that the rest endorse it — the preference falsification that Kuran showed can keep an unloved order standing long after its support has rotted away. And it is why the act of seeing a norm as made rather than given — the act this entire table has been performing, price by price and rule by rule — is not a merely intellectual exercise but the first move of emancipation. To recognize that the done thing was done by us, and could be done otherwise, is to convert a fact of nature back into a choice; and though the choice cannot be exercised alone, since the lone deviant is only punished, it can be exercised together, by the coordinated crossing of the threshold that turns a trap into a memory. The unwritten rule that feels eternal is the survivor of a contest we have forgotten was ever held. Remembering that it was held is how we reopen it.

Plate the Twelfth · The Behaviors · № XXVI

The Understood

the unwritten foundation; the rules merely understood beneath all the rest

The plate asks when everything written has been read, what remains — and what holds it up?

We asked, at the opening of this volume, which of the market's rules are written and which are merely understood, and the long answer the plates have given is that the understood are the deeper and the larger. The conventions that let strangers coordinate, the reciprocity that turns prudence into cooperation, the conscience that keeps a rule unobserved, the sanctions and the costly punishers that enforce what no court will hear, the trust that is a society's hidden capital, the community that governs by norm what neither market nor state governs well — these are the informal institutions, and they do the greater part of the work of holding an economy together. The formal rules of The Rule are the visible writing; the norms are the understood foundation on which the writing rests. And the dark eleventh plate forbade us to sentimentalize them, for the same machinery that sustains honesty sustains corruption, and a society can be trapped by its unwritten rules as cruelly as it is served by them.

This is the twenty-sixth element, and the last, and it is fitting that the table should close here, at the rules that no one wrote. For the whole of this work has been a single sustained act of denaturalizing — of taking the things that present themselves as facts of nature, the wage and the rent and the rate, the price that seems as given as the weather, and showing each to be a made thing: a verdict on a hidden contest, an institution with a history, a choice that could have been otherwise. And beneath every made thing the table has examined lies this deepest made thing of all. The norm is the object that feels most like nature — the done thing, the way things simply are, the rule so woven into us that we mistake it for the grain of the world — and it is, of everything the work has touched, the most thoroughly our own doing.

Once this is seen, the whole edifice the table anatomized is revealed to be standing on it. The money in every pocket is accepted because each of us expects the next to accept it — a convention, as the volume on money found, faith resting on faith. The contract in every drawer is kept, in the main, not for fear of the court but because keeping one's word is the done thing, as the volume on the contract confessed at its end. The property is respected, the queue is honored, the debt is repaid, the weight is given honest, the market clears at dawn — all because a dense and mostly unspoken web of shared expectation tells each of us how the others will behave and what will befall us if we behave otherwise. The invisible hand, whose workings this table set out to make visible, turns out itself to rest on something more invisible still: an invisible substructure of norms, without which the hand would have nothing to grasp and the prices nothing to mean.

And so we come, by the longest road, to the thing this entire work has been seeking. From the first page it has sought a numéraire of honesty — a true standard against which the verdicts hidden inside prices might at last be read, a unit of honest reckoning to set against the dishonest reckonings that prices so often are. We have looked for it through twenty-six elements, and we find it here, at the foundation, and find that it was never a thing to be discovered outside the system of prices but a norm to be kept within it. The numéraire of honesty is the keystone norm itself: the unwritten rule that we will not cheat the count — in our dealings, in our promises, in our reckonings, and in our thinking. It cannot be minted or measured or bought; it can only be kept, and taught, and enforced among ourselves, in the slow unpurchasable way of all norms. The standard the work demanded turns out to be a standard the work can only commend, for it is not a fact to be established but a commitment to be made, the keystone norm of honest dealing on which the trustworthiness of every other reckoning depends.

This volume is also the first of the last family, the Behaviors, and in closing the table it opens that family with the observation on which the family is built: that the agent of economic life is not the solitary calculator of his own advantage whom the model has so long assumed. The punisher who pays from his own pocket to see unfairness answered, the conscience that keeps the rule in the dark where no one will ever know, the reciprocator who returns a kindness without reckoning the return, the ordinary person who does the done thing because it is done — none of these is the creature of pure self-interest the equations describe. They are creatures shot through with norms, with fairness and conscience and the need to belong woven into the very preferences the model takes as given. The plates that were to follow — the Choice and the ways it departs from the rational ideal, the Bias that bends it, the Game that strangers play against one another — would develop this agent in his full and puzzling reality. The Norm has done only the foundational thing: it has shown, beneath the calculation, the creature of rules.

For this last element reaches back through the whole table, and gathers it. It is the informal twin of The Rule, the unwritten institutions standing beside the written. It is the hand-back for the handshake of The Contract, the trust and reputation that fill the gaps no clause can close. It is the deep structure beneath the faith of The Money, the convention that lends the worthless token its worth. It is the third way of The Commons, the community that governs by norm what the market would exhaust and the state would bungle. It is the foundation of the fairness that troubled The Wage and haunted the division of The Surplus, the unwritten sense of the just share that no productivity can fully explain. And it is the soil in which the strategies of The Game take root, the shared expectations that turn a tangle of self-interested moves into the patterned cooperation of a society. The norms are everywhere beneath the table, and in ending on them the work does not so much conclude as return to its own foundations.

So the table ends where it had to end, at the rules that were never written, and finds even these to be no exception to its one persistent finding. The norms, like the prices the work began with, are not given by nature. They are grown and evolved, enforced and internalized, and they are contingent through and through — the survivors of contests we have forgotten were ever held, the done things that could have been done otherwise and may yet be done otherwise still. To see them as made is not to wish them away, for a society without norms is a society without trust, cooperation, or honesty, which is to say no society at all; the unwritten rules are not a prison to be escaped but the ground on which any common life must stand. It is, rather, to hold them as ours — to keep the good ones knowingly rather than blindly, and to know that the bad ones, however eternal they feel, are traps that human beings built and that human beings could, acting together, unbuild. This has been the argument of the whole work, pursued through twenty-six elements and rendered here at its foundation: that the economy is not a piece of nature to be suffered but a human construction to be understood, judged, and where it fails us, remade. Its deepest rules are unwritten, and they hold up everything else. They are also our own work, and they remain open — as this argument, having reached its last element, remains open — to revision by the hands that made them. The argument remains open.
