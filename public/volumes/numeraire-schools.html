On the contest among economists

The Same Table, Different Eyes

This book has been written from a single vantage, and the gaps confessed it: one lens, with its own emphases and its own blind spots and its own quarrel. But there is a deeper thing the confession points to, which this companion makes plain. Economics has never been a single science with one settled reading of its objects. It is, and always has been, a contest of schools — rival traditions, each with a whole worldview, each reading the same elements through different eyes.

To one school the wage is what the last hour of labor adds at the margin; to another it is the surplus skimmed from labor’s product and called profit; to a third it is a price held up by fairness and by custom; to a fourth, a bargain freely struck whose worth no outsider can judge. The word on the plate is the same. The reading is a battlefield, and it has been fought over for the better part of three centuries.

What follow are seven of the great schools, and the same handful of elements read through each in turn. Choose a tradition and watch the table re-write itself — the wage, the surplus, the slump, the choice, the rule, each meaning something new in the new light. The book has argued, plate after plate, that the contest hides inside every price. Here is that contest run all the way up: into the very meaning of the elements, where the economists themselves have never agreed.

Choose a school. The same five elements are read below through its eyes.

Classical

Adam Smith, David Ricardo, and John Stuart Mill — from 1776 onward.

Value springs from labor and from land, and the market, left to itself, coordinates the whole through the pursuit of private gain.

The Wage

The wage tends toward the cost of reproducing the laborer — what it takes to keep and to replace a worker — while the surplus above that subsistence flows away into profit and rent.

The Surplus

The surplus is the produce that remains once wages and replacement are met, divided among the classes as profit and rent; how it divides is the master question of political economy.

The Cycle

Gluts are temporary and self-correcting; supply creates its own demand, and in the long run the system returns of itself to its natural balance.

The Choice

Each man pursues his own gain, and in so doing is led, as if by an invisible hand, to promote an end that was no part of his intention.

The Rule

The state should hold the ring — defense, justice, a few necessary public works — and otherwise leave the natural liberty of the market to do its coordinating work.

Neoclassical

Jevons, Menger, Walras, and Marshall — the marginal revolution of the 1870s and its heirs.

Value lies in the margin and in the mind of the buyer; price is where the schedules of supply and demand, each built from rational choice, come to rest.

The Wage

The wage equals the marginal product of labor — the value of what the last worker adds — settled, like any price, where the demand for labor meets its supply.

The Surplus

Surplus is the gain from trade that each side keeps: the consumer’s above what she pays, the producer’s above his cost; and a free market maximizes their sum.

The Cycle

Departures from full employment are frictions and shocks; left to adjust, prices and wages clear every market and the economy returns to its equilibrium.

The Choice

The agent is a rational optimizer, ranking options by utility and choosing the most preferred within his constraints; preferences are given, and revealed by what he does.

The Rule

The state acts only to correct a market failure — an externality, a public good, a monopoly — and otherwise leaves the equilibrium that voluntary exchange achieves.

Marxian

Karl Marx and his successors — turning the classical labor theory against capital, from 1867 onward.

The price hides a theft: the surplus that labor produces beyond its wage, taken by the one who owns the means, and called profit.

The Wage

The wage buys labor-power at its value, the cost of the worker’s subsistence; but labor produces more than it costs, and that excess — surplus value — is taken by the owner.

The Surplus

Surplus value is the unpaid labor at the heart of profit — not a gain from trade but the very measure of exploitation, the engine of the system and its injustice at once.

The Cycle

Crises are not accidents but contradictions: capital overproduces, the rate of profit tends to fall, and the system convulses by its own internal laws toward recurring slump.

The Choice

Choice is not free but structured by class; the worker who must sell his labor to eat does not choose as the owner chooses, and the market’s freedom is, for most, a compulsion.

The Rule

The state is no neutral referee but an instrument of the owning class, its laws and its order shaped to secure the conditions under which accumulation goes on.

Keynesian

John Maynard Keynes and his successors — after the slump that classical theory could not explain, from 1936 onward.

The whole is not the sum of its parts; demand can fail, slumps can persist, and the state must fill the gap the market leaves.

The Wage

Wages are sticky — they do not fall freely to clear the labor market — so unemployment can persist; and cutting them in a slump may deepen it, by cutting the demand that wages fund.

The Surplus

What matters is not the surplus of a single trade but aggregate demand — whether the spending of all is enough to employ all; saving that is not invested is demand simply withdrawn.

The Cycle

The slump is a failure of demand, driven by the animal spirits of investors and the fear that hoards money; it can settle into a lasting under-employment that only spending breaks.

The Choice

Agents act under deep uncertainty, not calculable risk, and fall back on convention and confidence; their collective mood, not cold optimization, is what moves the economy.

The Rule

In a slump the state must act — spending when others will not, easing the rate, sustaining demand — for the market, left alone, may sit at the bottom indefinitely.

Austrian

Carl Menger, Ludwig von Mises, and Friedrich Hayek — the Vienna line and its heirs.

The price is a message no planner could compose; value is subjective, order is unplanned, and the aggregate is a fiction.

The Wage

The wage is a price discovered in the market, reflecting the subjective value placed on what labor helps to bring about; no formula fixes it, and no authority knows it better than the market reveals.

The Surplus

Value is subjective and forward-looking; there is no objective surplus lying about to be extracted, only the gains each party expects from an exchange both enter freely and judge for themselves.

The Cycle

The boom is the error and the bust the cure: credit pushed below its natural rate misdirects investment, and the slump is the painful correction of those malinvestments.

The Choice

Man acts purposefully toward ends that are his own, subjective, and known only to him; the economist’s task is to trace the logic of that action, not to second-guess the ends.

The Rule

The market is a spontaneous order, coordinating a dispersed knowledge no single mind could hold; the planner who would improve on it suffers a fatal conceit, and the state should keep to the rule of law.

Behavioral

Herbert Simon, then Kahneman and Tversky, then Thaler — the psychological turn, from the 1950s onward.

The agent is not the rational ghost of the models but a real mind — bounded, biased, and doing its honest best.

The Wage

The wage is shaped by fairness as much as by marginal product: workers resist cuts they judge unjust, employers pay above the clearing rate to keep loyalty, and norms hold the wage in place.

The Surplus

Whether a person feels a gain at all depends on the reference point; the same outcome is a win or a loss by what it is set against, and a loss looms larger than an equal gain.

The Cycle

Markets swing on sentiment and herding; bubbles and panics are not the work of cold optimizers but of real psychology — overconfidence, anchoring, and the contagion of mood.

The Choice

The agent satisfices rather than optimizes, leans on rules of thumb, and departs from the rational ideal in regular, predictable ways; those very departures are the science.

The Rule

Policy can work with human psychology rather than against it — sensible defaults, careful framing, and the well-placed nudge that steers a choice while leaving it free.

Institutional

Thorstein Veblen, John R. Commons, and later Douglass North — the institutional and evolutionary line, from 1899 onward.

The market is not a state of nature but a web of rules, habits, and power, laid down by history and forever being remade.

The Wage

The wage is set by institutions — the law, the custom, the union, the firm’s internal ladder, the balance of bargaining power — far more than by any marginal product reckoned in the abstract.

The Surplus

How the surplus divides is a question of power and of rule, not of nature; who gets what is decided by the institutions a society has built, and those institutions could be built otherwise.

The Cycle

The economy evolves through its institutions — the rules of credit, the structure of finance, the habits of firms — and instability is bred by the very arrangements history has happened to lay down.

The Choice

Behavior is governed by habit and convention as much as by calculation; people follow the routines and rules of their time, and those rules, not bare preference, are what shape the economy.

The Rule

Institutions are the deep variable — the property rights, the laws, the norms that make a market possible at all; get the institutions wrong, and no quantity of market will save you.

What the contest means

No View From Nowhere

It would be a relief, and it would be false, to end by naming the school that is right. The honest report is harder: each of these traditions sees something the others miss, and each, pressed too far, goes blind in its own way. The neoclassical optimizer is a powerful fiction that the behavioralists caught departing from itself in regular ways; the Austrian faith in the price as knowledge is a deep insight that can harden into a refusal to see power; the Marxian eye for exploitation lights up what the gains-from-trade story leaves dark, and can miss the gains that are nonetheless real. They are lenses, and a lens is defined as much by what it blurs as by what it brings near.

This is not a counsel of despair, nor an invitation to believe whatever pleases you. The schools can be judged — against the evidence, against logic, against the cases they explain and the ones they cannot — and some readings have failed the test and been set aside. But no single one has won the whole field, and the pretense that economics speaks with one settled voice, from a view from nowhere, is the one thing every page of its history refutes. There is no reading of the table that is not also a choice of how to see.

Which is the book’s own thesis, reached now from the highest angle of all. It found the contest inside the price, then inside the table’s own composition, and finds it here inside the act of reading itself — for what an element means is contested, school against school, and the meaning you accept is a position taken in a quarrel that is not closed. To read the table at all is to read it as someone, from somewhere. The book has tried to say which someone it is, and where it stands, and to show you the other places one might stand instead. The rest, as ever, it leaves to you.

Return to the objections, where the mainstream answers the book back, and the chronology for how the schools arrived in time; the atlas for the elements in their order, the gaps for what the table leaves out and the priceless for where it stops; the cases for the table at work and the first person for how it is lived; the compounds and constellation for how the elements join, the properties and diagonals to read across them, the measures for the numbers, the laboratory for the models, the preface for the argument entire, the guided tour for a path, the index to find any element by name, the sources for the works behind the traditions, or the unsettled for the contests still open. The table is one; the readings are many. The argument remains open — and the schools are the proof that it always was.
