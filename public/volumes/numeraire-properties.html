On the grain of the table

What the Elements Are Like

The atlas arranges the twenty-six elements; it does not say what they are like. Yet a periodic table is more than an arrangement. The reason chemistry’s table is one of the great achievements of the mind is that its order predicts: atomic radius, electronegativity, reactivity rise and fall in regular waves as you cross it, so that an empty cell can be filled by inference before its element is ever found. The order is not a filing system but a theory. This companion asks whether the periodic table of economic life has any such grain — whether its elements, weighed by their properties, fall into patterns the alphabet hides.

They do. Below, each element is scored from one to five on five conceptual properties — how cleanly it can be measured, what scale it lives at, how much of it is a human artifice, how openly it can be seen, and how much turns on getting it right. Choose a property and the table re-colors, dark for high and pale for low, so the gradient is visible at a glance. Measurability is brightest among the prices and goes dark at the failures and the behaviors; artifice flares at the institutions and nowhere else; scale climbs steadily from the single choice to the whole economy. These are not accidents of layout. They are the shape of the subject — the reason the groups are the groups.

A caution, which is also the point. Unlike electronegativity, these scores cannot be measured; they are judgments, mine, and every one of them is arguable. You may think the wage more visible than I have marked it, or the norm more central; the cells are built to be quarreled with, and a reader who disputes a score has understood the exercise better than one who accepts it. That a quantity’s very properties are contestable is, after all, the argument of the whole work. What is not in doubt is that the grain is there. Weigh the elements however you like, and they will still arrange themselves into a hard, measured, visible core and a soft, inferred, contested periphery — with the price at the center of the light, and the norm at the edge of the dark.

What the trends reveal

The Table Has a Grain

Read across the five properties and a single structure keeps reasserting itself. There is a core of the subject — the prices, and the quantities counted on a ledger — that is measured, visible, and brute: a rate posted to the decimal, a stock counted in a warehouse. And there is a periphery — the failures, the behaviors, and the softer aggregates — that is inferred, hidden, and contested: an externality that has no price because it has escaped one, a norm that no one wrote down, a share of the whole that two Cambridges could not agree how to measure. The institutions stand apart on the one property where they dominate utterly, artifice, because they are the things the economy makes rather than finds. The order of the table is the order of this descent, from the hard and posted to the soft and inferred.

But the work’s wager is that the descent is gentler than it looks — that the bright cells are not as solid as their brightness suggests. A wage is measured, but what it measures is contested; an interest rate is posted, but what sets it is a quarrel about time and risk and the central bank’s nerve; even the price, the brightest cell of all, is the resolved value of a hidden bargain. The grain is real, and the periphery really is harder to pin than the core. Yet there is no cell that is pure fact, no quantity that is only found and not also, in part, decided. That is why the floor of the constructedness scale is never quite reached, and why the contest the whole work describes runs under the measured cells as surely as the unmeasured ones. The table has a grain; it does not have a foundation that is not, somewhere, an argument.
