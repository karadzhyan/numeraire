The Priceless

the goods the market cannot settle

the cases showed how far the table reaches; here is where it stops — the goods whose nature a price would alter, degrade, or destroy

Why it marks a limit A way of seeing as powerful as the economic one carries a standing temptation: to find a market in every relation and a price for every good. But some goods are spoiled by sale, and some trade-offs corrupt by the mere offer. The table’s last wisdom is not how far it reaches. It is knowing where to stop.

On the boundary of the market

The Edge of the Table

The book has spent its whole length showing that economic quantities are not facts of nature but contested verdicts, and the companion just before this one showed the table taking real questions apart with an unsettling reach — the rent, the crisis, the warming planet, all dissolving into elements. It would be easy to draw the wrong conclusion: that everything is a price, everything a market, everything for sale at the right number. That conclusion is the one error this book most wants to refuse.

For there are goods whose nature is altered, degraded, or destroyed by being priced — not because we measure them badly, as the measures conceal and omit, but because the very act of pricing them is a category mistake or a moral corruption. Pay for blood and you may get less of it; fine the latecomer and you may license the lateness; offer to buy a vote, a kidney, a friendship, and you change the thing into something else — something democracy, or the body, or friendship can no longer use. The economist’s instrument, turned on these goods, does not measure them. It mars them.

And yet the boundary is not obvious, and it is fiercely contested. Economists push it outward in good faith — a market in organs would save lives, a price on carbon is the only tool that scales, a paid surrogacy can lift a family from poverty — and the moralists push back, and both sides are sometimes right. What follows are nine goods set along the edge where the market meets its limit, each with the case for the price beside the good’s resistance to it, and an honest note on where the contested boundary now runs. Roughly where each sits is shown below; the order is itself disputed, and the whole scale drifts with the centuries.

i

Carbon, and Nature

the limit of the externality

The market’s case

This very book has urged it: an unpriced harm is overdone, so price the carbon, and the market — the only instrument that works at the scale required — will find the cheapest path to less of it. A price on pollution has cleaned, demonstrably, against the worst of it.

The good’s resistance

But to price a stable climate, a living species, a sacred mountain is to concede that each is for sale at the right number, and that the rich may buy the right to destroy what an offset lets them call paid for — the indulgence reborn. Some of nature, the objection runs, is incommensurable with money, and to price it is the first step in losing it.

The boundaryThe work’s own externality argues for the price; its limits argue against — and the tension does not resolve, because both the harm of leaving carbon unpriced and the harm of pricing the sacred are real.

ii

Care, and the Womb

the limit of the contract

The market’s case

A woman may sell her labor; why not her care, or the labor of bearing a child for another? To forbid paid care work or paid surrogacy is to deny consenting adults a contract that can lift one from poverty and grant another a family — paternalism dressed as protection.

The good’s resistance

But intimacy may be the kind of good that payment degrades: care given for love is not the same good as care bought by the hour, and a pregnancy carried under contract risks turning a body, and a child, into things produced to order. Where the poor alone supply the wombs and the wealthy alone commission them, the free contract starts to look like the old coercion.

The boundaryNations split sharply — some permit the paid surrogacy that others prosecute as trafficking — which is the surest sign the boundary here is contested all the way down.

iii

Blood

the limit of the wage

The market’s case

A shortage is a shortage: pay for blood and plasma and the supply rises, as the thriving paid-plasma industry attests. Squeamishness about payment costs the lives that a market would save.

The good’s resistance

Titmuss found the reverse for whole blood. Britain’s unpaid, voluntary system produced more blood, and safer blood, than America’s paid one, because payment drove out the altruist and drew in the desperate, who hid the illnesses that made their blood dangerous. The price did not add to the gift; it crowded the gift out.

The boundaryThe line now runs between blood, mostly given, and plasma, mostly paid — an unstable settlement that every shortage reopens.

iv

The Late Fine

the limit of the norm

The market’s case

People respond to incentives, so a fine for arriving late should reduce lateness — putting an honest cost on the imposition and letting those who truly value an extra ten minutes pay for it.

The good’s resistance

When a daycare tried exactly this, lateness rose. The fine became a price, and the price dissolved the obligation: parents who had felt the moral pull of not keeping the staff waiting now simply bought the right to be late — and when the fine was lifted, the old norm did not return. A price had crowded out a duty, and done so permanently.

The boundarySome duties survive being priced; others, once priced, never recover the force they held as duties — and there is no telling in advance which is which.

v

Kidneys

the limit of the power

The market’s case

Thousands die each year on the transplant lists while healthy kidneys go unsold. A regulated market, argue economists from Becker onward, would clear the shortage and save those lives; the ban, on this view, is a sentiment that kills.

The good’s resistance

A market in organs would fall hardest on the poor, who alone would sell, making destitution itself a reason to surrender a kidney — consent under duress. So strong is the revulsion that the economist Alvin Roth built a system of kidney exchange, a barter clearinghouse with no money in it at all, precisely to win the lives without crossing into sale. Repugnance, he showed, is itself an economic force.

The boundaryAlmost every nation forbids the sale and permits the gift, and the lists remain — the cost, counted in lives, of a boundary most are still unwilling to erase.

vi

A Human Life

the limit of the utility

The market’s case

Every safety rule costs money, and money is finite, so a regulator must decide how much to spend to save a life — and does, pricing a statistical life at several million dollars. To refuse the number is not noble; it is to spend blindly, and so to save fewer lives than an honest figure would.

The good’s resistance

Yet a person, Kant insisted, has not a price but a dignity — a worth above all price, by which no life can be exchanged for another or for any sum. The value of a statistical life is a useful fiction for spreading a safety budget; mistaken for the true worth of a life, it becomes monstrous, treating as commensurable what is not.

The boundaryWe price lives in the aggregate, by the thousand, to allocate — while denying that any particular life has a price at all. We live inside the contradiction because neither half of it can be given up.

vii

The Gift

the limit of the contract

The market’s case

A gift is merely an inefficient transfer: the giver guesses what you want and usually guesses wrong, destroying value that cash — which you could spend exactly as you wished — would have preserved. Economists have written, in earnest, of the deadweight loss of Christmas.

The good’s resistance

But the gift is not trying to be an efficient transfer. It is a language of relationship — of attention, obligation, and honor — and cash speaks the wrong language entirely: to hand a friend a bill for dinner, or to repay a kindness with an invoice, is not to economize but to insult. Mauss showed the gift to be its own order, with its own laws, and none of them the laws of price.

The boundaryThe home, the friendship, the favor run on gift and duty; the market runs on price; and most of us police the border between them daily, without ever being told where it lies.

viii

The Vote

the limit of the rule

The market’s case

If I care little for my vote and you care greatly for yours, a trade would leave us both better off — the plainest logic of exchange. Why should this one good alone be barred from the gains that voluntary trade confers?

The good’s resistance

Because a vote is not a private possession but a share in collective self-rule, and to sell it is to convert a citizen into a seller and an election into an auction won by the rich. The good is changed by the sale: a purchased vote is no longer the thing democracy requires of it, however identical the ballot looks.

The boundaryEvery democracy bans the sale of votes outright — one of the few places the market’s reach is refused flatly, and without apology.

ix

The Sacred

the limit of the choice

The market’s case

Everything has its trade-offs, and refusing to name a price for what we hold sacred is mere squeamishness — an evasion of the hard choices that scarcity forces on everyone, whether they will admit it or not.

The good’s resistance

Yet Tetlock found that merely being asked to price the sacred — what would you take to sell your vote, your child, your loyalty? — provokes not calculation but moral outrage, and a frantic effort to repair a self that even entertained the question. Some values are constituted by our refusal to trade them; to weigh them against money is already to have betrayed them. The offer itself is the corruption.

The boundaryThis is the table’s outer edge — the goods that are sacred precisely because they are kept from the market, and that the mere naming of a price would profane.

What the edge teaches

Knowing Where to Stop

Three things are true of this edge at once, and the difficulty is that they pull in different directions. The first is that the boundary is real: crowding out is not a moral intuition but a measured fact, in the blood bank and the daycare, where a price demonstrably drove out a gift or a duty and left less behind than there had been. Some goods genuinely are spoiled by sale, and the spoiling can be observed.

The second is that the boundary is contested, and the people pushing the market outward are not villains. A market in kidneys really would save lives now lost; a price on carbon really is the most powerful tool we have; a ban on paid care or surrogacy really can be a wealthy society’s way of denying the poor a choice. Against each stands a real fear of coercion, of degradation, of the slow conversion of citizens into customers — and the honest position, here as everywhere in the book, is that the argument is open, not that one side has already won it.

The third is the deepest, and it is the book’s own thesis returning to its source: the boundary moves. Things once unthinkable to sell — people, children, votes, offices, pardons for sin — were once routinely sold, and were placed off the market only by long moral struggle; and things once held priceless — the right to pollute, a stretch of human attention, the record of a life — have been quietly brought onto it in our own time. There is no fixed, natural line between what may be priced and what may not. The line itself is a verdict on a hidden contest — the very verdict the book has found inside every price, now found at the market’s outermost edge.

Which is the last thing the table has to teach. A way of seeing as powerful as the economic one — and it is powerful; thirteen companions have borne witness to its reach — carries a standing temptation to see everything its way, to find a market in every relation and a price for every good. The discipline’s own name for going too far is economic imperialism, and the cure is not to throw the instrument away but to know its edge: to hold at once that every price is a verdict on a hidden contest, and that the deepest contest of all is over what should be brought to a price at all. That contest the table cannot settle. It can only, at its wisest, mark the place where it begins.
