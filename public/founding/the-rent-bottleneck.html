The Bottleneck

Three plates were spent on land, and it is time to confess that land was only ever the example. Everything the law of rent describes — a factor fixed in supply, a surplus that demand alone determines, a price determined by rent rather than determining it — depends not at all on the factor's being soil. It depends only on the factor's being scarce, and fixed, and pressed upon by demand. The economist who saw this most clearly was Alfred Marshall, and the instrument he forged for it was the quasi-rent: the rent-like surplus earned by any factor whose supply cannot quickly adjust — a machine, a skill, a patent, a brand — which obeys every law of rent while its fixity lasts, and erodes when supply at last responds. This plate follows the generalization to its end. It shows that the difference between rent and the ordinary returns to capital and labour is one of time, not kind; that rent appears wherever there is a bottleneck; and that the line between a quasi-rent rewarding genuine creation and a rent merely extracting a toll is the line the whole volume has been drawing. It is the bridge from the land of the first plates to the talents, licences, and platforms of the plates to come.

I

Beyond the Field

Three plates have been spent on land, and it is time to confess that land was only ever the example. Everything the law of rent describes — a factor fixed in supply, a surplus that demand alone determines, a price determined by rent rather than determining it — depends not at all on the factor's being soil. It depends only on the factor's being scarce, and fixed, and pressed upon by demand. Land is the purest case, because its supply is fixed forever and by nature; but it is a case, not the category. The category is larger, and once seen it is seen everywhere.

The economist who saw it most clearly, and gave the seeing its lasting vocabulary, was Alfred Marshall, whose Principles of Economics (1890) did for the generation around 1900 what Ricardo's Principles had done eighty years before: it set the terms of the discipline. Marshall's contribution here was a single fertile idea — that the surplus Ricardo had found in land is a special case of a surplus that attaches, for a time, to any factor whose supply cannot quickly adjust. He called it quasi-rent, and with it he turned a theory of ground rent into a theory of the whole economy.1

II

The Fixed Factor in the Short Run

Begin with a machine. A manufacturer builds one — a press, a loom, a turbine — at considerable cost. The moment it is finished, something important changes about it: its cost of construction is sunk, gone, beyond recovery, and therefore irrelevant to every decision that follows. What governs the machine's use from now on is not what it cost to build but what it costs to run; and so long as the machine earns more than that running cost, it will be kept working, for the only alternative is to leave it idle and earn nothing at all.2

This means that the machine's earnings, in the period before more machines like it can be built, are set not by its cost but by demand. If the product it makes is keenly wanted, the machine earns far above its running cost, and the surplus is large; if the product is little wanted, the surplus is small; but in either case the surplus is whatever demand leaves after the running cost is met. The machine, in the short run, behaves exactly as land does: its supply is fixed, demand alone fixes its return, and the return is a residual — price-determined, not price-determining. The fixed factor need not be a gift of nature. It need only be fixed.3

III

Quasi-Rent

Marshall gave this surplus its name. The income earned by a factor whose supply is fixed in the short run he called quasi-rent — rent-like, because while the fixity lasts it obeys every law of rent, but only quasi, because the fixity will not last. In the short run the distinction between quasi-rent and true rent is invisible. Both are surpluses thrown off by a factor that cannot multiply; both are governed by demand; both could be taxed away without changing anyone's behaviour, since the factor sits there earning its surplus regardless. Stand close to a machine earning a fat return in a boom and you cannot tell its income from the rent of land. The two look identical, behave identically, and are, for the moment, the same thing.

The word quasi carries the whole of the difference, and the difference is not of kind but of duration. True rent is the surplus of a factor fixed forever; quasi-rent is the surplus of a factor fixed for now. To see what for now does to the picture, one has only to let time pass.4

IV

The Difference Is Time

Let the boom continue, and the machines earning their fat returns will not go unnoticed. Other manufacturers, seeing the surplus, build machines of their own; the supply that was fixed in the short run begins, in the long run, to grow; and as it grows, the product becomes more plentiful, its price falls, and the surplus on every machine shrinks. The process halts only when the return on a machine has fallen to the level that just suffices to call forth new ones — the cost of building them, plus the ordinary return on the capital they absorb. At that point the quasi-rent has been competed away entirely, and what remains is merely the normal return to capital, the long-run supply price of the machine. The surplus was real, but it was temporary; it lasted exactly as long as the supply stayed fixed, and no longer.5

Here is the deep result, the one that reorganises the theory of distribution. The difference between rent and the ordinary returns to capital and labour is not a difference of kind; it is a difference of time horizon. In the very short run, every factor is fixed and earns a quasi-rent. Over the long run, the reproducible factors — capital, ordinary labour — watch their quasi-rents competed away as supply adjusts, and are left earning only their cost of reproduction. Only the factors that cannot be reproduced — land, and the handful of things as fixed as land — go on earning a true and permanent rent. Rent is not a separate species of income sitting beside wages and profit; it is what every income would be if its factor could never be increased, and wages and profit are merely the rents of things that can. Time, Marshall held, lies near the centre of almost every difficulty in economics, and nowhere more than here, where it alone divides the rent that lasts from the rent that fades.6

V

The Many Bottlenecks

Once rent is unchained from land, it appears wherever the same conditions are met — wherever supply is fixed, or barred from adjusting, and demand presses against the limit. The condition is general, and so the phenomenon is general. A drug under patent earns a rent, because the law forbids anyone else to make it. A surgeon of rare gifts, or a singer, earns a rent, because the supply of such gifts cannot be expanded to meet the want of them. A taxi medallion, a liquor licence, a banking charter earns a rent, because the number of them is capped. A platform that everyone must use to reach everyone else earns a rent, because its position cannot be duplicated. A brand a century in the building earns a rent, because a century is how long a rival would need to match it. Each is a bottleneck — a narrow place that others must pass through and one party controls — and at each, the controller collects a surplus above what was needed to bring the thing into being.7

The bottlenecks differ in their nature and in their permanence, and the plates to come take them one by one: the rent of scarce talent in the next, the rent of legal restriction in the one after, the rents of patent and platform in the modern plates near the end. But they share a skeleton, and it is Ricardo's — a fixed supply, a pressing demand, a surplus to whoever holds the fixed thing. Land was the first bottleneck economics understood. It was not the last, nor, in a modern economy, the largest.8

VI

Reward or Extraction?

There is a distinction here that the volume has been circling since its first plate, and quasi-rent forces it into the open. Land rent, the earlier plates insisted, is unearned: no one made the land, and its rent rewards no act of creation. But quasi-rent is different, in a way that matters morally and analytically both. Someone built the machine. Someone risked the capital, and might have lost it; someone spent the decade acquiring the rare skill. The quasi-rent that the machine or the skill earns is, taken over its whole life, the reward for that building and risking and acquiring — and it must be, or the building would never be done. A manufacturer who could never earn a surplus over his running costs would never construct the machine in the first place; the quasi-rents of its fat years are precisely what repay the cost and the hazard of making it. Seen over the full span, quasi-rent is not a windfall but a return on investment, the indispensable lure that calls forth the capital and the effort a society needs.9

And yet. The same income, viewed at a single instant, is indistinguishable from a pure rent; and the difference between a quasi-rent that is rewarding genuine creation and a rent that is merely being extracted can be desperately hard to read from the outside — the more so when the surplus is large, and lasting, and defended. A quasi-rent that springs from luck rather than effort; one inflated by an artificial barrier the holder lobbied into being; one that persists, decade upon decade, long after the investment that justified it has been repaid many times over, because something keeps the supply from ever adjusting — such a surplus has drifted across the line, from the reward of creation toward the extraction of a toll. The whole moral argument of this volume lives in that drift, and in the difficulty of seeing it. The plates ahead will watch a series of quasi-rents and ask, of each, on which side of the line it has come to rest.10

VII

The Spectrum of Fixity

It is best, in the end, to stop thinking of rent as a box and start thinking of it as a pole. At one extreme stand land and the things as permanently scarce as land — a unique site, a finite ore, a band of the spectrum — whose supply can never grow, and whose rent is therefore pure and perpetual. At the other extreme stand the perfectly reproducible things, made freely and at once to meet any demand, which earn no rent at all because their supply forever answers their price. And between the poles lies almost everything: the vast middle country of quasi-rents, where supply adjusts but slowly, and the surplus lasts a while — a year, a generation, the term of a patent, the life of a brand — before it fades, if it fades. Every asset in an economy can be placed somewhere on that line by asking a single question: how fast can its supply respond? The nearer the fixed pole, the more rent-like and lasting its return; the nearer the reproducible pole, the more fleeting.11

This is the map the rest of the volume will walk. The land plates explored the fixed pole; the plates to come move through the middle country, where the modern economy increasingly lives — among talents that cannot be copied, licences that cannot be had, platforms and patents and networks whose fixity is engineered and whose quasi-rents show, year upon year, a stubborn refusal to erode.12 The concept's reach runs wider still: Marshall's own extension of it — the composite quasi-rent shared between two factors worth far more together than apart — became, generations later, a seed of the modern theory of the firm, in which the scramble to capture a jointly created surplus helps explain why some exchanges are folded inside a company rather than trusted to the open market.13 Whether the modern quasi-rents' refusal to fade is the honest reward of creation or the quiet entrenchment of a toll is the question the volume now carries into its second half. Ricardo found rent in the ground; Marshall found it everywhere the ground's condition was met; and the everywhere has only grown.

A bottleneck is a simple thing: a narrow place, a thing in fixed supply, a crowd that must pass through, and a hand at the valve. The land was the first such place economics learned to see, but it was never the only one, and in an age of patents and platforms and networks it may no longer be the most important. What unites the toll on a field with the toll on a franchise and the toll on a platform is not the soil but the structure — supply that cannot answer demand, and an owner who collects the difference. Marshall taught economics to see the structure wherever it stood, and to mark, by the speed at which supply could answer, how long each toll would last. The tolls that fade are the wages of creation. The tolls that do not fade are something else — and telling the two apart, asset by asset and age by age, is the work the rest of these plates have set themselves.

1Alfred Marshall, Principles of Economics (1890), especially Book V on value and the role of time, where the concept of quasi-rent is developed. Marshall's Principles was the dominant English-language economics text for a generation; the generalisation of rent to any temporarily-fixed factor is among its most influential moves.

2Sunk costs and the short run: once a durable asset is built, its construction cost is sunk and irrelevant to the decision whether to operate it; only variable (running) costs bear on that decision, and the asset is used so long as it covers them. This is the foundation of the short-run / long-run distinction in the theory of the firm, and the reason a fixed factor's return is a residual.

3With the asset's quantity fixed in the short run, its return is set by the demand for its output, not by its cost — exactly Ricardo's “price-determined, not price-determining” (cf. plates i, ii), now applied to a man-made factor. The return is what demand leaves after variable costs are met: the quasi-rent.

4Quasi-rent, defined: the surplus earned by a factor fixed in the short run but not the long. While the fixity lasts it is indistinguishable from true rent — both demand-governed, both taxable without distortion — and the difference appears only over time, as quasi-rent erodes while true rent does not. The term and the distinction are Marshall's.

5The long-run erosion: high quasi-rents attract new supply (more of the reproducible factor is built), driving the return down to the long-run supply price — the cost of reproduction plus the normal return on capital. There the quasi-rent is zero and only the normal return remains. For a truly fixed factor such as land, no such adjustment occurs, and the rent persists.

6The reorganisation of distribution theory: the difference between rent and the returns to capital and labour is one of time horizon, not kind. In the very short run all factors earn quasi-rents; in the long run, reproducible factors are left with their cost of reproduction, and only non-reproducible factors earn permanent rent. This is the marginal-productivity vision (cf. plate ii, n6) seen through time — rent as the limiting case of any factor's return when its supply is fixed for good. Marshall held that time lies at the centre of nearly every difficulty in economics (Principles, Book V), and the rent / quasi-rent distinction is a leading instance.

7The generality of the bottleneck: rent and quasi-rent arise wherever supply is fixed or restricted and demand presses — patents (legal restriction), scarce talent (natural limit), licences and charters (statutory caps), network platforms (positional uniqueness), durable brands (slow replicability). Each is taken up later: talent in plate v, legal restriction in vi, patents and platforms in xi.

8The shared skeleton: each of these is Ricardo's structure — fixed supply, pressing demand, surplus to the holder of the fixed thing — in a different costume. Land was the first such structure economics analysed; it is neither the last nor, in a modern economy, necessarily the largest, as plates xi and xii will argue.

9Quasi-rent as a return on investment: unlike land rent, the quasi-rent on a created asset rewards the act of creating it. Over the asset's life the quasi-rents must sum to recover the sunk cost plus a normal return, or the asset would never be built; the surplus of the good years is the reward, paid in arrears, for the investment and the risk. This is the crucial disanalogy with land, which no one made — cf. plate i on pure rent versus the return on improvement, and plate iii on the unearned increment.

10The drift from reward to extraction: a quasi-rent shades into a pure, extractive rent when it springs from luck rather than effort, from an artificial barrier the holder procured, or from a persistence far beyond what the original investment requires — sustained by something that keeps supply from adjusting. The line between compensatory quasi-rent and extractive rent is the line the volume draws; reading it from the outside is the central practical difficulty, and the matter of the rent-seeking (vii), efficiency (viii), and modern-rent (xi, xii) plates.

11The spectrum of fixity: assets range from the permanently fixed (land, finite resources, spectrum — pure perpetual rent) to the perfectly reproducible (no rent), with quasi-rents of every durability between. An asset's place is set by how fast its supply can respond to price; the slower the response, the more rent-like and durable the return.

12The map of the volume's second half: the remaining plates walk the spectrum from the fixed pole into the middle country — scarce talent (v), legal restriction (vi), and the engineered fixities of the modern economy (xi, xii) — asking of each quasi-rent whether it rewards creation or entrenches a toll.

13The concept's wider afterlife: Marshall also described the composite quasi-rent earned jointly by two factors specialised to each other and worth far more together than apart — a surplus whose division between them the market cannot settle. A century on, that idea became a foundation of the modern theory of the firm (Klein, Crawford & Alchian, 1978; Williamson), where the danger of being held up over a jointly-created surplus explains why specialised exchanges are often brought inside a single firm rather than left to contract. Quasi-rent, born to generalise Ricardo, turned out to illuminate the boundaries of the corporation too.
