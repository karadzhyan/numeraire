Numéraire The Rent · № II · vii

The Seekers

Every plate so far has asked what a rent is and who collects it. This one asks a harder question: what does the contest to capture a rent cost the world? For most of the history of economics the answer was thought to be almost nothing — the rent a mere transfer, costless to society, the only real loss the small triangle of trades a monopoly prevents. Gordon Tullock saw, in 1967, that this was profoundly wrong. The rent is a prize, and a prize that can be competed for will be; firms spend real resources — lobbying, litigating, capturing the officials who write the rules — struggling to win and keep it, and those resources are not transferred but consumed, burned in the fight, producing nothing. The great rectangle the old accounting dismissed is in truth largely dissipated in the seeking. This plate follows Anne Krueger, who named rent-seeking and measured its staggering scale; it draws the crucial line between profit-seeking, paid for creating value, and rent-seeking, paid for capturing it; and it presses, with Baumol, to the deepest cost of all — the diversion of a society's ablest people from making things to capturing them, compounding over time into the sclerosis of nations. The mere existence of capturable rents, it argues, taxes a society before a single rent is paid: every rent left lying about is a wage offered, to the most capable, to do nothing at all.

I

The Two Costs

Every plate so far has asked what a rent is and who pockets it. This one asks a different and harder question: what does the contest to capture a rent cost the world? The answer, for most of the history of economics, was thought to be almost nothing. When a monopoly or a protected trade raises its price above cost, the textbook drew two regions. One was a triangle — the trades that no longer happen because the price has risen too high, a genuine loss to society, value that simply vanishes. The other was a rectangle — the extra that consumers now pay and the favoured few now collect, larger by far than the triangle. But the rectangle, the textbook said, was a mere transfer: the consumer's loss is the recipient's gain, the two cancel, and society as a whole is no poorer for it. On this view the only real cost of monopoly was the little triangle; and when Arnold Harberger measured those triangles for the whole American economy in 1954, he found them astonishingly small — a fraction of one percent of national income. Monopoly, it seemed, was barely worth the worry.1

Gordon Tullock saw, in 1967, that this was profoundly wrong, and that the error lay in calling the rectangle a mere transfer. For the rectangle is a prize — a stream of rent that goes to whoever captures the monopoly or the protection — and a prize, if it can be competed for, will be. Parties will spend real resources, up toward the whole value of the rectangle, struggling to win and keep the privilege that yields it: lobbying, litigating, advertising, courting the powerful, maintaining the offices and lawyers and lobbyists whose only function is to capture the rent. Those resources are not transferred; they are consumed, used up in the fight, producing nothing. The rectangle is not costlessly handed from one pocket to another — it is, in large part, burned in the contest over whose pocket it lands in. And since the rectangle dwarfs the triangle, the true cost of monopoly and protection is far larger than the old measure supposed: not the small loss of the trades prevented, but the great loss of the resources spent fighting over the spoils.2

II

Rent-Seeking

The behaviour Tullock had identified needed a name, and Anne Krueger gave it one in 1974: rent-seeking — the pursuit of income by capturing an existing rent rather than by creating new wealth. Krueger had studied economies organised around the permit and the licence — India under its licence-permit raj, Turkey under its import quotas — where to hold a government licence was to hold a fortune, and where, in consequence, vast energy was poured into obtaining licences. She tried to measure the rents at stake, and the figures were staggering: in India in 1964, she reckoned, the rents created by government restrictions came to more than seven percent of national income; in Turkey in 1968, the rents from import licences alone amounted to some fifteen percent of the entire economy. Where rents of that scale lie about to be captured, capturing them becomes a national occupation, and the resources spent capturing them are subtracted from everything else a nation might have done.3

Her studies turned up illustrations so pure they would be comic if they were not ruinous. In Turkey, exporters could earn a government subsidy for shipping goods abroad; some accordingly loaded vessels with stones, sailed them out to sea, dumped the cargo overboard, and collected the subsidy — an entire voyage undertaken, an entire crew employed, to produce precisely nothing, the cargo destroyed and the reward pocketed. It is the emblem of the whole phenomenon: effort and resources expended not to make anything but to capture a transfer, the making of wealth replaced by the chasing of it.

III

Seeking versus Making

The force of the idea comes from a distinction it draws between two kinds of seeking that look alike and are opposite. There is profit-seeking, the pursuit of gain by making something — a better product, a cheaper process, a service someone wants — and being paid for the value one has created. And there is rent-seeking, the pursuit of gain by capturing something — a tariff that shuts out a rival, a licence that bars a competitor, a subsidy, a monopoly grant — and being paid for a transfer one has arranged. The first is the engine of the economy: the competition to make better and cheaper things drives prices down and quality up, and leaves the world richer even for those who lose the contest, since the goods they failed to sell were nonetheless made and sold by someone. The second is a drain: the competition to capture a privilege drives nothing forward, consuming real resources to shift wealth from one party to another, and leaving the world poorer by exactly the resources consumed.4

The two are hard to tell apart from the inside, for the rent-seeker experiences his lobbying as hard and skilful work, no less than the entrepreneur experiences hers; and the rewards can be equal, or larger, for the seeker. The difference is not in the effort or the talent but in what the effort produces: the maker leaves behind a thing that did not exist before, and the seeker leaves behind nothing but a rearrangement of who owns what. An economy is the sum of these choices, and what it becomes depends on which of them it pays for.

IV

Where the Talent Goes

This is the insight William Baumol pressed to its profound conclusion in 1990. The supply of talent and ambition in a society, he argued, is more or less given; what varies, and varies enormously, is how that talent is allocated — whether a society's ablest and most driven people pour themselves into making things or into capturing them. And what governs the allocation is the structure of rewards: the rules of the game that decide whether the surest path to a great fortune runs through building a better enterprise or through securing a government favour, through innovation or through influence. Where the rules reward making, the talent builds, and the society grows rich on what it builds. Where the rules reward seeking — where the largest fortunes are made by obtaining monopolies, exemptions, and protections — the same talent turns to seeking, and the society stagnates, not because it lacks ability but because it has aimed its ability at the wrong target.5

This is why the cost of rent-seeking is so much greater than any tally of lobbying budgets would suggest. The deepest loss is not the money spent on lobbyists; it is the diversion of a society's best minds from the work of creation to the work of capture — the engineer who becomes a tax lawyer, the inventor who becomes a lobbyist, the genius for building turned into a genius for extracting. Economies that set more of their talent to making tend to grow faster than those that set it to claiming; the allocation of a nation's ability is, in the long run, among the deepest determinants of whether it prospers.6 A society reveals its character, and seals its fate, in the question of what it makes most worthwhile to do.

V

How Much Is Lost?

How much of the rent, then, is actually dissipated in the seeking of it? The pure theory gives a startling answer: if many parties compete for a rent on equal terms, the total they spend competing tends toward the whole value of the rent, so that the entire rectangle is consumed and nothing survives as a net transfer — the privilege paid for in full by the struggle to obtain it. Richard Posner pressed this logic hard in 1975, treating the rent as fully dissipated and concluding that the social cost of regulation was correspondingly vast. Yet the world does not always oblige the theory. Observed spending on lobbying, even by industries that win enormous favours, often looks surprisingly small beside the rents at stake — a puzzle known as the Tullock paradox, whose explanations range from the advantages of incumbents, who need spend little to keep what they already hold, to the sheer difficulty of seeing rent-seeking that takes the form of relationships, favours, and quiet understandings rather than invoiced expense. The true dissipation lies somewhere between the theory's totality and the ledgers' modesty, and is genuinely hard to pin down.7

What is not in doubt is that the cost compounds over time, and Mancur Olson explained why in 1982. A stable society, he argued, steadily accumulates what he called distributional coalitions — trade associations, licensed professions, protected industries, every group organised to capture and defend a rent, and each, once entrenched, nearly impossible to dislodge. As these coalitions multiply, the economy fills with rents to be sought and barriers to be guarded; more and more of its energy flows into the seeking and the guarding; and its capacity to grow and adapt slowly ossifies. The accumulation of rent-seekers is, in Olson's telling, a principal reason that nations rise and then decline — that the most settled and successful economies are precisely the ones most prone, in time, to sclerosis.8

VI

The Honest Complications

The argument can be pressed too far, and honesty requires marking where it strains. Not all influence is waste. Some of what looks like rent-seeking is the provision of genuine information: a firm that explains its industry to a legislator may be lobbying for advantage, but it may also be supplying knowledge that makes for better policy, and the line between the two is not always clear. Some competition for a rent is, in effect, a competition to use a resource well — when a licence or a contract goes to the highest bidder, the bidding is rent-seeking in form, but it may select the party who will put the underlying asset to its most productive use, and the payment, if it goes to the public, is captured rather than wasted. And the boundary between profit-seeking and rent-seeking, so crisp in principle, blurs in practice: the innovator who wins a temporary monopoly through a better product is capturing a rent, yet the prospect of that rent is exactly what drove the innovation — the productive and the extractive entangled, as the plates on the bottleneck and the talent already found them.9

There is, finally, the difficulty of measurement and even of definition. What counts as unproductive is not always self-evident — is advertising rent-seeking, or information? is litigation? — and reasonable people draw the line differently, which makes the total cost of rent-seeking a matter of estimate and argument rather than of settled fact.10 None of this overturns the core insight; it qualifies it. Resources spent capturing transfers rather than creating value are a loss; the existence of capturable rents creates the incentive to spend them; and a society that rewards capture over creation will get less creation. That much is robust. The complications tell us only that the loss is hard to measure, and that not every instance is pure — not that the phenomenon is unreal.

VII

The Tax on a Rent-Ridden World

Stand back, and the full cost of a manufactured rent comes into view, far larger than any single plate has shown. There is the transfer itself, the rent extracted from the public — unfair, but in the old accounting not a social loss. There is the deadweight loss, the trades the restriction prevents — the small triangle of the textbooks. There is the dissipation, the resources consumed in the contest to capture the rent — the great rectangle, burned in the seeking. And there is the deepest cost of all, the misallocation of a society's talent and ambition away from making and toward capturing, compounding year over year into the difference between a society that grows and one that ossifies.11 The mere existence of capturable rents taxes a society before a single rent is paid, for the prospect of them bends its energy toward the contest; and a world thick with rents to be seized is a world that has set its ablest people to seizing rather than building.

This is why the structure of an economy — whether its rules make the path to fortune run through creation or through capture — may matter more for its prosperity than almost anything else, a theme that runs from this plate out into the largest questions of why some nations are rich and others poor.12 It is also why the next two plates are necessary. If the contest for rents is so costly, the question presses whether any of it can be defended — whether some rents, or some competition for them, might serve a purpose the bare theory misses, acting as a signal or a spur the economy could not do without; that is the work of the plate to come. And if rents cannot be wished away, the question becomes how to capture or neutralise them without paying the price of the scramble — how to take the rent while killing the seeking; that is the work of the plate after.13 The volume's long thread — the difference between earning by making and earning by holding — reaches here its sharpest formulation: that the contest over who shall hold is itself a cost, and often the largest one, levied on everyone by the simple fact that there is something to hold.

A society can spend its genius building or it can spend it grasping, and mostly it does some of each; the proportion is the measure of its health, and in the long run of its wealth. The rents are always there to be had — in the land, the gift, the licence, the charter, the privilege — and wherever they are large and capturable they call to the ambitious like a second profession, one that pays as well as making and produces nothing. The cost of that calling is not chiefly the money spent answering it. It is the bridges unbuilt, the cures undiscovered, the enterprises unfounded, because the people who might have built and discovered and founded them were busy, instead, at the gate, collecting. A world that wishes to be rich must make the making worth more than the seeking — and must see that every rent it leaves lying about is a wage it is offering, to its most capable citizens, to do nothing at all.
