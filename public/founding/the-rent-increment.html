The Increment

Ricardo drew the mechanism with a cold eye and left the moral question lying in the field. It was picked up, two generations later, by a self-taught San Francisco journalist who had watched a young country's land values explode and its poverty deepen in the very same motion, and who concluded the two were one fact seen twice. Henry George's answer, in the best-selling work of economics of its century, was that the rent of land is an unearned increment — value created by the growth and works of the community, captured by whoever holds the title — and that this, not any failure of industry or thrift, is why material progress, instead of abolishing want, deepens it. This plate follows his diagnosis, his prophetic account of land speculation and the boom-bust cycle, and his remedy: the single tax, which would take the rent of the earth and abolish every other tax, claiming three virtues at once that no other tax can hold together. It then marks where the great indictment overreaches — and why its core has outlived the movement built on it.

I

The Paradox

Ricardo had drawn the mechanism and walked on, leaving the moral question lying in the field. It was picked up, two generations later and half a world away, by a man with no university behind him — a printer's case, a few hard years at sea, a journalist's eye — who had watched the thing happen in front of him. Henry George came to San Francisco as a young man and saw California convulse with growth: the railroad arriving, the population multiplying, fortunes erupting from the bare ground. And he saw, in the same streets and the same years, want and squalor deepen exactly as the wealth piled up. The two facts sat side by side and would not be reconciled by anything he had been taught. In 1879 he published the book that tried to reconcile them, and it made him, for a season, one of the most famous men alive.1

The puzzle in its title — Progress and Poverty — was the puzzle of the century. The nineteenth century had believed, with some reason, that material progress would lift the poor: that growing wealth and advancing technique and rising productivity would raise the floor beneath the worst-off. And in the aggregate the wealth was indeed swelling astoundingly. Yet the poverty would not lift. In the most advanced cities, amid the greatest accumulations, festered the most abject slums; progress and poverty advanced together, and the advance of the one seemed somehow to feed the other. Why should the richest societies harbour the bitterest want? Why did the tide that raised so many vessels strand so many others on the bottom? George named this the great enigma of the age and set out to find its cause — and found it, he believed, in the ground.2

II

The Answer in the Ground

His answer was Ricardo's law, taken with deadly seriousness and followed to its end. The gains of progress, George argued, are captured by the rent of land. As a society advances — as its people grow more numerous, its arts more cunning, its capital deeper — the produce it can wring from the earth swells. But that swelling produce does not raise wages or the return on capital; it is intercepted, on its way, by rent. The growth lifts the demand for land, and so its rent; and because the land is fixed, the rent simply rises to absorb the gain, leaving the labourer and the capitalist where they were — and often worse, for as rent climbs, the margin at which they must work recedes, and the share left to be divided between them thins.

Here was the resolution of the paradox. Progress does not fail to produce wealth; it produces it in floods. It fails to distribute it, because a third party stands at the gate — the landowner — and takes the increase as it goes by. The more the society produces, the more there is for rent to claim, and the more it claims. The worker on the advancing frontier of a rich country runs faster and faster and stays in the same place, because the ground he runs on is owned, and its owner collects the speed.3

III

The Unearned Increment

And now the moral charge, which is the book's true engine and the reason it set fire to a generation. The rise in the value of land — the swelling rent that intercepts the gains of progress — is created by no act of the landowner. It is created by the community.

John Stuart Mill had given the thing its name a few years earlier and called it the unearned increment, and had proposed, gently, to tax its future growth for the public benefit. George took the name and removed the gentleness.4 For if the increment is unearned — if the value of land is the community's creation — then to let it fall into private hands is to let a few collect a private tax on the presence and progress of everyone else. The landowner, in George's accounting, is not a producer but a tollgate: he stands on a spot the community has made valuable and charges the community for the use of it. And because land underlies everything — every farm, every factory, every home must stand somewhere — the toll is universal and inescapable. This, and not any defect of industry or thrift or character, was for George the root of the poverty that shadowed progress: that the value the many created was captured by the few who held the ground. He was emphatic that the fruits of a man's own labour were sacredly his — he was no socialist, and said so often and hotly — but the bounty of nature, and the increment the community laid upon it, belonged by right to all.5

IV

The Speculator's Withholding

To Ricardo's mechanism George added one of his own, and it was prophetic. The expectation of rising land values, he saw, sets off a speculation that makes the whole process worse. If land will be worth more next year, its owners have reason to hold it now — to keep it idle or half-used, waiting for the price to climb. But land held out of use is land the community must do without; to meet its needs, production is forced onto land further out, or driven harder where it sits, and the margin is pushed past where present need alone would set it. Speculation, in other words, lifts rent above even the level the bare growth would justify, and depresses wages further, by withholding the good land and crowding everyone onto the poorer.

And because the expectation feeds on itself, the speculation inflates until it cannot be sustained, and breaks. George argued that the great commercial depressions of his century were, at root, land crises — booms in the value of the ground, overrunning what any use could justify, collapsing and dragging the productive economy down behind them. He was describing, a century and a half early and in all its essentials, the mechanism of the land and housing bubbles that would convulse the next century and our own; that the cause of the boom and the cause of the bust were the same ownership of the same scarce thing.6

V

The Single Tax

What, then, was to be done? George's answer was as bold as his diagnosis, and far more carefully built than his enemies allowed. He did not propose to seize the land. He had no wish to nationalise farms or dispossess households or set the state to managing fields — he saw, as clearly as any critic, that private possession gives land its best use, and that the security of improvements must be preserved or no one would improve. What he proposed was to leave the land in private hands and take its rent. A tax upon the value of land — the bare site, excluding every improvement — pitched high enough to absorb very nearly the whole of the rent, and used to fund the government in place of all other taxes. The single tax.7

The proposal carried three virtues at once, and the combination is what made it so beguiling. It was just: it returned to the community the value the community had created, and left to each person the full fruits of his own labour, taxing nothing that anyone had made. It was sufficient: the rent of land, George believed, was a fund so vast that it could meet the entire expense of the state, allowing every other tax — on wages, on trade, on industry, on the products of effort — to be swept away. And it was efficient in a way no other tax could match: because land is fixed in quantity, a tax on its value cannot shrink the supply or drive it into hiding, as a tax on labour or capital or goods will; it falls on a surplus, alters no one's conduct at the margin, and so carries none of the deadweight loss that every other tax inflicts. A levy that was just, ample, and costless — and that would, into the bargain, end the speculation by stripping away the gain from holding land idle. It is no wonder the idea swept the world. The deepest of its claims, the efficiency one, is not a Georgist enthusiasm but a theorem of public finance; the ninth plate will prove it, and weigh how much of the rest can be made to stand.8

VI

What the Indictment Proves and Overreaches

The argument is more than a historical curiosity, and parts of it are simply correct. That the value of land is largely a social creation is true, and important, and too often forgotten. That a tax on pure land rent is uniquely efficient — that it can raise revenue without the distortion every other tax carries — is not a Georgist enthusiasm but a theorem, which the ninth plate will prove, and which remains one of the most underused results in public finance. And that land, and the housing built upon it, sits closer to the centre of wealth and inequality than the textbooks of the twentieth century allowed is a thesis the twenty-first century's data have powerfully revived, as the tenth and twelfth plates will show. On its central insight — that rent is unearned, that taxing it is efficient, that land is the quiet engine of maldistribution — George was largely right.9

But the indictment overreaches at its edges, and honesty requires marking where. The boldest claim — that land rent alone could fund the entire state, the single in the single tax — was probably never quite true and is not true now; pure land rent, though large, is not by itself enough to run a modern government, and the totalising form of the proposal asks more of the land than the land can bear. The transition is a graver difficulty still. A man who has bought his land at a price already reflecting its rent — often with the savings of a lifetime, or a mortgage he is still paying down — has not pocketed the unearned increment; the seller did. A tax confiscating the rent at a stroke would fall not on the windfall-takers but on him, wiping out a value he paid for in full. A justice aimed at the unearned can, applied suddenly, land hardest on the innocent. And the old practical trouble has never gone away: to tax the value of bare land one must first separate it from the value of the buildings and drains and clearing heaped upon it, and that assessment, though far from impossible, is delicate enough to have halted many a reform at the threshold. These are not refutations. They are the boundaries of a true idea — and the plates ahead return to each.10

VII

The Afterlife

For a moment George was everywhere. Progress and Poverty sold in the millions, was translated across Europe and Asia, pressed on workingmen and read aloud in lecture halls, and by some reckonings was outsold in English, in the 1890s, by no book but the Bible; its admirers ran from Shaw, who credited it with making him a socialist, to Einstein, who called it a marriage of intellectual keenness and the love of justice. Its author, the self-taught printer, ran for mayor of New York in 1886 and came second — ahead of the young Theodore Roosevelt, behind a Tammany-backed Hewitt, amid lasting and credible charges that the count was rigged against him. Even the board game now sold as a monument to acquisitive capitalism began life as a Georgist parable, patented by a follower to show how the landlord's rent grinds the other players, one by one, into ruin.11 A movement took his name; single-tax clubs sprang up on three continents; and the idea seeped, in diluted forms, into the law of many places — the land taxes of Australia and Denmark, the split-rate levies of Pennsylvania towns, the public land-leasing of Hong Kong and Singapore, the rating of unimproved value across the Anglophone world — and into the mainstream of politics, in Churchill's famous orations against the unearned increment.12

The single tax, in its pure and total form, was adopted nowhere, and for the reasons the last movement gave. But that is the wrong measure of George. His enduring bequest is not the programme but the insight beneath it: that there exists a kind of income — the rent of scarce, unmade things — that is at once unearned and, uniquely, taxable without harm, so that here, if nowhere else, justice and efficiency point the same way. That insight did not die with the single-tax clubs. It is the hinge of this volume's middle, the key the ninth plate will turn, and the lens through which its last plates read an economy in which the rent of land — and of much that has come to behave like land — quietly dominates. Ricardo found the mechanism; George found the question and the remedy; and though the remedy overreached, the question he forced — by what right is the increment of the many kept by the few? — has never been answered, only postponed. The plates that follow take up the postponement.13

The increment is the community's, and the title-holder's, and the contest between those two claims is as old as property in land and as current as the price of a house. A lot grows dearer because a city grows around it; the growth is everyone's work and the lot is one man's possession, and the law, for now, gives the man the growth. George thought this the central injustice of the modern world, and spent his life trying to undo it with a single surgical tax that would take the increment and leave all else untouched. He failed, as totalising remedies do. But he saw the thing clearly, and named it truly, and the naming outlived the failure: that between what we make and what we merely hold lies a difference on which the whole moral weight of an economy comes to rest — and that the rent of the earth falls, stubbornly, on the wrong side of it.

1Henry George (1839–1897) was born in Philadelphia, left school at fourteen, went to sea, and settled in California as a printer and journalist, where he watched San Francisco convulse with growth during the railroad boom. Progress and Poverty (1879) made him world-famous. “The great enigma of our times” is his own phrase for the paradox of the title.

2The paradox was George's central question: why want persists, and even deepens, amid advancing wealth, and most sharply in the most advanced places. He rejected the Malthusian (overpopulation) and wage-fund explanations then in vogue. The book's full subtitle names the inquiry: “the cause of industrial depressions and of increase of want with increase of wealth.”

3George's resolution runs through Ricardo: progress lifts the demand for land and so its rent, which intercepts the gains, leaving wages and interest no better. He carried the classical analysis of “land monopoly” — already present in Smith, Ricardo, and Mill — to its conclusion, and popularised it with a force the academic economists never matched.

4The term unearned increment was coined by John Stuart Mill, who proposed taxing the future growth of land values for the public benefit — the aim of his Land Tenure Reform Association. George took the concept and radicalised it: from a tax on future increments to the reclamation of the whole rent, present and future alike.

5The moral argument: land is not a product of labour but the gift of nature and the common inheritance of all. George held private property in the fruits of labour to be inviolable — he was vehemently not a socialist, and quarrelled bitterly with those who called him one — but the private appropriation of land rent he regarded as the theft of a value the community alone creates.

6Land speculation and the cycle were George's distinctive addition. The expectation of rising values leads owners to withhold land, forcing production onto poorer ground and depressing wages; and the speculative inflation periodically collapses, dragging the productive economy down with it. He argued the commercial depressions of his century were at bottom land-speculation crises — anticipating, in outline, modern accounts of land and housing bubbles.

7The single tax: George proposed not to nationalise land but to tax away its rent — the value of the bare site, net of improvements — at a rate approaching the whole, abolishing every other tax. He insisted that private possession and the security of improvements be preserved, lest the land's good use, and the incentive to improve it, be destroyed.

8The three claims: just (it returns to the community the value the community created, and taxes nothing anyone made); sufficient (land rent alone could fund the whole state — the “single” claim); and efficient (because land is fixed, the tax falls on a surplus and imposes no deadweight loss, unlike taxes on labour or capital). The efficiency result — that a tax on a perfectly inelastic factor distorts nothing — is a theorem, developed in plate ix.

9On what George got right: the social creation of land value; the unique efficiency of taxing pure land rent (plate ix); and the centrality of land and housing to inequality, a thesis the data of the present century have powerfully revived (plates x, xii). Economists of very different schools have endorsed the efficiency point, from the marginalists to the present day.

10The overreaches: (a) the “single” claim — pure land rent is large but probably insufficient to fund a modern state; (b) the transition, or capitalisation, problem — current owners who bought land at prices already reflecting its rent, frequently with mortgages, would be wiped out by a sudden confiscation, though they never captured the original increment; (c) the standing difficulty of assessment, separating the value of bare land from the improvements upon it (cf. plates i, ii). These are the boundaries of a true idea, not its refutation; plates ix and x return to them.

11Progress and Poverty sold several million copies — some estimates put it near six million — in many translations, and by some accounts was outsold in English in the 1890s by no book but the Bible; its admirers ran from George Bernard Shaw, who credited it with his conversion, to Einstein. Lizzie Magie's 1904 board game, The Landlord's Game — patented to demonstrate Georgist ideas and the workings of rent — is the direct ancestor of Monopoly.

12Land-value taxation entered the law in diluted forms — in Australia and Denmark, in the split-rate property levies of Pennsylvania cities, in the public land-leasing systems of Hong Kong and Singapore, and in the rating of unimproved value across the Anglophone world. Winston Churchill, during the “People's Budget” battles of 1909, delivered celebrated speeches against the unearned increment and the land monopoly, carrying the argument into front-rank politics.

13The pure single tax was adopted nowhere, but the recognition that rent can be taxed without harm — that for this one kind of income justice and efficiency coincide — survives, and frames the volume's middle and its end (plates ix, x, xii). The third plate of The Rent — the moral case, and the volume's turn from mechanism to justice. It then bounds the argument where it overreaches, and traces its remarkable afterlife.
