Plate the Tenth, of twelve

The Toll

The plate that grants the oldest suspicion its due. Through nine plates the volume has defended the middleman, insisting he mostly earns his cut; this one isolates the case it has kept circling — the cut that answers to no service at all, but only to a chokepoint seized and held. The toll-keeper of the one bridge, the holder of the patent all must use, the controller of the permission all must buy: the rentier of the passage, whose charge reflects not a service he renders, for he renders none, nor a risk he bears, for he bears none, but only the bare fact of his position. His take is no wage but a rent — the landlord's claim transposed from the land to the passage — and here, for the first time, the parasite charge that opened the volume is simply correct. Yet even here three scruples hold: that the perfectly pure toll is rare, that not every toll is waste, and that the toll's very reality is what vindicated the labour of telling the wage from it.

in print · published

For nine plates this volume has argued, with what patience it could muster, on the middleman's behalf. Against the immemorial charge that he is a parasite who produces nothing and merely skims the labour of others, it has shown him bridging gaps of space and search and trust, bearing risks no one else would bear, monitoring borrowers no saver could watch, reconciling prices no one else would reconcile, building markets that would not otherwise exist — and it has insisted, plate after plate, that his cut is mostly the honest wage of a real and difficult service. This plate is where the volume keeps its faith with the other side of the case. For there is a kind of middleman to whom none of that defence applies, whose cut answers to no service at all, and in whom the ancient suspicion is not a slander but a plain description. He is the rentier of the passage — the man who holds a chokepoint and charges for the right to pass through it, and who does nothing else whatever. He has built nothing, or built it once and long ago; he bears no risk, for his position is secure; he renders no service, for the thing would pass as well or better without him. He simply stands in the way, where there is no way around, and levies. His cut is the toll, and it is the one price in this volume that is extraction unmixed with service — the pure form of the thing every other plate has had to separate, with such labour, from its honest admixture.

The toll is as old as trade, and its emblem is the chokepoint: the single bridge over the river that must be crossed, the one pass through the mountains the road must take, the strait that every ship must thread, the gate in the wall through which all goods must enter. Whoever holds such a point holds everyone who must pass it, and can charge them what the passage is worth to them rather than what it costs him to permit it — which is to say, can charge nearly anything, for a traveller who must cross will pay a great deal rather than not cross at all. The robber baron on the Rhine who strung his chain across the river and taxed the boats; the sovereign who levied the Sound Dues on every vessel entering the Baltic; the lord whose castle commanded the only ford for a hundred miles — these are the toll-keepers of history, and what unites them is that they sold nothing. They did not make the river passable or the cargo more valuable; they found a place where passage was unavoidable and inserted themselves into it, and their wealth was the measure not of any service they performed but of the sheer inescapability of their position. The toll is the price of a chokepoint held, and the chokepoint-holder is the limiting case of the middleman: one who has reduced the business of standing between to its barest essence, the levy without the service, the cut without the bridge.

The right name for what the toll-keeper collects is not wage but rent, and the distinction is the hinge of this plate. A wage, in the broad sense this volume has used, is the return to a service performed — to labour done, to a risk borne, to a function discharged; it is earned, in the plain sense that something was given in exchange for it. A rent is the return to the mere control of a scarce and necessary thing — the payment one can exact simply because one holds what others need and they cannot get it elsewhere; it is owed to no labour and no risk, but only to the position. The landlord's rent is the model: he does not work the land or improve it or bear its risks, yet he is paid for the use of it, because it is scarce and he controls it and the tenant must have it. And the toll-keeper's take is rent of exactly this kind, with the passage standing where the land stands — the return to the control of a scarce and necessary point, exacted from those who must pass and cannot go around, owed to nothing the toll-keeper does but only to the chokepoint he holds. The middleman, in the toll, has stopped being a worker of any sort and become a landlord, and his cut has stopped being a wage of any sort and become a rent: the same claim the owner of a scarce thing has always made on those who need it, transposed from the field to the road, from the soil to the strait.

His take is no wage but a rent — owed to no labour and no risk, but only to the chokepoint he holds; the landlord's claim transposed from the field to the road, from the soil to the strait.

The chokepoint did not die with the river-barons; it changed its dress. The modern toll-keeper rarely holds a literal bridge, but he holds the functional equivalent — a point of unavoidable passage in some flow of value, into which he has inserted a levy. The patent troll is the clearest case: he acquires a patent, often one he had no hand in inventing, on some technique that others must use, and then charges them for the use of it — not because he contributed the invention, which he bought from someone else or extracted from a failed firm, but because he now controls a thing the others cannot design around, and can hold up their whole enterprise until they pay. The owner of a standard-essential patent does the same with more leverage still, for a technology written into a standard is one that everyone in an industry must use to be compatible, and the holder of a patent upon it commands a chokepoint that an entire market must pass. The controller of a sole necessary input, the holder of a license others must buy, the official whose permission cannot be gone around, the firm that has quietly made itself the one channel through which some transaction must flow — all are toll-keepers in the modern manner, and all are paid the same way the river-baron was paid: not for a service rendered but for a passage controlled, the cut levied on a flow they did not create and do not improve but merely command.

It is here, and only here, that the charge the volume opened with comes fully true. The suspicion of the middleman as parasite, which the first plate named as the oldest in commerce and which the nine plates since have laboured to qualify, is in the toll-keeper no longer a suspicion to be qualified but a fact to be conceded. This middleman really does merely levy. He produces nothing, bears nothing, reconciles nothing, builds nothing that he is still building; he occupies a point others must pass and takes a cut for letting them pass it, and the whole of his economic function, if it can be called one, is the holding of the position. The volume's animating question — when does the middleman earn his cut, and when does he merely levy it? — has wandered, through all the prior plates, among cases where the answer was mixed and the proportions hidden, where the cut was part wage and part toll and the number would not say how much of each. In the toll, for once, the answer is clean: it is all toll. This is the extraction end of the volume's seam reached at last in its pure form, the cut that is levy and nothing but, the case the whole investigation has been circling and against which every other price has had, implicitly, to be measured. The parasite charge, wrong about the dealer and the bank and the arbitrageur and even, in its honest half, the platform, is right about the toll-keeper, and the volume owes it that admission.

The parasite charge, wrong about the dealer and the bank and the arbitrageur, is right about the toll-keeper: this middleman really does merely levy, and his cut is all toll, and nothing else.

And yet the volume cannot let even this admission stand without its scruples, for the toll, examined closely, is less pure and less simple than the indictment wants it to be. Two qualifications press in. The first is that the perfectly pure toll — the cut with not a single particle of service in it — is an ideal more often approached than reached. The bridge, even the inherited one, must be kept up if it is to be crossed; the patent, even in the troll's hands, did once reward an act of invention, whatever became of the inventor; the chokepoint-holder commonly performs some sliver of genuine function alongside his levy, so that most real tolls are mixtures too, distinguished from the honest spreads of earlier plates not by containing no wage at all but by the toll's having grown to dominate and dwarf whatever wage is buried in them. The second qualification is that not every toll is mere waste. A charge for crossing a congested bridge can ration a scarce capacity that would otherwise be squandered, sending across only those who value the crossing most; a patent, even when it falls at last into a non-inventor's hands, sustains the market in which inventions can be bought and sold, and so underwrites the inventing in the first place; a fee for a scarce and genuinely limited passage may allocate it better than a free-for-all would. The line between the toll that merely extracts and the toll that actually allocates is real, and contested, and not to be drawn by the mere fact that a cut is taken. Even the rentier of the passage, it turns out, is not always quite the pure parasite the oldest charge imagines.

There is a third scruple, and it is the deepest, for it turns the toll from an embarrassment to the volume's argument into the very thing that vindicates it. Why, after all, did the wage have to be so painstakingly distinguished from the toll, plate after plate, with such insistence that the dealer's spread and the bank's margin and the platform's cut each held some honest service the parasite charge overlooked? Precisely because the toll is real. If there were no such thing as the pure levy, no genuine rentier of the passage, then the suspicion of the middleman would be simple paranoia, and the volume's careful defence of him a labour against a phantom. It is because the toll exists — because some middlemen really do merely levy — that the others must be defended rather than simply assumed innocent, and that the cut alone can never settle the matter. The toll is the reason the seam runs where it does; it is the extraction against which the wage is defined, the real and present possibility that makes the question worth asking at all. So the volume's verdict on the toll is not that it is an exception to be explained away, but that it is the pole the whole inquiry has been oriented toward. The middleman's cut is sometimes the wage of a service and sometimes the toll of a position, and the two wear, maddeningly, the same face; and the toll-keeper is the proof that the second is no fantasy — that behind the suspicion the volume has spent itself qualifying there stands, sometimes, a real parasite, charging for a passage he does nothing to deserve.

The toll, then, is the volume's pure case of the thing it has everywhere else found impurely mixed: the cut that is rent and not wage, levy and not service, the price of a chokepoint held by one who does nothing but hold it. In the toll-keeper the middleman ceases altogether to be a worker and becomes a landlord, and his take ceases to be earned in any sense and becomes the rent of a guarded position — the same return that the owner of any scarce and necessary thing has always commanded from those who need it and cannot do without. That return, in its own right and at its full extent, is the subject of another volume entirely, the one that anatomizes the rent of mere control wherever it appears, whether the thing controlled is land or a passage or a patent or a name; and the bridge from this plate runs there, where the toll-keeper takes his place beside the landlord, the monopolist, and the rent-seeker as a collector of the one kind of due that is owed to position alone. One plate of the volume now remains. Having traced the middleman from his honest services to his pure extraction, it turns to the recurring dream that would be rid of him entirely — the standing promise, made in every age and never quite kept, to cut the middleman out, to let the two sides meet without the one in between, and so to keep for them the cut that he, deserving or not, has always taken. As the table says of every price, first to last: the argument remains open.
