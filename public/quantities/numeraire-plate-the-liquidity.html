Plate the Fourth, of twelve

The Liquidity

The first and broadest face of the spread: immediacy itself, the right to trade now — to turn an asset into cash the instant one needs it, at a price near its worth. What liquidity is, in its dimensions of tightness, depth, and resilience; the liquidity premium, by which the world pays for it a second time, in the lower return it accepts on what it can sell at once; and the two shadows that fall across this, the most genuinely earned of the dealer's services — its fragility, for liquidity is abundant in the calm and gone in the storm, and its tendency to pool, for liquidity gathers where liquidity already is.

in print · published

The plate before this opened the dealer's gap and found three things folded inside it; this plate draws out the first and broadest of them, the one that gives the dealer his reason for being. It is immediacy — the bare ability to trade at once, this instant, the moment one wishes — and the deeper thing that ability is a piece of, which the markets call liquidity. To say an asset is liquid is to say it can be turned into cash quickly, in quantity, at small cost, and at a price near its true worth; to say it is illiquid is to say that selling it takes time, or a discount, or both, and that a hurried sale will fetch far less than a patient one. Cash itself is perfectly liquid; a great company's shares or a government's bonds are nearly so; a house, a private business, a thin and seldom-traded stock are not. The dealer's whole trade is the manufacture and sale of this liquidity: he stands ready to buy what you must sell and to sell what you must buy, so that you may trade the instant you wish rather than wait for the other side to appear. The bid-ask spread of the last plate is simply the retail price of that service for a small and ordinary trade. But liquidity runs deeper than any single spread, is paid for in more places than the dealer's window, and behaves, in the moments that matter most, in ways that make it at once the most genuinely valuable of the dealer's services and the most treacherous.

Liquidity is not one quality but several, and it is worth separating them, for a market may be liquid in one sense and illiquid in another. There is tightness, which is the narrowness of the spread — how little one loses on the round trip between buying and selling, and the dimension the last plate measured. There is depth, which is how much one can trade before the price itself begins to move — a deep market swallows a large order without flinching, a shallow one lurches away from it. There is resilience, which is how quickly the price returns to where it was after a large trade has pushed it — how fast the market heals. And there is immediacy proper, the sheer speed with which a trade can be done at all. A market that is tight, deep, resilient, and immediate is liquid in the full sense: one can trade much, fast, cheaply, and without moving the price against oneself. The spread captures only the first of these, and only for the small trade; the buyer or seller who must move a great quantity at once discovers the others, for his own order, pressing through the available bids or offers, walks the price away from him as it goes, and the distance it walks — the price impact of his trade — is the part of liquidity's cost that no posted spread reveals. Liquidity, then, is a richer and more variable thing than the dealer's two quotes let on, and its full price is felt most by those who need the most of it.

For liquidity, like any service, has a price, and the price rises with the quantity and the haste of what one demands. The small and patient trader pays almost nothing: he can place a modest order and let it rest until the other side comes to him, paying neither spread nor impact. The ordinary impatient trader pays the spread, crossing at once to the dealer's quote rather than waiting. And the trader who must move a great block immediately pays the most of all, for his own demand for liquidity exhausts what is cheaply available and forces him deeper into the book, each successive share bought a little dearer and each sold a little cheaper than the last, until the price he achieves on the whole is well away from where it stood when he began. This is the iron logic of liquidity's price: it is cheap in small sips and dear in great gulps, cheap to the patient and dear to the hurried, so that the very act of demanding much liquidity at once is what makes it expensive. The dealer, and behind him the whole apparatus of the market, supplies this liquidity and is paid for it in exact proportion to how much of it, and how urgently, each trader takes — which is as it should be, for the readiness to absorb a large and sudden order is precisely the costliest readiness to maintain.

Liquidity is cheap in small sips and dear in great gulps; the patient pay almost nothing for it, and the trader who must move much at once pays the price his own haste moves against him.

But the price of liquidity is paid in a second place, far from the dealer's window, and this second payment is the truer measure of how much liquidity is worth. For an asset that is hard to sell — that trades thinly, that cannot be turned to cash without a wait or a discount — is, for that reason alone, worth less to hold, since its owner may someday need to sell it in a hurry and suffer for the haste. Investors, knowing this, will not hold an illiquid asset unless it compensates them for the disadvantage, and it compensates them in the only coin an asset has: a lower price, and so a higher expected return. The illiquid asset must offer a richer yield than an otherwise identical liquid one, and the difference — the liquidity premium — is the market's standing price for the quality of being easy to sell. It is everywhere once one looks for it: in the lower yield of the freshly issued, heavily traded government bond against its older, less-traded twin of identical maturity; in the discount at which a stake in a private company changes hands against a comparable public one; in the extra return demanded of any asset that locks its holder in. Liquidity is thus priced twice over — once as a fee, in the spread paid to trade, and again as a yield, in the return foregone to hold the liquid — and the size of that second, quieter payment, spread across every asset in the world, is the great proof that liquidity is no contrivance of the dealer's but a thing the market profoundly values.

Of all the services this volume examines, then, liquidity has the strongest claim to be genuinely earned, for the market itself attests its worth more loudly than for any other. The world does not merely tolerate the price of liquidity; it pays that price twice, willingly, and the doubling is its confession that the service is real and dearly wanted. And the price is, in the purest sense, optional. The patient investor who is content to hold and to wait can decline to pay for liquidity altogether — can buy the illiquid asset, pocket its premium, and trade slowly when he trades at all — while the impatient one who insists on the power to sell at any moment pays for that power in spread and in foregone yield, and counts it worth the cost. No one is forced to buy liquidity who does not value it; everyone who buys it reveals, by buying, that he values it more than its price. To this extent the dealer who manufactures liquidity earns his spread as cleanly as any producer earns the price of his good, for he supplies, at a cost the buyer freely accepts, a thing the buyer demonstrably wants: the standing assurance that he may turn his holdings into money the instant he wishes, instead of waiting, exposed, for a buyer who may be slow to come.

The world pays for liquidity twice — once in the spread, to trade it, and again in the price of every asset, to hold the liquid rather than the illiquid — and that it pays twice, willingly, is the measure of how real the service is.

And yet two shadows fall across this, the most earned of services, that fall across no other in the volume — and the first and graver is that liquidity is treacherous, present in abundance when it is least needed and absent when it is needed most. In calm markets liquidity is everywhere: dealers quote tight and deep, large orders are absorbed without a tremor, and the assurance that one can sell at any moment seems a permanent feature of the world. But let fear enter — let prices fall sharply, let everyone wish to sell at once — and the liquidity evaporates exactly when it is wanted. The dealers widen their spreads or withdraw their quotes; the depth that swallowed large orders in the calm vanishes; the patient buyers who once stood ready are gone; and the seller who must sell finds himself dumping into a market with no bottom, at fire-sale prices, his very selling driving the price down faster as he goes. Liquidity, it turns out, is procyclical: it floods in when the seas are smooth and drains away in the storm, a fair-weather friend that deserts precisely in the foul weather that tests it. And because so much of finance is built on the silent assumption that one can always sell — that positions can be unwound, that collateral can be turned to cash, that the exit is open — the disappearance of liquidity in a crisis is not a private misfortune but a systemic one, in which each seller's flight worsens the market into which the next must flee. The dealer is paid, in the long calm, for supplying a liquidity that fails in the one moment it is most needed; and that failure is no accident of his but the nature of the thing he sells.

The second shadow is the one the volume has met before, and will meet again: that liquidity concentrates, pooling where it already is. A market that is deep draws the traders who want depth, and their coming makes it deeper still, until liquidity gathers in a single venue or a single dealer by the same self-feeding logic that gathered the matchmaker's crowd. And the provider in whom liquidity has so concentrated holds, once more, the power of the indispensable: traders come to him not because his price is keenest but because his is the only pool deep enough to trade in, and he may charge for that depth what the want of any alternative will bear. So even liquidity, the most honestly earned of the dealer's services, arrives trailing the volume's standing question. Most of its price is a true wage — the genuine, doubly-attested value of being able to trade at once, which the patient may decline and the impatient gladly pay. But part may be the toll of a pool grown too dominant to bypass, and part is owed to a service that is reliable only so long as it is not truly tested. Liquidity is thus the dealer's finest offering and his most flattering, promising an ease of exit that is real in the calm, dear when concentrated, and apt to fail in the storm — a wage, mostly, but a wage shadowed by an illusion and a power.

At its root, the thing the dealer sells under the name of liquidity is a likeness of money. For money is simply the perfectly liquid asset — the one thing that need never be sold, because it is already what everyone will take, exchangeable for anything at any moment at no cost at all. Every other asset is more or less liquid as it is more or less like money in this respect, and the price of liquidity is, at the last, the price of being nearly as good as money without quite being it: the spread one pays to convert into money now, the premium one foregoes to hold what is close to money already. The demand for liquidity is therefore the demand to hold money itself, the very hunger the price of time knew as liquidity preference, and the rate of interest — there understood as the reward for parting with money — is only the price of liquidity seen from the other side. The matter belongs, in the end, to the volume on money, where the most liquid asset of all is examined in its own right. The next plate turns from the immediacy the dealer sells to the inventory he is left holding when he sells it — the second face of the gap, and the risk the dealer bears in carrying the thing while he waits for the other side to come.
