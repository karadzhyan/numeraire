The argument of the group

Every volume so far has rested on a single assumption it rarely paused to examine: that the human being at the centre of it all is rational — that people know what they want, rank it without contradiction, and choose what serves them best. The prices were verdicts such agents reached; the quantities were what they optimized; the failures were the traps that caught them; the institutions were the structures they raised; the aggregates were their sum. This last group is where the assumption itself is finally brought under the lamp — and where economics, having built so much upon a stylised human being, turns to meet the actual one.

The four volumes are the four faces of that reckoning. The Choice states the assumption and tests it at its own joints — the slender axioms of rational preference, and the seams, from uncertainty to bounded cognition to the social self, where they begin to give. The Bias falsifies it, setting down the systematic, predictable, repeatable ways the real mind departs from the model: the anchor that drags, the loss that looms too large, the present that swells out of proportion. The Game makes it interactive, asking what "rational" can even mean once your best move depends on everyone else's, and how, against the cold logic of the lone optimizer, cooperation ever holds. And The Norm transcends it, naming what binds every market together that no price sets and no contract writes.

The honest finding that orders the group is a paradox the discipline has learned to live inside: the rational-actor model is at once the most powerful instrument in all of social science and plainly false as a portrait of any living person. It earns its keep by predicting, not by being true. And the deepest of these unwritten rules — the one the final volume ends upon, and the one this whole table was named to seek — is honesty itself: the quiet expectation, beneath every exchange, that others will by and large tell the truth and keep their word. A numéraire of honesty is not, in the end, a unit of measure at all. It is the oldest and least written of the norms — the one that makes a market, an argument, and a table such as this one, possible at all.

The sixth group — the behavioral foundations, brought from sketch to structure. The Choice, The Bias, The Game, The Norm — each now set in twelve plates, none yet in print; Volume I, The Wage, still holds that distinction. Together they turn the discipline's scrutiny upon its own first assumption — that the chooser is rational — and end, in the final plate, where the whole project began: with honesty itself, the least written of all the rules, named at last as the very numéraire the table set out to find. Of the six families, one now remains unbuilt — The Prices, where the table opened and where The Wage already stands; with it, the circle closes. Set in Fraunces and Newsreader. The argument remains open.
