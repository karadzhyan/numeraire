The Black Swan

Nassim Taleb's rare, high-impact, unforeseeable event — the catastrophe that no induction from a calm past prepares us for, and that the bell curve, on which so much of finance is built, banishes to a tail too thin to fear. Why the real world keeps its disasters in a fatter tail than the model allows; why a premium computed from that tame curve is set too low for the wild risk it must price; and why, in the long calm between catastrophes, the reckless and the prudent wear the same steady returns.

in print · published

The previous plate found in uncertainty a generative power — the unforeseeable opportunity that, borne by someone willing, brings new things into the world and earns, when it is read aright, the noblest wage of all. This plate turns the same unforeseeability over and looks at its other face, which is not generative but annihilating: the rare, sudden, devastating event that no study of a calm past prepares anyone for. Nassim Taleb gave it a name drawn from an old certainty overturned — for Europeans were sure, on the evidence of every swan they had ever seen, that all swans were white, until the discovery of black ones in the south destroyed in an instant a belief that millennia of confirmation had built. A black swan, in his sense, is an event of just that kind: an outlier far beyond the reach of ordinary expectation, of extreme and often catastrophic impact, and seeming, only after it has happened, to have been foreseeable all along. The trouble such events make for the price of risk is profound, and it is the burden of this plate. For the mathematics by which finance measures and prices risk is built, very largely, upon a curve — the bell curve, the Gaussian — that banishes the catastrophe to a tail so thin it may be safely ignored; while the actual world, as this plate will show, keeps its catastrophes in a tail far fatter, so that the premium computed from the tame curve is set too low for the wild risk it is meant to cover, and those who draw that premium in the long quiet between disasters cannot be told, until the disaster comes, from men picking coins from the path of a steamroller they have not yet seen.

The deepest root of the difficulty is older than finance, and Taleb traces it to the philosophers' ancient problem of induction: that no number of confirming instances can ever establish a general truth, for a single counterinstance may always lie in wait. The turkey, fed kindly by the same hand every morning, grows more confident with each passing day that the hand means it well — and is most confident of all on the morning before the feast for which it has, all along, been fattened. So with the holder of any belief drawn from a run of calm experience: the very length of the calm, which feels like reassurance, is quietly building the false confidence that the catastrophe will exploit. A black swan is precisely the event that the past not only fails to predict but actively conceals, by lulling the observer into mistaking the absence of disaster for its impossibility. And because such events are, by their nature, absent from the record until they strike, every method that learns the size of a risk from its past — every model fitted to history, every premium calibrated on the years we happen to have lived through — is blind in exactly the place where the danger is greatest. The black swan is not merely unlikely; it is unprovided for, and unprovided for by the very habits of inference that feel the most prudent.

That blindness takes a precise mathematical form, and naming it is the heart of the matter. The workhorse of quantitative finance is the normal distribution, the bell curve, which describes a world where outcomes cluster tightly about their average and grow vanishingly improbable as they depart from it — a world in which an extreme event is not merely rare but, for any truly large extreme, effectively impossible. For some quantities this is a faithful picture: human heights cluster so, and no man is a hundred times the average height. But the quantities that matter most in economic life — the movements of markets, the sizes of fortunes, the losses in a crisis — do not cluster so at all. They follow what the mathematicians call fat-tailed, or power-law, distributions, in which the extreme, though rare, is enormously more probable than the bell curve allows, and a single observation can dwarf the sum of all the others. Mandelbrot saw this in the prices of cotton half a century ago, and it has been confirmed across economics again and again: the world of prices is not the mild domain where the bell curve reigns, but a wild one in which the rare event is no negligible footnote but the main event, the thing that, over a long enough span, comes to determine almost everything. To model such a world with the bell curve is not a small error of degree; it is to search for the catastrophe in the one place it has agreed never to be.

The bell curve banishes the catastrophe to a tail too thin to fear; the world keeps its catastrophes in a tail so fat they come to rule everything.

The consequence for the price of risk is direct and grave. A premium, or any measure of risk built upon the Gaussian, will be set far too low, for it has counted the catastrophic tail as nearly weightless when in truth that tail carries much of the risk there is. The point is plainest in the risk measures the banks themselves came to rely on, which promised, with comforting precision, that losses would not exceed some stated figure except on a tiny fraction of days — a promise that quietly assumed away the very days that mattered, since the rare day on which the loss runs far past the stated figure is exactly the black swan, and exactly the day the measure was powerless to foresee. The world has furnished cruel illustrations. In the years before the crisis of 2008, the instruments that bundled mortgage debt were valued by a formula that treated the chance of many borrowers defaulting together as a tidy, bell-shaped, and reassuringly small thing; the formula held beautifully in the calm, and then the calm ended, the defaults came together exactly as the formula had ruled they could not, and the catastrophe it had priced away arrived in full. A premium computed in this fashion is not a fair price for catastrophe insurance; it is a fair price for the catastrophe-free world the model imagines, sold into a world that is nothing of the kind.

And here the volume's question takes a sharp and troubling turn, for consider what such mispricing does to the very appearance of skill. A man who sells insurance against catastrophe — who takes on, for a steady stream of small premiums, the obligation to pay enormously should the rare disaster strike — will, in every year that the disaster does not strike, show a tidy profit and a placid record. He collects, and collects, and pays nothing out; his returns are high and, by every conventional measure the bell curve informs, his risk appears low. Year after calm year he looks like a genius, and is paid and praised as one. But his profit is borrowed, every penny of it, against the day the disaster comes — and when it comes, as in a fat-tailed world it eventually must, it takes back not only the years of premiums but very often the whole of his capital besides. He has been, in Taleb's mordant image, picking up pennies in front of a steamroller: the pennies are real and the gait is jaunty, right up until the instant it is not. The fearsome thing is that, until that instant, his record is indistinguishable from genuine skill — and the survivors we crown in any given year are, disproportionately, exactly those who have taken on such hidden catastrophe-risk and not yet been made to pay for it.

He who sells catastrophe insurance and collects the steady premium looks, year upon calm year, like a genius — until the year he does not.

None of this is to say that no premium for catastrophe is honestly earned, and the volume must be careful here, for the truth cuts both ways. The catastrophe is real; the risk of it is real; and someone who genuinely bears that risk — who holds the assets that will be ruined in the crash, knowing they may be, and does not merely pretend the tail away — has borne something heavy and deserves a reward for it. Indeed the fat tail supplies the most powerful version of the rational rescue the puzzle plate examined: if real catastrophes lurk in the tail, then the large premium on risky assets is no anomaly but the fair price of a genuine, if rarely realized, danger, and the calm decades merely a stretch in which the danger has stayed its hand. The difficulty is not that the premium is always unearned; it is that the earned premium and the borrowed one wear, in the calm years, precisely the same face. The prudent bearer of real catastrophe-risk and the reckless seller of underpriced disaster insurance both show steady gains and placid records; nothing whatever in the returns of the quiet years distinguishes the one who has soberly priced the tail from the one who has merely been lucky that it has not yet arrived. The skill is real, and the luck is real, and they are, until the swan descends, wholly indistinguishable — which is the precise predicament the next plate must confront.

Set beside the puzzle plate, the black swan completes a strange and double indictment of the price of risk. The puzzle showed the premium to be too large — far larger than any sober weighing of ordinary, measurable risk could justify. The black swan shows it, at the same time, to be too small — far smaller than the true weight of the catastrophic tail demands, because the curve that sets it cannot see that tail at all. The two charges do not contradict each other, for they concern different parts of the distribution: the premium is too large to be the cool wage of the moderate ups and downs the bell curve does describe, and too small to be a fair price for the wild extremes the bell curve does not. The price of risk, in short, is mismeasured from both ends at once — swollen by a fear the moderate hazard does not warrant, and starved against a catastrophe the model has hidden. And the reward drawn from it in the long calm is, for all who have not genuinely provided against the tail, partly an advance against a reckoning not yet come: not the clean wage of risk well borne that the early plates described, but something closer to the borrowed winnings of a survivor whose luck has simply not yet run out. The confident accounting of the premium as desert, already cracked by the puzzle, the swan breaks further: the market neither prices risk correctly nor pays it out to the deserving in any manner the calm years can reveal.

So the black swan leaves the price of risk doubly wrong — too large for the moderate hazard, too small for the catastrophic one — and leaves the reward drawn from it, in the quiet between disasters, impossible to tell from the borrowed luck of a survivor not yet struck. One large question it raises it cannot itself answer, and it is the question that carries the volume toward its companions. Are these catastrophes truly black swans — exogenous, unforeseeable, arriving from outside the system like a meteor from a clear sky? Or are they something less innocent: the recurring crises that a financial economy breeds within itself, the long calm itself encouraging the leverage and the daring that guarantee the eventual collapse, so that the disaster is not a bolt from the blue but the predictable ripening of the system's own excesses? The long history of financial folly — crisis following crisis down the centuries with a dreary regularity — suggests the catastrophes are less unforeseeable than the word swan implies, that stability is the very thing that breeds instability, and the crash is written into the boom. That is the argument the bridge to the cycle takes up, and it belongs to volumes still to come. What this volume must now do is face the predicament the swan has sharpened to its point: that in a world where the unlucky vanish and only the survivors remain to be counted, skill and luck grow desperately, perhaps fatally, hard to tell apart. As the table says of every price, first to last: the argument remains open.

  2. Benoît B. Mandelbrot, “The Variation of Certain Speculative Prices” (1963) — fat tails in market prices, the first reckoning.
  3. David Hume, An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding (1748) — the problem of induction, the swan's philosophical root.

  7. Carmen M. Reinhart & Kenneth S. Rogoff, This Time Is Different: Eight Centuries of Financial Folly (2009) — the long recurrence of the supposedly unforeseeable.
