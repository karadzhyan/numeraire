The argument of the group

A market is not a state of nature. It is an artifact, and it rests on structures the price system cannot supply out of itself — which is what this group is. Where the last group watched the price mechanism fail, this one is what stands in its place, around it, beneath it: the firm, where command takes over because using the market has grown too dear; the contract, the promise that lashes today's bargain to tomorrow's performance, and the law that fills the gaps no promise can foresee; money, the shared confidence that makes any exchange possible at all; the tax, the state's hand reaching into every price; and the rule, the institution that quietly designs all the others.

Each is treated, in the casual telling, as neutral plumbing — and each is nothing of the kind. The firm is a small government, ruling by fiat where the price would otherwise rule. The private contract is enforced by a public court. Money holds its worth only because a people, and a state, have agreed that it shall. A tax settles its weight not where the statute aims but where the market sends it. And the rules of exchange are written by someone, for someone. To name these institutions is already to have left the fairy tale of a market that simply is.

For the deepest point of the group — the one the whole table is named against — is that there is no clearing in the woods, no pre-institutional ground of pure free exchange, against which a meddling can be measured. Property is public law; contract is public law; the firm and the money and the market are, all of them, already institutions. The numéraire of honesty here is to stop pretending the baseline exists — to see that the question was never market or state, but always the harder one the last of these volumes is built to ask: which rules, written by whom, and in whose interest?

Numéraire Group IV · The Institutions

The fourth of the table's six groups, and the largest — five volumes brought from sketch to structure. The Firm, The Contract, The Money, The Tax, The Rule — each now set in twelve plates, none yet in print; Volume I, The Wage, still holds that distinction. Together they are the scaffolding a market cannot raise from within: command where the price is too costly to use, the promise that binds across time, the trust that makes a token worth real goods, the state's reach into every price, and the design of the rules of exchange. The discipline of the group is to remember that none of these is neutral plumbing — each is a seat of power, and the market, looked at closely, is already an institution. Set in Fraunces and Newsreader. The argument remains open.
