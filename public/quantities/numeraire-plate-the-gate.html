Plate the Twelfth, of twelve

The Gate

The platform and its toll: the digital landlord who owns the marketplace all must pass through, and levies a fee on every transaction that flows. The volume's culmination — location, agglomeration, monopoly, and the unearned increment gathered at a single gate — and its oldest question, posed of the newest landlord: the fair price of a service, or the rent of a gate that none can go around?

in print · published

The volume began in a field — Ricardo's differential rent, the return to owning the more fertile acre — and it ends at a gate: the digital platform, the marketplace, the network through which the commerce of the age must pass, and whose owner levies a toll upon all who pass. This last plate takes up the platform rent, the toll of the digital landlord; and it finds in it not a new thing but the oldest thing in the volume, made wholly modern. For the platform is the volume's founding emblem made literal — the spot that others must pass through — and its toll gathers into one all the rents the volume has anatomized: the rent of location, of agglomeration, of monopoly, of the unearned increment, every one of them collected at the gate of the marketplace by whoever happens to own it. The plate will show the platform as the new landlord; trace its toll to the network effects that make its gate unavoidable; and arrive, at the volume's close, at the volume's question — posed now of the most modern rent, and answered no more finally than it was of the most ancient. For the platform, like the field, earns the value of what it provides and extracts the rent of the spot it holds, and where the line falls between them is, here as everywhere, the contest the price conceals.

Begin with what the platform is. To reach the customer, the seller must now, more and more, pass through a platform — the great marketplace where the buyers gather, the store through which the software must be sold, the search box through which the seeker finds his way, the network through which the payment clears. The platform owns the passage: it is the gate between the buyer and the seller, the chokepoint through which the transaction must flow. And like every owner of a passage the world is obliged to use, it levies a toll — the commission on the sale, the cut of the fare, the fee upon the transaction, the price of the advertisement that buys a place in the buyer's eye. The platform is the landlord of the digital marketplace, and its fee is the rent of the digital location, the price of standing where the customers already are. The volume opened with the rent of the field and the rent of the corner lot; it closes with the rent of the gate — which is the very same rent, levied now upon the commerce that must pass through a privately owned door.

But why must the commerce pass through this gate, and not another, or none? The answer is the force that built the cities, returned in digital dress: the network effect. A marketplace is worth using in proportion to how many others use it — the buyers go where the sellers are, the sellers go where the buyers are — and so the platform that has already gathered the most users is, for that very reason, the most valuable to join, and gathers still more. The pooling feeds upon itself, exactly as the agglomeration plate described, until one platform stands where all must come: the dominant marketplace, the universal search, the one store for the one kind of phone. This is the agglomeration of the digital age, value pooling where value already is; and it has the agglomeration's old consequence, that the pool, once formed, cannot easily be gone around, for to leave the platform is to leave behind everyone else who is on it. The gate is unavoidable not because any law compels its use, but because everyone else is already through it; and the network effect, which is only the agglomeration's externality in modern dress, is precisely what hands the platform the power to charge.

And here the platform reveals itself as the volume's whole argument gathered into a single figure. For the toll it levies is, at one and the same time, every rent the volume has named. It is a location rent, the price of the digital nearness to the customer, the platform the central spot and the seller paying to stand upon it. It is an agglomeration rent, the capitalized worth of the network the users themselves created merely by being there — made by all, as the seventh plate had it, and owned by one. It is a monopoly rent, for the network effect that makes the gate unavoidable is a barrier no rival can easily surmount, and the platform, secure behind it, may set its toll as a gatekeeper sets it, above what open competition would ever allow. And it is an unearned increment, for the value the platform taxes — the thronging marketplace, the trusted reviews, the network worth the joining — is very largely the creation of the buyers and sellers who use it, captured by the owner of the gate exactly as the urban landlord captures the value of the city his tenants built. The platform rent is location and agglomeration and monopoly and the unearned increment, all at once, levied at a single gate: the volume's whole bestiary of rents, discovered together in the newest landlord of them all.

And yet — for the volume's scruple must hold even here, at its very end, and hold doubly — the platform is no mere toll-collector, and its fee is not all of it extraction. The platform does real and valuable work, work that did not exist to be done before it came. It matches, bringing together the buyer and the seller who would never otherwise have found one another, dissolving the search frictions that the wage volume found at the root of so much loss. It provides trust — the reviews, the ratings, the guaranteed payment, the recourse against the cheat — that lets strangers transact who could never otherwise have dared. It builds infrastructure, the marketplace and the logistics and the payment rails, at great expense and greater risk. And it offers reach, access at a single stroke to the whole vast network, which no seller could ever assemble alone. These are genuine services, and great ones; they lower the costs of trade, and bring into being exchanges that would never have occurred at all; and for them the platform earns, justly, a return. The toll is, in real and large part, the earned price of a service that makes the marketplace possible — and any honest reckoning of the platform must begin by granting that the gate is also a genuine convenience, and that much of what passes through it could not have passed at all without it.

But the toll is not only the price of the service, and there exactly is the trouble. For once the platform is dominant — once the network effect has made its gate unavoidable and the users are locked within it, unable to leave because all the others remain — it may charge not what its service is worth but what its position permits, lifting the toll above the value of the matching and the trust and the reach, into the pure rent of the gate. The thirty in the hundred of the app store, the swelling fees of the marketplace, the rising price of the advertisement a seller must now buy merely to be seen where once a listing sufficed: these are the subjects of the age's fiercest disputes precisely because no one can easily say how much is the fair price of the service and how much the monopoly rent of the gatekeeper. The platform sets the rules of its own marketplace, and may bend them to its favour — preferring its own wares, demoting the rival, taxing the very seller against whom it has chosen to compete. It captures the unearned increment, the value its users make, and captures the more of it the more securely the gate is held. The platform's toll is earned up to the worth of what it provides, and extracted beyond — and the beyond grows with the gate's dominion, until the convenience that began as a genuine service hardens, if competition does not check it, into the tollgate of a private lord across a road the public has no other way to travel.

Where, then, does the earned end and the extracted begin? It is the central and unsettled question of the platform age, and the plate will not pretend to settle what the courts and the economists cannot. The platforms answer that their fees reflect their value and their vast investment, and that the seller who complains is free, in principle, to sell elsewhere; their critics answer that the freedom is a fiction where the network holds all the customers, and that the fee is the rent of an unavoidable gate. Both are partly right, and the proportion differs from platform to platform and from year to year. Nor is the gate's dominion certain to endure: the network effect that locks a platform in can also, now and again, be overthrown, as a livelier rival or a shift in the ground beneath topples the giant that had seemed unassailable — the deposed social network, the dethroned search of an earlier day, the very medallion dissolved by the application. The gale of creative destruction blows through the digital marketplace as through every other, and the gate that looks eternal may prove, like all such gates, the temporary winner of a contest not yet over. Yet the network effect makes the toppling hard and rare, and the incumbent's advantage vast; and whether the ordinary working of competition suffices to keep the platform's toll honest, or whether the public must intervene — by the antitrust suit, the mandated interoperability, the rule that pries the gate open — is the live political economy of our own moment, and as open as the volume's every other question.

And so the volume ends where it began: at a price that is a verdict on a hidden contest. The field of the first plate and the gate of the last are, the volume has tried to show, the same thing in changing dress — the spot that others must pass through, and the rent its owner draws from their passing. Across the twelve plates the spot has altered its form: the fertile acre, the no-rent margin, the dammed supply, the advantage that time dissolves, the unearned increment, the central location, the agglomerated crowd, the contrived monopoly, the rent that men waste their lives pursuing, the finite seam, the scarcity decreed, and now the digital gate. But the question has held its shape throughout — whether the return to owning the spot is earned or merely extracted — and so, too, has the answer: that it is neither, purely, but the settlement of a contest between the two, the rent earned up to the value the owner genuinely adds and extracted beyond it to the limit the position permits, with the line between them fixed not by nature nor by justice but by the balance of power and competition and rule that the price so quietly records. The platform is only the latest place to put the question: is the toll the fair price of a service, or the rent of a gate that none can go around? And the volume's last word is the table's first — that every ordinary price is a verdict on a hidden contest, commonly mistaken for a fact of nature; that the contest, in the digital marketplace as in the most ancient field, is real, and is not yet decided. As the table says of every price within it, the argument remains open.
