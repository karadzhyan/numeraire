Volume IV · The Premium · The Prices
Plate the Twelfth, and last

The Fortune

The volume's keystone, and its verdict. How much of any great fortune is the earned wage of courage — of bearing what the cautious will pay to shed, and facing what no one can foresee — and how much the mere prize of having borne uncertainty and survived? The case for the fortune as courage's wage, at full strength; the case against, gathered from the puzzle, the swan, and the survivor; and the honest judgment the volume can finally render — that courage is genuinely in the fortune, nowhere near the whole of it, and, in any single case, sealed beyond our telling from luck.

in print · published

This volume opened with the smallest of observations — that a man offered a certain sum will commonly prefer it to a gamble of the same average, and so reveals himself willing to pay something to be spared uncertainty — and it has climbed, plate by plate, from that modest footing toward the largest question it can ask. The question is the great fortune. When a man amasses, by his ventures in a world of risk, a fortune vast beyond any ordinary wage, what is that fortune the reward of? Is it the earned wage of a courage genuinely shown — the price the world rightly pays him for bearing what the cautious would pay to be spared, and for facing an unforeseeable that he alone would face? Or is it the mere prize of survival — the windfall of one who took his chances along with a thousand others now forgotten, and happened, by a luck the world mistakes for merit, to be the one left standing? This is the keystone of the arch the volume has been raising, and it is the masthead's thesis carried to its sharpest point: that the great fortune, most admired and least questioned of prices, is a verdict on a hidden contest — here the contest between courage and luck — that we are poorly equipped to judge and powerfully inclined to misread. This final plate cannot settle the contest for any single fortune, for the plate before it proved that settlement impossible; what it can do, and must, is render the honest verdict the whole volume has been assembling — on the great fortune not in the particular, where the proportions are sealed, but in the general, where something true may still be said.

It is worth seeing how far the question has climbed. The volume began among foundations: that to bear risk is to render a service the risk-averse will pay for, that the worth of a gamble lives in the one who must bear it, that the routine risks can be pooled and priced away by an arithmetic that needs no courage at all. It moved to the market for risk: the premium that risky assets must offer to find a holder, the discovery that only the inescapable part of risk is ever paid for, the single coefficient that weighs it, and the unsettling claim that no skill can reliably beat the price the market has already set. And it arrived, in its later plates, at the limits: the premium found too large for any sober fear to justify, the profit that rewards the bearing of an uncertainty no insurer will touch, the catastrophe the smooth models cannot see, and at last the survivor, whose fortune we cannot tell, from the outcome alone, to be the fruit of skill or the gift of luck. At every step the same seam has run beneath the surface — the seam between the earned and the merely fallen-into — and at every step the volume has refused to paper it over. The great fortune is where that seam surfaces most visibly and matters most, for it is the largest stake, the most admired prize, and the one whose true nature the world is least willing to examine.

Let the case for the fortune as courage's wage be put at its full strength, for it is a strong one and the volume has never denied it. A world of uncertain ventures requires that someone bear the uncertainty. The cautious will not: they will pay, in the premium they forgo and the insurance they buy, to be spared it. The lenders and the workers will not: they take their fixed and certain claims and leave the hazard to another. Someone must hold the risky asset that might collapse, must fund the venture that might fail, must stake real resources on a future no one can foresee — and if no one would, the risky asset would go unheld, the venture unfunded, the new thing unmade, and the whole economy would seize for want of anyone willing to stand in the path of what might go wrong. The one who does stand there performs a genuine and indispensable service, and the fortune that rewards him, on this reading, is its honest wage: not a thing seized or stumbled upon, but the price the world pays, and rightly pays, the few who will bear what the many will pay to escape. The great fortune is, at its root and in its principle, the wage of that bearing — of a courage the world genuinely needed shown, and would have been the poorer had no one shown.

Someone must hold what the fearful will not, and stand where the cautious will not stand; and the great fortune is, at its root, the wage of that holding and that standing.

But the volume has spent its later plates assembling the case against, and it is as strong as the case for. The premium that rewards the fortune is, the puzzle showed, far larger than any sober weighing of risk can justify — swollen, in part, by a fear beyond reason, so that some of the fortune is the wage not of a danger soberly borne but of a discomfort needlessly suffered. The premium is, the swan showed, set too low against true catastrophe, so that the fortune drawn in the long calm may be an advance against a reckoning not yet come, the jaunty earnings of one picking coins before a steamroller he has not yet met. The apparent skill of the great investor is, the efficient-market plate showed, in the main either luck or disguised risk-bearing, for the price he claims to beat has already been set by the labour of a thousand others. And the survivor plate showed the deepest thing of all: that the failures who took the identical risks and lost are gone, uncounted, so that the fortune we behold is a survivor's, and survival, from the outcome alone, cannot be told from skill. To this must be added a fact the profit plate already noted — that the bearing of risk requires resources to bear, so that the chance to win the great fortune belongs disproportionately to those already secure enough to lose, and is in that measure an advantage of wealth as much as a wage of courage. The fortune, on this reading, is in large and unmeasurable part the prize of luck, the wage of a panic, and the privilege of the already-rich.

The two cases do not cancel; they coexist, and the decisive difficulty is that we cannot say, for any given fortune, in what proportion. The survivor plate sealed this conclusion: when the unlucky have vanished, the outcome alone — the fortune's existence, its size, the glittering record behind it — cannot tell us how much was courage and how much was luck. The same vast fortune is consistent with a genuine master of uncertainty, who bore real risk and read the unforeseeable truly, and with a reckless or merely ordinary gambler, who took his chances among thousands and was the one the dice happened to favour; and nothing visible in the fortune itself distinguishes the two. We may be certain that both ingredients, courage and luck, are present in every great fortune in some measure; we can almost never say in what measure, for the one instrument that might tell us — the outcome — is precisely the instrument the survivor's lesson has shown to be mute. The fortune announces its magnitude to all the world and confesses its cause to no one. That it was won, we see plainly; whether it was won by a courage that deserved it or a luck that merely fell upon it, the fortune itself will never say — and the admiring world, which reads every fortune as the proof of a merit, is reading into it what cannot be read out.

The fortune announces its magnitude to all the world and confesses its cause to no one: that it was won we see, but whether by courage or by luck it will never say.

What verdict, then, can the volume render? Not that the great fortune is undeserved, for that would deny the genuine service its core rewards, and the volume has insisted the service is genuine: someone must bear what others shed, and the bearing has a wage that is honestly earned. Nor that the great fortune is deserved, for that would ignore everything the later plates established — the fear that swells the premium beyond reason, the catastrophe the calm years borrow against, the luck the vanished failures conceal. The honest verdict lies, uncomfortably, between, and it has three parts. The reward for bearing risk is a true wage at its root, for the bearing is a real service the world requires. It is a swollen wage in its size, for it pays for more fear than reason warrants, and is mismeasured against the catastrophe it should most dread. And it is a lottery in its allotment, for which of the willing bearers actually collects the great fortune, and which is ruined and forgotten, is settled by a luck the outcome forever disguises as skill. The great fortune is therefore earned as a category — someone must bear risk, and bearing it is a service worth a wage — and undecidable as a desert, for whether any particular holder earned his by courage or won it by luck is sealed beyond our reading. Courage is genuinely among the ingredients of every great fortune; it is nowhere near the whole of any; and the world's serene conviction that it is the whole — that the fortunate are simply the deserving — is the masthead's verdict mistaken, one final time, for a fact of nature.

One matter the volume has held back, and it belongs at the threshold of what comes next. This volume has asked how the great fortune is made — whether by courage or by luck — but not what becomes of it once made, and the second question may unsettle the first. For a fortune, however it was won, does not sit idle. Once amassed, it becomes a stock of wealth that earns a return by the mere fact of its ownership — that grows, compounds, and throws off income whether or not its holder ever again bears a single uncertainty or shows a moment's further courage. The wage of risk, whatever its original alloy of merit and luck, hardens into a possession that pays its holder simply for the having; and a fortune that perpetuates and multiplies itself across the decades, and down the generations, by the quiet arithmetic of accumulation owes its persistence to no courage at all. So even the most genuinely earned fortune becomes, in time, something other than a wage — a self-feeding stock whose growth is the return on having, not the reward for doing. Whether that persistence is itself earned, and what it does to an economy when wealth begets wealth faster than labour can close the gap, is a question this volume cannot take up, for it concerns not the price of risk but the nature of accumulated wealth itself. It is the matter of the stock, and the bridge runs there.

And so the arch is closed. The volume that began with a man's small reluctance to gamble ends with the great fortune and its verdict: that the reward for bearing risk is a real wage at its root, swollen beyond reason in its size, and parcelled out by a luck we crown as merit — earned as a category, undecidable as a desert, and never the pure proof of courage the world takes it for. With this the fourth of the table's volumes stands complete, and the price of risk takes its place beside the price of labour, the price of land, and the price of time — four of the five fundamental prices now anatomized, four of the verdicts now rendered, each on the same unsettled seam between the earned and the extracted. One volume of the first family remains, the price that is the difference between two prices, before the family of the prices is itself complete. The numéraire the work set out to find was never a number; it was this — a way of reading each ordinary price for the hidden contest it conceals, and of refusing the comfortable conviction that the price is simply a fact of nature, and the fortunate simply the deserving. As the table says of every price, first to last: the argument remains open.

A note on the plate's sources

  1. Friedrich A. Hayek, The Constitution of Liberty (1960) — that market reward tracks value to others, not merit or desert.
  2. Frank H. Knight, “The Ethics of Competition” (1923) — that the rewards of competition carry little ethical sanction.
  3. John Rawls, A Theory of Justice (1971) — the distribution of talents and fortune as arbitrary from a moral point of view.
  4. Thomas Nagel, “Moral Luck” (1979) — the depth to which what we praise is hostage to luck.
  5. Christopher Jencks et al., Who Gets Ahead? (1979) — the large unmeasured residual in economic success, much of it luck.
  6. Robert H. Frank, Success and Luck: Good Fortune and the Myth of Meritocracy (2016) — the modern reckoning of luck's underrated role.
  7. Andrew Carnegie, “The Gospel of Wealth” (1889) — the fortune-maker's own account of the fortune.
