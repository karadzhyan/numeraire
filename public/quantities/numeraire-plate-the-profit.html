Plate the Ninth, of twelve

The Profit

Frank Knight's great distinction between risk, which can be measured, insured, and priced, and uncertainty, which can be none of these — the genuine unforeseeable that faces the founder of any novel venture. And his claim that profit, the entrepreneur's pure return above the wages of land and labour and capital, is the reward not for bearing risk, which the market prices away, but for bearing that unpriceable uncertainty: the wage of facing the unknown.

in print · published

Every plate of this volume so far has treated risk as a thing that can be measured — a known spread of outcomes with knowable odds, to be pooled in an insurer's book, washed out by diversification, or priced into a premium that beta weighs. This plate, following the great distinction Frank Knight drew in 1921, insists that the most consequential uncertainty in economic life is of an entirely different kind, and that a different and grander reward attaches to bearing it. Knight separated two things that ordinary speech confuses. There is risk, which is measurable: the uncertainty of a coin or a mortality table, where the odds, if not the outcome, are known, and which can therefore be insured, pooled, and priced away. And there is uncertainty proper — true, unmeasurable uncertainty — the genuine unforeseeable of a wholly novel venture, a situation so unique that no odds can be fixed to it at all, that no insurer will cover and no market can price. And profit, Knight argued — the pure return of the entrepreneur, the residue left over once the wages of labour and the rent of land and the interest on capital have all been paid — is the reward not for bearing risk, which the market prices away to nothing, but for bearing that unpriceable uncertainty: the wage of facing the genuinely unknown. This plate will set out the distinction and the claim that hangs upon it; will show how the entrepreneur, by paying his factors certain sums and keeping the uncertain remainder, comes to bear the unforeseeable and to earn or lose by it; and will ask, for the volume's question, whether profit so understood is the noblest earned reward of all — or whether it dissolves, on closer inspection, into monopoly, luck, and the advantage of those who could afford to gamble.

The distinction is best seen in what can and cannot be done about each. Risk, in Knight's sense, is uncertainty of a measurable kind: the gambler at the wheel does not know where the ball will fall, but he knows the odds exactly; the insurer does not know which house will burn, but he knows, from a thousand houses, very nearly how many will. Where the odds are known, the previous plates have shown, the uncertainty can be tamed — insured against, pooled away, diversified into nothing, or priced into a premium — and because it can be tamed, bearing it commands no special reward beyond the ordinary premium the market sets. Uncertainty proper is another matter entirely. It is the situation for which no odds exist, because the case is genuinely novel and unrepeatable: the launch of a product the world has never seen, the entry into a market that does not yet exist, the wager on a future that resembles no past. Here there is no table of frequencies to consult, no thousand similar cases to average, nothing whatever on which an insurer could set a rate. The uncertainty cannot be measured, and therefore cannot be insured, pooled, diversified, or priced. It can only be borne. And the whole of Knight's theory turns on the difference: that the first kind of uncertainty the market handles routinely and rewards modestly, while the second it cannot handle at all, and rewards — when the bearer has judged it rightly — with profit.

Why should profit attach to the second kind and not the first? Because anything that can be priced has, in the very act of being priced, already had its reward competed down to the ordinary level. If a venture's hazards were merely risky — a known chance of a known loss — the entrepreneur could insure against them, or the lenders and investors who funded him would price the risk into the return they required, and what he bore would earn only the going premium for risk that the earlier plates anatomized: a fair return, but no pure profit, nothing above what the market pays everyone who bears measurable risk. Pure profit, the excess over all such ordinary returns, cannot come from bearing what the market already knows how to price. It can come only from bearing what the market cannot price at all — the genuine uncertainty that no premium captures because no one can compute it. The entrepreneur who commits real resources to a genuinely uncertain venture is doing the thing no insurer will do for him and no market will price for him; he is standing, alone and uninsured, in the path of an unforeseeable future. If he has judged it rightly, the residue that falls to him after every priced and contracted claim is met is his profit — and that profit is precisely the wage of the unpriceable uncertainty he, and he alone, agreed to bear.

Risk is the uncertainty one can price and shed; profit is the reward for the uncertainty one can neither price nor shed, but only bear.

The mechanism by which the entrepreneur comes to bear this uncertainty is the firm itself, and it is worth seeing in detail, for it explains the peculiar shape of his reward. Consider what the founder of a venture does. He engages workers and promises them wages — fixed sums, payable whether the venture flourishes or fails. He rents premises and machines at agreed prices; he borrows capital at a stated rate of interest. To each of these he offers certainty: the worker will have his wage, the landlord his rent, the lender his interest, regardless of how the uncertain enterprise turns out. And in exchange for granting them that certainty, the entrepreneur claims for himself the one thing that is left — the residual, whatever remains of the venture's proceeds once all the fixed and certain claims have been satisfied. That residual is radically uncertain, for it is the venture's success, the very thing no one can foresee, that determines its size. So the firm is revealed as an engine for the redistribution of uncertainty: it gathers the unforeseeable into a single pair of hands and parcels out certainty to everyone else. The factors are insured, in effect, by the entrepreneur, who takes their share of the uncertainty onto himself and is paid, if he is paid at all, out of the residue. Why production should be organized in just this way — why these islands of fixed command should form within the open sea of the market — is the question the bridge to the firm takes up; but its bearing on profit is already plain. Profit is the name of the residual, and the residual is uncertain because the future is.

It follows, and the point is easy to forget amid the celebration of the founders who succeed, that the residual can be negative. The entrepreneur who has promised his workers their wages and his lenders their interest, and whose venture then fails to sell, must meet those certain claims out of proceeds that have not materialized — and the residue that falls to him is then not profit but loss, borne by him precisely because he agreed to absorb the uncertainty the others were spared. Profit and loss are the two faces of a single act of uncertainty-bearing; the entrepreneur is the one who has agreed to take whichever face the future turns up. And the future, far more often than the folklore of enterprise admits, turns up the unkind one. The great majority of new ventures fail; most of those who set out to bear the unforeseeable bear it straight into loss and vanish, leaving the field to the minority who guessed right. This asymmetry of attention — the survivors loud and studied, the failed silent and gone — will trouble the volume's reckoning of profit at every turn, for the profits we observe are the winnings of those who happened to survive, and the identical courage of those who did not is nowhere in the ledger. The wage of uncertainty, unlike an ordinary wage, is paid to some and exacted from others, and only those it is paid to are counted.

For all that, Knight's account gives profit the most genuinely earned reading any reward in this volume has yet received, and it deserves to be stated at its full strength. Bearing true uncertainty is a real service, and an indispensable one. Someone must commit resources to the ventures whose outcome no one can foresee, or nothing new is ever attempted: no product that did not exist before, no method untried, no market unopened. The risk-averse will not do it, for the uncertainty cannot be insured away to make it palatable; the workers and lenders will not do it, for they have taken certain claims and left the hazard behind. It falls to the entrepreneur, who alone agrees to stand in the path of the unforeseeable and absorb whatever it brings. In doing so he confers a double benefit: he brings the new thing into being, and he grants to everyone he engages the certainty of a wage or a rent or an interest that does not depend upon the venture's success. Profit, on this reading, is the wage of that service — the reward for facing the genuinely unknown so that others need not, and for converting the world's irreducible uncertainty into the fixed and liveable incomes of those who could not have borne it themselves. It is, if Knight is right, the noblest earned reward in the whole anatomy of prices: not the rent of a position, nor the premium of a measured risk, but the wage of courage in the face of the dark.

Someone must face the unforeseeable, or nothing new is ever ventured; and profit is the wage of the one who does.

And yet the Knightian account is the best case for profit, not the whole truth of it, and honesty requires the qualifications be set down as plainly as the ideal. They are three. First, much of what is called profit in the world is not the wage of borne uncertainty at all, but the rent of a protected position — the extra return that flows to the holder of a patent, a franchise, a dominant share, a regulatory moat, none of which is the reward for facing the unforeseeable, and all of which the volume's companion on rent would call, less flatteringly, monopoly rent. A great fortune labelled profit may prove, on inspection, a toll levied from a position of power, earned in no harder sense than the gatekeeper earns who holds the only road. Second, profit accrues only to the successful, and success in the face of true uncertainty is desperately hard to tell from luck. Knight held that the entrepreneur exercises a genuine judgment, a skill in reading the unforeseeable that deserves its reward; but where the odds truly cannot be known, the line between the founder who judged well and the founder who merely guessed right grows faint, and the survivors who ascribe their fortunes to judgment are exactly the ones we should expect to see whether judgment or luck had saved them. Third, the bearing of uncertainty requires resources to commit and to lose, so that the chance to earn profit belongs disproportionately to those who already hold capital — who can absorb a loss that would ruin a poorer venturer — and is in that measure not only a wage for courage but a privilege of the secure. Profit's earned core is real; but in the world it comes alloyed with rent, with luck, and with the advantages of wealth, and the alloy does not easily refine away.

So profit stands, at its Knightian core, as the most genuinely earned of all the rewards the volume has examined — the wage of bearing the one thing that can be neither priced nor pooled nor shed, the true uncertainty of a future no one can foresee — and as, in its worldly form, the most thoroughly alloyed, shot through with the rent of position, the luck of the survivor, and the advantage of those rich enough to gamble. The volume cannot separate the core from the alloy in any actual fortune; the same sum that is, in part, the just wage of courage is, in part, the toll of a monopoly and the prize of a lucky draw, and the proportions stay hidden. What Knight established beyond dispute is the category: that there is a kind of uncertainty the market cannot reach, that someone must bear it for anything new to happen, and that the bearing is a service for which profit is, at least in part, the honest payment. The two threads the volume has left dangling — the unforeseeable that brings ruin as readily as riches, and the impossibility of telling the skilled survivor from the lucky one — it now gathers up. The next plate turns to uncertainty's cruel face: the rare and unforeseeable catastrophe that the smooth calculations of risk consistently fail to see, and the premium consistently fails to price. A note on the plate's sources

  1. Frank H. Knight, Risk, Uncertainty and Profit (1921) — the distinction, and profit as the wage of the unforeseeable.
  2. Joseph A. Schumpeter, The Theory of Economic Development (1911) — profit as the reward for innovation, the entrepreneur as disturber of the calm.
  3. Ronald H. Coase, “The Nature of the Firm” (1937) — why the firm exists, and the hand that directs it.
  4. Israel M. Kirzner, Competition and Entrepreneurship (1973) — the entrepreneur as alert to the opportunity others miss.
  5. Armen A. Alchian & Harold Demsetz, “Production, Information Costs, and Economic Organization” (1972) — the entrepreneur as residual claimant.
  6. William J. Baumol, “Entrepreneurship: Productive, Unproductive, and Destructive” (1990) — profit-seeking that creates value, and that merely extracts it.
  7. Mark Casson, The Entrepreneur: An Economic Theory (1982) — the modern theory of judgment under uncertainty.
