The argument of the group

With this group the table changes scale. The first four worked at the level of the part — a single price, a quantity, a failure, an institution. Here we step back to the whole: the economy in aggregate, the sum that obeys none of the rules of its parts. And the founding scandal of the field, the thing macroeconomics exists to explain and has never settled, is exactly that — that the whole is not the sum of its parts.

The fallacy of composition runs through every volume. One worker, by accepting less, can find work; all workers, lowering their wages together, only lower the demand for their own labor. One household grows richer by saving more; a nation that all saves at once may merely shrink its own income, and save no more for the trying. One person can step out of cash before the inflation arrives; everyone cannot. What is prudent for the one is, again and again, ruinous for the all — and the four aggregates are the four faces of that stubborn whole: how large the pie grows over the long run, why it lurches over the short, in what unit it is measured, and how, in the end, it is cut.

The group opens and closes on a single figure: the wage. Its first scandal is the willing worker who cannot find work — a wage that will not clear. Its last question, whether the division between labor and capital is set by productivity or by power, runs, as its own description admits, straight back to The Wage — the table's first volume, and the only one in print. The whole began there; here it returns, at the scale of the nation.

And the honest finding that orders the group is the hardest the table has had to make. This is the least settled province of the discipline: the one where the same data has fit incompatible theories for a century, where the forecasts fail most publicly, where measurement and conviction are most thoroughly entangled. The numéraire of honesty is thinnest exactly where the stakes run highest — over unemployment, inflation, growth, and the division of the whole, on which the discipline's claim to know is at once least secure and most consequential.

The fifth of the table's six groups — the macroeconomics, brought from sketch to structure. The Cycle, The Growth, The Level, The Share — each now set in twelve plates, none yet in print; Volume I, The Wage, still holds that distinction. Together they are the four faces of the whole: its size over the long run, its wobble over the short, the value of the unit it is measured in, and the division of all of it — bound throughout by the one law its parts do not obey, that the sum behaves unlike its summands. The group opens on the willing worker who cannot find work and closes on whether labor's slice is set by productivity or by power; both run back to The Wage, where the table began. The discipline of the group is candour about how little, on the questions that matter most, is truly settled. Set in Fraunces and Newsreader. The argument remains open.
