Plate the Ninth, of twelve

The Rent-Seeking

Tullock's missing cost and Krueger's rent-seeking society: the discovery that a rent costs more than the rent itself, because the very scramble to capture it consumes effort and talent that make nothing. The volume's darkest extraction — the creation forgone by the minds that turn from making value to seizing it — and the institutions that decide which way they turn.

in print · published

The last plate showed the wall — the barrier that makes the monopoly rent. But a rent, once it exists, is a prize; and where there is a prize, there will be a scramble to win it. This plate takes up the scramble. Rent-seeking is the expenditure of real resources not to make value but to capture a rent that already exists — the lobbying, the litigating, the bribing, the cultivating of whoever holds the power to confer the prize — and in it the volume finds its darkest discovery: that a rent costs more than the rent itself, because the very contest to seize it consumes effort and talent and capital that produce nothing whatever. The plate follows the idea from Tullock, who saw that the standard reckoning of monopoly's cost had left out the largest part of it, to Krueger, who named the rent-seeking society and measured its waste, to the deepest implication of all — that whether a people's energy flows into creating wealth or merely into capturing it is settled not by their character but by the rules they happen to live under. And it will find that even this darkest extraction has an edge that will not come quite clean.

Begin with the cost the standard analysis missed. The plate before measured the harm of monopoly, after Harberger, as the dead weight — the trades the high price kills, the surplus that simply vanishes. But that reckoning treated the monopoly rent itself — the supernormal profit, the rectangle of price above cost — as a mere transfer: a sum shifted from the pockets of the consumers to the pocket of the monopolist, regrettable enough, but no loss to society at large, since what the one side loses the other gains. Gordon Tullock, in 1967, saw that this was mistaken, and mistaken by a very great deal. For the monopoly rent is not simply handed to the monopolist; it is a prize, and men will spend to win it. The firm that wants the protective tariff will lobby for it; the merchant who wants the exclusive licence will court the official with the power to grant it; and the resources poured into all this courting and lobbying and contriving — the labour, the talent, the money spent in the fight to be the one who gets the rent — produce nothing at all. They merely settle who shall receive a transfer. And so the rent, which the textbook had dismissed as a harmless redistribution, turns into a genuine social waste: not the whole of it in every case, but as much of it as is consumed in the struggle to capture it. The true cost of the monopoly, Tullock showed, is the dead-weight triangle plus the resources dissipated in the race for the rent — and the second can dwarf the first.

The idea found its name, and its measure, in the work of Anne Krueger. Studying the economies of the developing world in 1974 — places where the state rationed scarce import licences, each licence a small fortune to whoever held it, for it let its bearer buy abroad cheap and sell at home dear — Krueger saw Tullock's logic written across whole nations. Where a government creates a rent and parcels it out, those who covet it will compete for it: not by making anything, but by lobbying, by bribing, by cultivating the officials, by building idle factories to qualify for a quota, by waiting in the queue. And this competition, Krueger showed, can dissipate a staggering share of the rent — she reckoned the waste, in the countries she studied, at large fractions of their national income, fortunes of effort and talent and corruption poured not into production but into the capture of privileges the state itself had conjured into being. She gave the thing its enduring name: the rent-seeking society — an economy in which the surest road to wealth runs not through the workshop but through the ministry, and in which the energy of the ablest is spent not on enlarging the common stock but on enlarging their own slice of it.

And here the plate reaches the distinction that gives it its depth. The pursuit of gain, the economists have always known, can be the very engine of plenty: in the open market, the man who seeks his profit must serve his customers to earn it, and his self-interest is turned, by competition, to the public good — the pursuit of profit is the pursuit of value created, and Smith's whole system rests upon it. But the same hunger for gain can run in a wholly different channel. Where wealth can be got not by creating value but by capturing it — by securing a privilege, a protection, a monopoly, a transfer — the pursuit of gain becomes the pursuit of rent, and it creates nothing; it merely seizes, while burning resources in the seizing. Profit-seeking grows the common stock; rent-seeking only fights over it, and shrinks it in the fighting. And the unsettling truth at the heart of the matter is that the same human energy — the same ambition, the same cleverness, the same capital — can flow into either channel, and what decides which is not the virtue of the people but the rules of the game. Where the rules reward the making of value, the energy goes to making it; where the rules reward the capture of rents, the energy goes to capturing them.

This truth a later economist, William Baumol, pressed into a striking general law. The supply of energetic, ambitious, enterprising people, Baumol argued, is roughly the same in every society and every age; what changes, and changes dramatically, is how that enterprise is allocated — between the productive work of innovation and trade, and the unproductive work of rent-seeking and litigation, and worse. In a society whose rules make political privilege the road to fortune, the able will become courtiers and contrivers, and the society will stagnate however gifted its people; in a society whose rules make invention and commerce the road to fortune, those same able people will become engineers and merchants, and the society will flourish. The wealth or poverty of nations, on this telling, turns not on whether they possess talented and ambitious people — for they nearly all do — but on whether their institutions channel that talent into creating value or into capturing it. Rent-seeking, seen so, is not the moral failing of particular greedy men but a response to the incentives before them, an allocation of effort that bad rules invite and good rules forbid; and the remedy is never to scold the seekers, who answer rationally to the rewards laid out for them, but to reform the rules that set a prize upon the seeking.

Now return to the volume's question, which rent-seeking carries to its darkest point. Through the volume's first plates the rent was, at worst, a transfer — the landlord's unearned increment, the value society made and the owner took: hard on the renter, sweet for the owner, but the surplus itself preserved, merely moved from hand to hand. The monopoly rent went further, destroying a surplus by restriction in order to capture a slice of it. But rent-seeking reveals a cost deeper still. For the mere existence of a rent — any rent, capturable by effort — calls forth the effort to capture it; and that effort is a pure deduction from the wealth of the world, talent and capital and labour spent in a zero-sum struggle that leaves no product whatever behind. The rent does not merely move the surplus, nor merely shrink it; it corrupts the allocation of human energy itself, drawing the ablest away from the work of creation and into the work of capture. This is extraction at its most destructive — not the quiet transfer of a value already made, nor even the killing of a few trades at the margin, but the active diversion of the very effort that might have made new value, into the sterile business of fighting over the value that exists. The deepest cost of a rent is not the rent, nor even the trades it kills, but the inventions never made by the minds that spent themselves seeking it.

And yet the indictment, for all its force, has an edge that will not come clean, and an honest plate must say so. For the line between capturing value and creating it — between rent-seeking and honest competition — is, in the particular case, genuinely hard to draw. The firm that advertises: does it inform the customer, or merely battle a rival over a fixed market? The company that lobbies: does it corrupt the legislature, or bring it real knowledge that it needs in order to legislate well? The litigant: does he extract, or enforce a right that truly ought to be enforced? The firms that race to the patent office: do they merely duplicate one another's effort, or call forth, in the very racing, inventions that genuinely advance the world? Each is partly the one thing and partly the other, and the proportion is fiercely disputed. Nor is the dissipation ever quite so total as the starkest model implies: the competition for a rent seldom consumes the whole of it, and the seeking often throws off some real product along the way — the information, the invention, the right enforced — so that the waste, though real, is rarely the entire rent. And the measure of it all — how much of a nation's lobbying and litigating and advertising is sterile rent-seeking, and how much is productive competition — is among the hardest quantities in all of economics to pin down, and the estimates range enormously. The waste is real, and may be vast; but its boundary with honest enterprise is a matter of judgement, and its magnitude a matter of dispute.

Return, then, to the volume's question, which rent-seeking answers with a peculiar and disquieting force. The rents of this volume are, it has argued, very largely extraction — values made by one party and captured by another who owns the spot, or the barrier, or the privilege. Rent-seeking shows the last and largest cost of that extraction: that the prize of a capturable rent draws the world's energy toward the capturing and away from the making — that a society thick with rents will spend its talent fighting over wealth instead of creating it, and grow poor in the very midst of its own cleverness. This is the darkest verdict the volume has reached: that the cost of a rent is not only what it takes from the renter, nor only the trades the barrier kills, but the creation forgone by every able mind that turns from making value to seizing it. And yet — for this volume's scruples never quite loosen their hold — the line between the seizing and the making is, in any real case, hard to find; the seeking is rarely pure waste; and the fault, where there is fault, lies less in the seekers, who answer the rewards before them, than in the rules that set those rewards. Rent-seeking is the proof that institutions are not neutral — that the same human energy will build, or merely grab, according to what the rules repay. Whether a given expense of effort is the honest pursuit of value or the sterile pursuit of a rent — and the same lobbying, the same lawsuit, the same advertisement can be argued either way — is the question, asked now of the chase itself. As the table says of every price within it, the argument remains open.
