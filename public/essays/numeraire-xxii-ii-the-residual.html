The Residual

the theory of profit; what capital claims after wages are paid, and why what is left falls to the owner rather than to labor.

The remainder waits for the future; its owner is named before the future arrives.

— the argument of this plate, after Knight and the earlier Profit

The Function leaves the product exactly exhausted, while the business owner opens a ledger and finds a profit. The apparent contradiction begins with the name: the return to capital in a production function and the remainder in an owner's accounts cover different things. The earlier Quantities plate, The Profit, approached this remainder through Frank Knight's entrepreneur, who commits resources before their outcome can be known. Here the question is the division itself: what remains after the promised payments, what part of that remainder compensates resources supplied by the owner, and what gives the owner the claim? The answer runs through three arrangements that can occupy the same business: a productive contribution, a commitment under uncertainty, and an allocation of ownership. Calling the final line profit joins them on the page. It does not make them a single economic cause.

The first subtraction must include everything production has used, and the second must recognize what the owner could have earned elsewhere. In an invented business, sales are 200; purchased inputs cost 80, employee compensation 70, depreciation 10, and interest 5. With taxes and other charges omitted by assumption, the owner's accounting remainder is 35. Suppose the owner's unpaid work has an alternative earning of 15, and the equity committed could earn a comparable return of 10 elsewhere. Deduct those opportunity costs and economic profit is 10. The owner receives 35, but only 10 exceeds the specified alternatives. A business earning zero economic profit could still pay its workers, replace its worn equipment, reward its working owner, and provide the assumed normal return on capital. The zero names the absence of an excess; it leaves the ordinary payments standing.1

1. The ledger is invented and simplified. BEA's definition of profits from current production deducts several categories beyond compensation and distinguishes national-accounting conventions from tax accounts. Knight, Part III, chapter X, separately discusses the owner's labor and property income and the difficulty of valuing their alternatives.

Knight's distinctive remainder arises because the payments are agreed before the receipts are known. The entrepreneur bids for services on a judgment about what their combination will yield, while other entrepreneurs bid on judgments of their own. Actual receipts arrive later and can vindicate or overturn the estimate. In Knight's account, a hazard with a reliably calculable cost belongs among the costs of doing business; pure profit concerns the part that escapes such advance imputation. A contracted premium, a managerial salary, and the uncertain surplus consequently perform different jobs. The owner can hire someone to make difficult decisions and still retain the exposure to their outcome. Knight follows responsibility through that delegation, back toward the judgment that selected the manager. The residual thus rewards no fixed number of hours and carries no promised rate. It is what an earlier commitment discovers when the future becomes an account.2

2. Knight, chapter IX, “Enterprise and Profit”, and chapter X, “The Salaried Manager”. This is his account of pure profit under uncertainty. It does not turn every observed business profit into a measured payment for uncertainty.

The same commitment can leave a loss, and a fixed promise can itself fail when the business cannot meet it. Hold the invented ledger's deductions constant and let sales fall from 200 to 160. The accounting remainder becomes minus 5; measured against the owner's specified alternatives, the economic result is minus 30. This loss requires no change in the owner's courage, hours, or formal claim. Yet the promise of a wage has never made a worker invulnerable: interrupted employment and an unpaid obligation can carry the failure beyond the owner's ledger. Knight himself recognized that guarantees are imperfect and that those supplying resources can remain exposed. The reassuring picture in which one person takes all uncertainty and everyone else receives certainty describes a limiting arrangement. The actual distribution of danger must be followed through the commitments, the resources behind them, and the consequences when those resources run out.3

3. The loss calculation retains the preceding example's explicit assumptions. Knight's chapter IX permits aggregate entrepreneurial losses; chapter X discusses incomplete guarantees and exposure of other resource suppliers. No general business-failure frequency or rule of insolvency priority is inferred.

The residual goes to the owner because the enterprise has assigned that claim there, and a different ownership arrangement changes its destination. In the wage-and-ownership arrangement posed by this plate, employees supply work for agreed compensation, while the owners hold the remaining interest. The act of subtraction can tell us its size; the arrangement tells us whose account receives it. Imagine instead that the workers jointly own the same undertaking, employ a manager at an agreed salary, and obtain outside finance on stipulated terms. After meeting those terms, the remainder belongs to them as owners, in addition to whatever compensation they receive for work. The machines need acquire no new productive power for this to happen. The questions that change concern who can finance the commitment, how decisions are controlled, and how gains and losses are divided among the members. Labor and ownership are roles that people can combine.4

4. The jointly owned undertaking is a hypothetical comparison of contractual arrangements, not a claim about a particular cooperative's law or performance. Knight's chapter IX expressly treats enterprise as one conceivable organization and separates services from the residual; chapter X examines combinations of labor, property, and responsibility.

The size of a positive remainder still leaves its origin to be explained. Superior judgment can discover an opportunity; luck can make a mistaken forecast profitable; a protected position can permit receipts that competition would otherwise press down. In the invented ledger, another 20 of revenue at unchanged costs would add 20 to the remainder whether it came from a better product or a price concession secured from customers. The accounts alone would not distinguish those histories. Knight likewise refused to treat one successful outcome as a clean test of ability, and his account of entry gave a place to the means of guaranteeing commitments as well as the capacity to direct them. Having resources to lose affects who can undertake the experiment. A realized profit therefore answers the question of who came out ahead under the arrangement more readily than it answers how much that person added, or why another person could not enter.5

5. Knight, chapter IX, discusses judgment, luck, self-confidence, resources, and access to enterprise; chapter X separates pure profit from other owner income. The 20-unit comparison is an accounting hypothetical. It supplies no estimate of monopoly rents or of their prevalence.

Profit is consequently a remainder with a history, and ownership is the arrangement that gives someone the right to receive it. Some of the owner's income meets the cost of labor and capital supplied personally; some can reflect successful commitment under uncertainty; some can arise from luck or a position that strengthens the terms of exchange. Workers can hold the residual too when they also hold the enterprise. This answers why capital claims what remains after wages in the familiar firm without making the claim an inference from the production function: productive services, exposure to outcomes, and rights over the proceeds have been combined in a particular way. The division is intelligible, and it is open to examination. The next plate asks what happens to that remainder when the seller can widen the margin between what production costs and what the customer must pay.
