The Bias

the systematic departures

The whole of this table has leaned on a model of the chooser as a maximizer — a being with well-ordered wants who, faced with risk, weighs each prize by its probability and takes the most he can expect. This volume takes up the systematic, repeatable ways that real choosers part company with that model, and it does so in the order the science itself unfolded. It opens with Maurice Allais, whose paradox furnished the first rigorous crack: people knowingly violate the axioms of expected utility, guarding a certain fortune with a weight that mere probability cannot explain. A second crack opens nearby, in Daniel Ellsberg's urn of partly hidden odds: people will pay to face a risk they can measure over one they cannot, an aversion to ambiguity that no assignment of probability can capture. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky then supply the description Allais demanded — prospect theory, in which the mind values not final wealth but gains and losses from a reference point, feels a loss more sharply than a like gain, and bends the very probabilities it weighs. In earlier work the same pair map the shortcuts of judgment, the heuristics of resemblance and recall and anchoring by which the mind guesses the odds it is never simply given, and the lawful errors those shortcuts breed. David Laibson carries the inquiry across time, to the present bias that divides a person against his future selves, breaking each resolution as its hour comes. And the flagship turns the whole indictment on its head. Gerd Gigerenzer asks against what standard these departures are scored, and argues that the standard — logic in a vacuum — is the wrong one for a mind that must act in a real world of scarce time and thin information, where the swift rule that ignores most of what it might weigh often beats the laborious reckoning that weighs it all. In his terms the volume answers the question the author set it: the mind departs from the model in regular ways, and the cost of those departures is no fixed quantity but depends entirely on the standard of reason they are measured against. The departures are real; whether they are defects is the open question on which the rest depend. With the bias, the behaviors are complete, and with them the table entire.

The argument of the volume

Begin where the cracks first showed. The rational theory of choice under risk holds that a person ranks gambles by their expected utility, and it rests on axioms so reasonable they seem beyond dispute — chief among them independence, the rule that a component shared by two gambles cannot affect the choice between them. Maurice Allais built a pair of choices that set this rule against the gut, and the gut won: offered a certain fortune or a gamble for a greater, people guard the certainty with a fervor that, carried to a second and parallel choice, contradicts itself outright, in flat violation of the axiom. The culprit is the certainty effect, a premium the mind places on a sure thing far beyond what its probability allows — and crucially, the violation survives reflection, sophisticated choosers standing by it even when the contradiction is laid bare. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky furnished the description that Allais's negative result demanded. Their prospect theory holds that the mind reckons not in final wealth but in gains and losses from a reference point; that losses are felt more sharply than equal gains, so that loss aversion pervades choice; that risk-aversion among gains gives way to risk-seeking among losses; and that probabilities are passed through a distorting lens which swells the rare and the certain — the very lens that generates the Allais paradox as a prediction. A second paradox of the same vintage exposed a crack the lens of prospect theory does not reach: Daniel Ellsberg showed that people shrink from a gamble whose odds are unknown in favor of one whose odds are known, an aversion to ambiguity that no single assignment of probability, however weighted, can explain — the mind treating the immeasurable as a thing apart, and to be feared.

If prospect theory describes how the mind values the odds, the heuristics describe how it guesses them, for the odds are seldom given. In work that preceded prospect theory, Tversky and Kahneman found that the mind, asked a hard question of likelihood, swaps it for an easier one: how much a case resembles its type, how readily an instance comes to mind, how near a quantity sits to whatever number the guess began from. These shortcuts — representativeness, availability, anchoring — serve well enough by daylight but fail in lawful directions, producing the neglect of base rates, the conjunction fallacy, the dragging of estimates toward arbitrary anchors. David Laibson then carries the departures across time. Where the rational chooser discounts the future at a steady rate and so keeps faith with his own plans, the real chooser discounts the near future far more steeply than the far, and so reverses himself as the future becomes the present — resolving to save, then spending; resolving to begin, then deferring. The result is a person divided into a procession of selves, and the commitment devices by which the far-sighted self binds the impatient one are the surest proof of the bias.

The flagship turns the case on its head. Across five plates the departures have been catalogued as errors, deviations from the rational ideal, and the very word bias enters that verdict in its name. Gerd Gigerenzer refuses the verdict, not by denying the departures but by challenging the standard that condemns them. Rationality, he argues, is ecological: a rule of thought is to be judged not against the rules of logic in the abstract but against the real environment it is used in, with its scarce time, its thin information, its noise. And measured so, the fast and frugal heuristics that ignore most of what they might weigh often beat the elaborate optimal computation that weighs it all, for the simple rule, by refusing to chase the noise in a small sample, generalizes better to the unseen case — a fact the statistician knows as the tradeoff of bias against variance, in which a measured bias is the guardian of accuracy, not its enemy. Many of the celebrated errors, posed in the natural frequencies the mind evolved to handle rather than the abstract probabilities of the seminar, simply dissolve. The debate this opens — whether the departures are defects of reason or fittings to reality — is genuine and unfinished, with present bias the hardest case for the ecological view; but it reveals that reason itself has more than one standard, coherence with logic and correspondence with the world, and that which one to honor is a question no experiment can settle. So the volume answers the author: the departures are real, their cost is a joint property of the departure, the world, and the yardstick, and the deepest yield of the science of bias is to turn the question back on its own premise — before we ask how far the mind falls short of reason, we must ask what reason is. With the bias, the behaviors are complete, and the table is closed.

The argument remains open. What follows is an attempt to fix its exact edges.

In closing

This volume took up the bias — the systematic, predictable ways that real choosers depart from the rational model the rest of the table assumed. Its subject was not the random error that practice corrects but the lawful one that practice entrenches: the regular shape of how people actually choose and judge, set against the elegant theory of how an ideal agent would. It followed the departures from the first rigorous crack in the model, through the description that replaced it and the shortcuts that drive it, to the present bias that divides a person across time, and at last to the question of whether any of it deserves the name of defect. The question the author set it was this: the mind departs from the model in regular ways — which, and at what cost?

The volume traced the departures in the order they were found. Maurice Allais opened the case, his paradox the first proof that people knowingly violate the axioms of expected utility, guarding a certainty with a weight no probability can justify. Daniel Ellsberg soon found a violation of a different stripe, a settled preference for risks one can measure over risks one cannot, the aversion to ambiguity that marks the unknown as a dimension of its own. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky built the description Allais demanded — prospect theory, with its reference point, its loss aversion, its distorted weighing of the odds — and, in earlier work, charted the heuristics of judgment, the shortcuts of resemblance and recall and anchoring whose lawful failures gave the field, and this volume, their name. David Laibson carried the inquiry across time to present bias, the steep discounting of the near future that turns a person into a procession of selves at war over a shared estate, and made the market for commitment the proof of the affliction.

The flagship refused the easy verdict. Gerd Gigerenzer granted the departures and denied that they are defects, arguing that rationality is ecological — a matter of success in the real and uncertain world, not coherence with logic in the abstract — and that the fast and frugal heuristics, ignoring most of what they might weigh, often beat the optimal computation that weighs it all, a measured bias guarding accuracy against the noise. In his terms the volume's question is answered: the departures are real, but their cost is no fixed quantity, depending entirely on the standard of reason they are scored against and the world they occur in; and the deepest yield of the science of bias is to turn the question back upon its premise, for before we can say how far the mind falls short of reason we must say what reason is. With the bias joining the choice, the game, and the norm in print, the family of the behaviors stands complete — and with it, the last of the six families, the whole periodic table of economic life is finished, all twenty-six of its elements in place.

The volume charted the regular ways the mind departs from the rational model, and the flagship unsettled the easy verdict that the departures are defects — showing that whether a leaning-away is a failure of reason or a fitting to the world depends on the standard one measures against, coherence with logic or correspondence with reality. But that leaves the deepest question open rather than closed. If reason has more than one standard, and no experiment can crown the rightful one, then the whole edifice the table has built — an economics raised upon the maximizing agent, the chooser who is coherent by construction — rests on a choice of yardstick it cannot itself justify. Is the rational agent at the foundation a true likeness of the human creature, idealized but faithful, or a useful fiction we have mistaken for a portrait? The science of bias can measure the distance between the model and the mind; whether that distance is the mind's failure or the model's, it cannot finally say. The argument remains open — and so, fittingly, does the table.
