The Rate

the price of time

The rate is the price of time — the premium that makes a dollar today worth more than a dollar tomorrow, and the number on which the whole architecture of interest, from a mortgage to a nation's debt, is built. It looks like the most impersonal of magnitudes, a fact of arithmetic and patience that the market merely reveals. It is the third of the prices this table examines, after the wage and the rent, and the one whose nature the discipline has least been able to agree on. Its twelve plates follow the rate from the ancient charge that interest is theft, through the rival theories that would ground it in impatience, in the productivity of capital, and in the demand for money, to the modern fact the others were converging on all along: that the price of time is not discovered in any market but set, every few weeks, by a committee — and that to ask what an hour of waiting is worth is, in the end, to ask who decides — and, where the wait runs across the generations, by what right the living set the price of the unborn.

The argument of the volume

Of the three great prices, the rate is the one that seems to belong least to human affairs and most to the order of things. The wage is paid to a person, the rent to an owner; but the rate of interest looks like a fact about time itself — the rate at which the future is discounted against the present, the terms on which now exchanges for later. A dollar today is worth more than a dollar next year, and the interest rate is simply how much more: a number that appears to measure the patience of the world, prior to anyone's choosing.

Yet on no other price has the discipline been so divided about what is actually being measured. That the rate is the reward for waiting; that it is the price of human impatience; that it is the yield of productive capital; that it is the price of parting with money — each has been proposed, defended, and found wanting, and they cannot all be right, for they locate the rate in different worlds: one in psychology, one in the technology of production, one in the money market. A price that everyone pays and few can define is a sign that the thing being priced is not the simple magnitude it appears, and the plates in print take the rival theories in turn, to show where each holds and where each fails.

What the rival theories were circling, the modern monetary economy has made plain. The anchor of every interest rate — the rate against which mortgages, bonds, and the debt of nations are priced — is not discovered in a market at all. It is set: announced by a central bank, a committee that meets, deliberates, and votes, steering the price of time toward goals it has chosen, against an unobservable natural rate it can only estimate. The price that looked like a fact of arithmetic turns out to be a decision, revised on a schedule; and the question the volume cannot finally avoid — who decides what an hour of waiting is worth — has, in the present economy, an institutional answer, and a distributive one, for a higher rate favors the creditor and a lower one the debtor, and the committee that sets it is choosing, every few weeks, a side. And behind that choice waits a deeper one: when the cost is borne now and the benefit, or the catastrophe averted, falls on generations not yet born, the rate at which their future is discounted decides how much their welfare weighs against our own — a reckoning less of arithmetic than of conscience, on which the same impersonal-looking number sits in silent judgment.

The argument remains open. What follows is an attempt to fix its exact edges.

In closing

This volume took up the most impersonal-seeming of the three prices and found it the least settled. The rate looks like a fact about time — how much more a thing is worth now than later — but the discipline has never agreed on what makes it what it is, and the plates in print followed the disagreement to its root.

The oldest verdict was that interest is theft, the breeding of barren money, a charge for time that belongs to no one to sell; and though the charge was answered, it was answered only by naming something the lender gives up, which left open what that something is. Impatience, said the one school: the rate is the premium we place on the present, the agio of now over later. The productivity of capital, said another: the rate is what the machines throw off — until the capital controversy showed that the machines cannot be counted except at the rate they are meant to determine. The price of money, said Keynes: not the reward for thrift but for parting with liquidity, a monetary thing set in the money market and held up, in part, by mere convention. Each theory located the rate in a different world, and none could claim it whole.

And what the theories were circling, the central bank made literal: the price of time is administered. The anchor of all rates is set by a committee, steered toward chosen goals, taking — in its level — a side in the oldest contest of all, between those who have money and those who need it. So the rate joins the wage and the rent in the lesson the family of prices was assembled to teach. The wage is part worth and part power; the rent is the toll of position; the rate is part real fact and part decision — and of the three it is the one where the decision has become most explicit, made by an institution built for the purpose and announced with a date and a vote. And the contest the rate conceals runs deeper still than the one between lender and borrower, for when the rate is turned upon the far future it sets the terms between the living and the unborn — and Frank Ramsey held that to weigh a later generation's welfare for less merely because its lives fall after ours is no finding of economics but a quiet injustice. The price of time is not read off the world. It is chosen — and the choosing, like every price in this family, is a verdict in a contest that the number is arranged to hide.

If the price of time is set by a committee and not by the world, then when the future is discounted — a project deferred, a debt repaid, a life lived later rather than now — at whose rate, and in whose interest, was it discounted?
