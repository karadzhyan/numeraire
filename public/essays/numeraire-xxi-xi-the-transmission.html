The Transmission

monetary transmission; the levers, the interest rate and the open-market operation, by which a central bank actually moves the value of money.

The decision travels through the terms on which people act.

— the argument of this plate, after the Bank of England (1999)

A committee announces an interest rate; a household postpones a purchase. Between these two events lies the practical power over money. The decision must enter a market, change the terms on which someone can act, and alter expenditure before it can influence the pressure on prices. The first connection depends on the monetary plumbing. In a system where reserves are scarce, open-market operations can change their availability and the overnight rate at which banks obtain them. In the ample-reserves framework described by Federal Reserve staff in 2020, control instead rests principally on administered rates: the return available on reserve balances and a supplementary overnight reverse-repurchase facility. These alternatives influence the rates participants will accept elsewhere. Access matters. Institutions unable to earn interest on reserves need not refuse every lower market return, so that rate alone supplies no universal floor. The committee's announced intention acquires force through a specified arrangement of balances, counterparties and standing offers.1

1. Ihrig, Senyuz and Weinbach, Implementing Monetary Policy in an “Ample-Reserves” Regime: The Basics, July 2020, complete model and administered-rate sections, notes 2–4. This is the specified 2020 framework. The note distinguishes movements along steep and nearly flat reserve-demand regions and explains why interest on reserves alone cannot establish a firm market-wide floor.

Beyond the overnight market, expectations carry the decision through time. A lender committing money for years considers the short rates likely to prevail during those years, together with the compensation required for the risks of the longer commitment. Today's increase can therefore coexist with a fall in a long yield if it changes the expected future sufficiently. A decision already anticipated can have little new effect when announced; an unexpected change in the bank's account of its future conduct can move prices before another rate decision occurs. The Bank of England's 1999 account makes this distinction within its description of transmission: longer rates can move either way after a change in the official rate. The passage from that rate to a particular loan adds further terms. The lender's funding, the borrower's risk and the conditions of competition enter the quote. Monetary authority influences this calculation without naming every number in it.2

2. Bank of England, The Transmission Mechanism of Monetary Policy, May 1999, pp. 162–166; Bernanke, Reflections on the Yield Curve and Monetary Policy, March 20, 2006, “The Recent Behavior of Longer-Term Yields.” Expected future short rates and compensation for risk both matter. The 1999 operating arrangements are distinct from the later Federal Reserve framework; neither account promises a fixed increase in every borrowing rate.

The investment decision gives the next connection a visible form. Consider a hypothetical project that costs a hundred today and returns a certain hundred and six one year later, with no other costs or benefits. At a discount rate of five percent its present value is about a hundred and ninety-five hundredths; after paying for the project, the gain is about ninety-five hundredths. At seven percent the same receipt is worth about ninety-nine and seven hundredths, leaving a loss of about ninety-three hundredths. A project that crossed the threshold at the first rate fails it at the second. The arithmetic fixes the receipt and changes the relevant discount rate; policy in an actual economy can change both. Expected sales may improve, financing may become unavailable, or the firm may face a different borrowing spread. If its discount rate and expected receipts remain unchanged, this particular investment calculation remains unchanged too. Transmission requires a connection at the level where the decision is made.3

The same decision reaches households through different contracts. Suppose a borrower owes ten thousand on an interest-only loan that immediately resets from four to five percent. Annual interest rises from four hundred to five hundred, reducing the cash left for other purposes by a hundred, before any change in income. A fixed-rate borrower does not face that immediate contractual increase. A depositor whose return rises may receive more income. Even an equal transfer between borrowers and savers need not leave expenditure unchanged if they spend different proportions of their receipts. Asset valuations and the exchange rate add further routes. A stronger domestic currency can make imports cheaper and change the position of firms competing with foreign producers, while revised asset values can alter wealth and collateral. These paths meet in demand but need not reinforce one another equally. The aggregate response contains differences in contracts, balance sheets and circumstances that the single announced rate conceals.

The final connection to inflation runs through expenditure, productive capacity and expectations. When demand presses harder against available supply, firms may find it easier to raise prices and compete for workers; weaker demand can relieve that pressure. The relevant capacity is uncertain, and imported costs can move prices even while domestic activity weakens. Expected inflation also changes the real terms on which money is lent. In another hypothetical comparison, a nominal rate of five percent with a certain two-percent price increase gives a real return of 1.05/1.02 − 1, about 2.94 percent. Cut the nominal rate to three percent while the expected price increase falls to zero, and the real return is three percent. The nominal cut has not reduced that real return. With inflation instead unchanged at two percent, it falls to about 0.98 percent. A policy setting acquires meaning through the expectations and conditions surrounding it.4

4. Bank of England (1999), pp. 167–169, including the complete inflation-expectations and imported-inflation sections. The real-return calculations assume the stated future price changes with certainty; under uncertainty the simple relation using expected inflation requires qualification. The loan illustration isolates contractual interest payments, not a household's entire welfare change.

Delays and financial disruption make these connections difficult to govern. A security price may respond at once while a mortgage resets months later and a factory changes its plans later still. A bank weakened by losses may tighten lending despite a lower official rate. When further conventional rate reductions become constrained, other instruments can remain relevant. In his 2010 account, Ben Bernanke described purchases of longer-term securities as another way to affect broad financial conditions, while acknowledging their fiscal side effects. Such purchases and statements about future policy extend the authority's means of influence; their existence does not supply a guaranteed amount of additional spending. Each acts through portfolios, expectations or financing conditions that can also change for other reasons. The practical task is to estimate those responses, examine where the connection has weakened, and reconsider the intervention when the evidence changes. Announcing an instrument setting completes the first stage of that task.5

5. Bank of England (1999), pp. 166–169, on indirect effects, delays and banking disturbances; Bernanke, Central Bank Independence, Transparency, and Accountability, May 25, 2010, the quantitative-easing discussion within “The Case for Central Bank Independence.” These historical accounts support the mechanisms stated here; their dated rate settings and numerical policy estimates are not used.

The lever is powerful because many decisions depend upon the terms of waiting, borrowing and holding money. Its power is distributed across those decisions. The committee influences the financial conditions under which households and firms choose; their responses, together with supply and events elsewhere, shape the path of prices. This explains both the reach of policy and the burden of judgment that accompanies it. A rise in rates does not reveal its full consequence in the announcement, and the final inflation reading does not by itself reveal which intervening forces produced it. The forecast in The Target attempts to make that passage intelligible before the outcome arrives. The institutional question now follows the economic one. If a public body can alter conditions so consequential to ordinary lives, who gives it its purpose, who chooses the acceptable costs, and how must it account for the way it uses its instruments?
