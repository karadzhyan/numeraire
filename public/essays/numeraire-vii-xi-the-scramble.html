The Scramble

rent-seeking, the surplus consumed by the resources spent fighting to capture it.

A prize can consume resources before it changes hands.

— the argument of this plate

A surplus can attract a second activity around the activity that created it. A right to sell, a scarce permit or a protected position offers its holder an income beyond what is needed to keep the underlying resource in use. Others see the prize and devote effort to obtaining it. The winner's payment, if there is one, may pass to a seller or a treasury. The hours spent pursuing the claim, the equipment acquired solely to qualify and the work of unsuccessful competitors are resources used up along the way. The surplus can therefore remain intact as a payment to its recipient while the contest consumes some of the advantage available to society. This is the problem of rent-seeking. It asks how much of the effort surrounding a valuable claim creates something worth having, and how much merely changes the name of the person entitled to collect.

Gordon Tullock's 1967 account began with a gap in the familiar picture of monopoly and protection. The triangle measures gains lost when otherwise beneficial transactions fail to occur. The rectangle transferred to a protected seller is received by someone, and counting that payment itself as destroyed would confuse the accounts. But the prospect of receiving it can call forth another cost. People may spend resources securing the restriction, preserving it, or overcoming it; unsuccessful seekers can spend as well as successful ones. Those costs lie outside the simple triangle. A useful comparison therefore distinguishes the trades prevented, the money transferred and the real resources devoted to determining who receives it. The size of the protected income does not, by itself, measure the third item. A contest might dissipate little of its prize, much of it, or, under some arrangements, more than the prize is worth. The result depends on the opportunities and incentives governing the pursuit.1

1. Gordon Tullock, “The Welfare Costs of Tariffs, Monopolies, and Theft,” Western Economic Journal 5 (1967), pp. 224–232, especially “Dynamics: The Cost of Transfers,” pp. 228–232. Tullock includes unsuccessful efforts and defensive responses. The preserved Capture introduces this problem in the Rent volume; this plate follows its consequences for the accounting and allocation of surplus.

Consider a small contest whose rules we can see completely. Two risk-neutral participants compete for a fixed prize worth a hundred. Each chooses an amount of costly effort, and each one's probability of winning equals his effort divided by their combined effort. Effort is paid whether he wins or loses; it produces no further benefit. If both choose zero, the prize is assigned with equal probability. When one participant spends twenty-five, the other's best response is also twenty-five. At that pair of choices each has a half chance of receiving a hundred, an expected gross receipt of fifty and an effort cost of twenty-five. Each therefore expects to keep twenty-five after costs. Together they consume fifty in pursuing a prize of a hundred. The prize still reaches its winner, but half its value has been absorbed by the contest. Neither participant can improve his own expected result by changing his effort alone, although both would save resources if the same prize could be assigned without this expenditure. The calculation illustrates partial dissipation; it does not establish that every contest consumes its entire prize.2

Its derivative is 2,500/(x + 25)² − 1 and its second derivative is negative, so the unique best response is 25. At the symmetric equilibrium the two real effort costs total 50; the prize transfer is not added to that cost. The assumed contest function is an illustration, not an estimate of an actual licensing or political process.

The rule that makes effort count is central to the result. Anne Krueger's 1974 study gives the example of import licenses allocated partly by installed capacity: a firm can have a reason to build capacity because it improves its claim on the license, even when the capacity is poorly used in production. A system that rewards waiting, connections or elaborate demonstrations of eligibility invites different expenditures. If a license is instead sold by a credible auction, the winning payment goes to the issuer; the resources used preparing bids or administering the auction still have to be counted separately. None of these descriptions alone decides which institution is best. The license may serve a purpose, and every allocation procedure has costs and consequences. They show why measuring rent-seeking requires learning what changes a claimant's chances. Krueger also makes clear that the activity can be lawful and can occur inside an otherwise productive business. Its economic character follows from the use of resources and the relevant alternative, rather than from whether the participant has broken a rule.3

3. Anne O. Krueger, “The Political Economy of the Rent-Seeking Society,” American Economic Review 64 (1974), pp. 291–303, introduction and §I.A, pp. 291–293, including notes 1–3; conclusions, pp. 301–303. The capacity example is one mechanism she identifies, not a claim that all investment by licensed firms is waste. No national-income estimate or contemporary licensing statistic is imported here.

The implications reach beyond the cost of one contest. William Baumol describes entrepreneurship as an activity whose direction responds to relative rewards. Ingenuity can find a less costly way to make something, but it can also find a more effective way to acquire a privileged claim. The same society can offer opportunities for both. If the expected return to securing an exclusive position rises relative to the return to improving a product, capable people have a reason to direct more effort toward the former. The resulting loss need not appear as a line item in the winner's accounts. It may consist partly of useful work that was never attempted because a different career or project promised more. Baumol presents an institutional hypothesis about the allocation of enterprise, supported by historical discussion; it is not a proof that a fixed stock of talent moves mechanically between two boxes. People learn, enter and invent. The point is that the rules help determine which forms of enterprise receive encouragement, and the measured success of the entrepreneur alone cannot tell us what those forms contribute.4

4. William J. Baumol, “Entrepreneurship: Productive, Unproductive, and Destructive,” Journal of Political Economy 98 (1990), pp. 893–921, introduction and §I, pp. 893–896; §VII, pp. 918–919. His question concerns the relative allocation of entrepreneurial activity, while allowing its total supply to vary. The paragraph states that hypothesis without treating his historical examples as an independently established causal estimate.

Identifying the counterfactual is therefore as important as naming the scramble. Information offered in a public proceeding may improve the decision. A suit may establish an enforceable right on which useful investment depends. Research undertaken in pursuit of a patent may produce a discovery valuable to people who never own the patent. These activities can combine the pursuit of a claim with the creation or protection of value. Their whole cost cannot be classified from the presence of a private prize, nor can their whole benefit be inferred from the winner's reward. The analysis must ask what the effort changes, what it displaces and what would happen under a feasible alternative arrangement. The pure contest above deliberately excludes productive side effects so that its waste can be measured exactly. An actual institution requires evidence about those effects. This also guards against using rent-seeking as a name for every rival's demand. An objection to who should receive a surplus is a distributional judgment; an account of resources consumed in securing it is a further claim that needs its own support.5

5. Tullock, pp. 228–232, compares resource-using responses with alternative arrangements; Krueger, pp. 291–293, distinguishes different ways of competing for rents. The applications in this paragraph identify questions for analysis rather than assign a measured net benefit to litigation, information or invention. The total expense of a contested process is not automatically its avoidable social cost, and a transfer should not be counted again as though it were a resource consumed.

The scramble joins the division of surplus to the institutions that make division possible. A rule can create a valuable position and then reward people for spending resources to occupy it. The private calculation can be entirely intelligible even when the collective result leaves less to enjoy. Reducing that expenditure requires attention to the process that turns effort into an advantage; deciding who should hold the resulting right requires a judgment about the allocation itself. A cheaper contest can still yield an unjust distribution, and a worthy distributional purpose can be pursued through an unnecessarily costly contest. The next plate, The Cap, puts these questions beside an apparently simpler intervention. When the money price is held below the level that would clear a market, access becomes a prize of its own. The payment falls, but some procedure must still decide who receives the scarce good—and what the pursuit of that access will cost.
