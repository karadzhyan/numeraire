Plate viii in print

The Position

the return to a chokepoint — land, network, patent, brand, license — and how much of modern income is a toll on position rather than a reward for product.

The modern fortune is made less by producing a thing than by owning the place through which others must pass, and charging them for the passage.

— the thesis of this plate, after Joseph Stiglitz, 2012

The volume began on a farm, with a surplus thrown off by the difference between fertile land and barren. It ends at the center of the modern economy, with the very same idea — a payment for owning a scarce position rather than for producing a thing — but now advanced as a candidate explanation for the largest fortunes and the widest inequalities of the present age. The claim of this plate is stark: that the chokepoint, not the workshop, has become the characteristic source of great wealth, and that a growing share of what we record as earnings is, in truth, a toll on position — which is to say, rent.

What are the scarce positions, the spots that others must pass through? The author's own list points the way. Land and location remain, and remain potent: urban land is among the most valuable assets on earth, and its rent the oldest of all. To it the modern economy has added new kinds of position. The network, or platform, is the purest: a marketplace, a social network, an operating system draws its value from the users already gathered on it, so that whoever owns the platform owns a position no rival can easily contest, and may levy a toll on every transaction that crosses it. Intellectual property is a position created outright by law — the patent and the copyright are state-granted monopolies, fenced scarcities, frequently extended in length and breadth far beyond what any creation needed as its spur. The brand is a position in the mind — a name that commands a premium the physical product alone would never earn. And the license, the franchise, the regulatory perch confer position by rule. Each is a scarce spot; each yields a rent; none, in itself, is production.1

1. The modern positions: land and location; the platform or network, whose value rests on the users already present and which therefore tends to "winner-take-most"; intellectual property, a monopoly granted by statute; the brand; and the regulatory license. Network effects are a self-reinforcing, rent-generating scarcity — the more who join, the harder the position is to contest.
⁂

This is not only theory; a body of empirical work has documented, across recent decades, a constellation of changes that the competitive model struggles to explain but that the economics of rent renders natural. Industry after industry has grown more concentrated, dominated by a few outsized firms. The markups that firms charge over their costs have risen substantially. The share of national income flowing to profit has climbed, and the share flowing to labor has fallen by a corresponding amount. In a genuinely competitive economy these things ought not to happen: competition should erode markups toward cost and drive profit to the normal return on capital. That they have happened together is hard to square with competition, and easy to square with a rising tide of rents — returns accruing to dominant firms that hold defended positions, returns that look less like the wages of competition than like the rent of a fortified spot.2

2. The evidence: rising firm markups over cost (De Loecker and Eeckhout); the rise of "superstar firms" and the falling labor share (Autor, Dorn, Katz, Patterson, and Van Reenen); increasing concentration alongside a rising profit share — a pattern difficult to reconcile with competition but natural if rents are growing. Compare The Wage, plate xii, The Share.

The reading is not the only one, and honesty requires the dissent. Some economists attribute the same rising markups and the same falling labor share not to rents but to the rise of intangible capital — software, design, organization — whose costs are sunk and whose presence inflates measured markups without implying a toll; others point to global competition, to the difficulty of measuring output in a digital economy, or to ordinary returns on the risk of winner-take-most investment. The magnitude of the rent share is, in truth, an active and unsettled empirical question, and a careful account neither asserts that all the rise is rent nor pretends the rent reading has been refuted. What is widely agreed is that the rent component is no longer negligible, and that it has grown.3

3. The contest over interpretation: rising markups and the falling labor share are read by some as intangible capital (Haskel and Westlake), by others as globalization, mismeasurement, or the normal return to winner-take-most risk, rather than as rents. The size of the rent share is unsettled; that it is substantial and growing is the more defensible claim.

Here the founding volume and this one meet, and a single argument completes itself. The Wage showed that what a worker is paid is part worth and part power — that an employer holding the upper hand pays below the value the worker adds, and the gap measures not contribution but the strength of position across the table. This plate gives that gap its name and its general theory. The power-component of any payment is an economic rent; the surplus the monopsonist retains from his workers and the toll the platform extracts from its users are, structurally, the same thing — a scarcity of alternatives, captured by the side that owns the chokepoint. The monopsonist owns the only local market for a kind of labor; the platform owns the only practical market for a kind of exchange; in each, the absence of alternatives for the other party is the source of the rent. The falling share of income going to labor and the rising share that is rent are, on this reading, one phenomenon seen from two sides.4

4. The dyad with The Wage: the "power" component of a price is an economic rent. Monopsony surplus and platform tolls are one structure — a captured scarcity of alternatives for the party on the other side — and the falling labor share is, in this language, a rising rent share.
⁂

The inversion can now be stated in full, and it is the inversion the whole table exists to make. Classical theory read the returns to land, capital, and enterprise as payments for their contributions to production — the marginal-product account of the founding volume's second plate, applied impartially to every factor. The economics of rent reads a large and growing share of those same returns the other way: not as payment for a contribution, but as a toll collected for control of something scarce — and much of it scarce by law or by network rather than by nature. What appears in the national accounts as the reward of capital and of enterprise is, in its rent component, the reward of position: of owning the parcel, holding the patent, controlling the platform, sitting behind the licensed barrier. Production earns; position merely collects. The figure and the ground exchange places exactly as they did in the founding volume: the ownership of scarce positions, which the orthodox theory treated as a neutral backdrop against which factors were paid their products, turns out to be doing a growing share of the distributive work — deciding not how much is produced but how much of it is handed, as a toll, to those who own the gates.5

5. The inversion: a return to contribution against a toll on position; rent as collection, not earning. It is the figure/ground reversal of The Wage's eighth plate, where market structure — treated by the orthodoxy as a neutral backdrop — turns out to be doing the distributive work.

This must be stated with care, for the strong version is false and the careful version is the powerful one. It is not the claim that all profit is rent, or that production has ceased to be rewarded. Innovation is real, and often nobly so; some scarcity is natural and ineradicable; some intellectual property genuinely rewards a creation that would not otherwise have been made; and the platform that organizes a vast market does provide a real and valuable service. The claim is one of degree and of direction: that the rent component of income is large and has been growing; that much modern scarcity is contrived rather than natural, and so could be reduced by changing the rules that create it; and that the distinction between earning and collecting — between making the thing and owning the gate — is the one the economics of rent insists we keep always in view. Where a surplus springs from natural scarcity or genuine creation, it is at least a real thing honestly come by; where it springs from a fence built by law and sustained by lobbying, it is the rent-seeking of the previous plate, grown to the scale of the whole economy.6

6. The careful version: not all profit is rent — innovation, natural scarcity, and genuine reward to creation are real, and platforms render real services. The claim concerns degree and direction, and the policy-relevant line falls between natural and contrived scarcity. The seventh plate, The Scarcity, draws that line.

The point can be seen in miniature in the law of intellectual property, where the line between reward and rent is drawn explicitly and then, repeatedly, moved. A patent or a copyright is a deliberate, temporary monopoly — a rent granted on purpose, because the prospect of it calls forth invention and authorship that benefit everyone once the monopoly lapses and the knowledge falls free. So far the rent is earned, the price of creation. But the term of copyright has been extended again and again, long past the death of the author and the exhaustion of any incentive effect; patents are granted broadly and defensively, thickets of them assembled less to protect a creation than to bar a competitor. Every extension beyond what was needed to summon the creation is pure rent — a toll on the use of knowledge that adds nothing to the stock of it. The same instrument, at one length, rewards production and, at a greater length, merely collects; and the distance between the two is the distance this whole volume has tried to measure.7

7. Intellectual property is the clearest case where reward shades into rent: the monopoly is justified as an incentive to create, so extensions of term and breadth beyond the incentive margin — repeated copyright-term extension, defensive patent thickets — are pure rent, a toll on knowledge that adds nothing to its stock. The point is pressed by Boldrin and Levine, among others.

The author asked, of this volume, who earns the value of merely owning the spot that others must pass through. The volume's answer, in the strict terms the discipline itself supplies, is that no one earns it — for rent is by definition the payment that buys no contribution and changes no behavior — but that a great and growing number collect it; and that the central distributive question of the age may turn less on the wage bargain than on the ownership of positions and the rents they throw off. Whether that return is "earned or merely extracted," in the author's phrase, the economics of rent answers without much comfort: extracted, in growing part — though sometimes from a scarcity that is natural and a creation that is real, and sometimes from a fence that someone built and means to keep.8

8. The opening question answered: rent is, by definition, unearned in the incentive sense, yet widely collected, and the weight of distribution shifts from the wage bargain toward the ownership of positions. The later plates pursue the consequences — The Rentier, The Ground, and The Toll.

Rent began as the gift of the soil and ends as the toll of the platform, but its structure never changed across two hundred years: a surplus from scarcity, handed to whoever owns the scarce thing, paid for no contribution and defended by whatever means — fertility, statute, network, or sheer market power — will keep the scarcity in place. What the orthodox theory called the return to a factor, the economics of rent obliges us to read, in large and growing part, as the return to a position. And a position, unlike a contribution, produces nothing by being owned. It only charges for the passing.
