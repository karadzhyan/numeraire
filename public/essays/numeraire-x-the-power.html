The Power

too few on one side

The power is the last of the failures, and the one that strikes the price most directly — not corrupting what it conveys, as the asymmetry did, nor escaping its reckoning, as the externality, nor lying beyond its reach, as the commons, but seizing the price itself, and bending it. Its subject is what happens when there are too few on one side of the market: too few sellers, and you have monopoly, the giant who sells too dear; too few buyers, and you have its less famous mirror, monopsony, the giant who buys too cheap. In the price families, every agent was a price-taker, given the price by a market too large for any one of them to move; market power is the condition of being a price-maker, large enough that one's own choices move the price, so that the price ceases to be a signal received and becomes a strategy chosen. The single buyer of labor met in the wage was one organ of this body; here it stands revealed entire. The volume's question is the one the trapped party asks: what is a fair price worth, when one side has nowhere else to go? Its twelve plates follow that question from the anatomy of monopoly, through the barriers that shelter it, the collusion that mimics it, and the law that would break it, to a discovery about power itself: that it is nothing but the absence, for the other side, of an alternative — and that a fair price is only ever what the freedom to walk away compels.

The argument of the volume

The price families rested, though they rarely said so, on a single assumption: that no one who traded could move the price. The worker took the wage the market set; the lender took the rate; the firm took the price of its wares as a fact of nature, and asked only how much to make at that price. This is the price-taker, and the competitive price is the price among price-takers — disciplined, by the press of many sellers and many buyers, down to the cost of the last unit made, so that no one is overcharged and no one underpaid, because anyone who tried would find his counterpart gone to a rival. Market power is the breaking of this assumption. Let one seller grow large enough to be the only seller, or one buyer the only buyer, and the price is no longer a fact he must accept but a lever he can pull. He becomes a price-maker; and the price he makes is not the competitive price but the price that serves him — higher, if he sells; lower, if he buys — wrung from a counterpart who, finding no rival to flee to, must take it.

Follow the monopolist, the famous case. Alone in his market, he faces not the flat demand of the price-taker — who can sell all he likes at the going price — but the whole downward-sloping demand of the market itself: to sell more, he must lower his price to all, and so the revenue from each extra sale is less than its price. Weighing this against his cost, he stops short — produces less, and charges more, than a crowd of competitors would have — and pockets the difference as the profit of his power. Some of what he gains is merely taken from his buyers, a transfer from their purses to his; but some is destroyed outright, the value of all the trades that would have happened at the competitive price and do not happen at his — the deadweight loss, the dead weight of market power, value that simply vanishes because the price was set above the cost. And the whole of this runs in mirror for the monopsonist: the single buyer, facing the market's upward-sloping supply, buys less and pays less than competition would, transferring to himself what should have gone to those he buys from, and destroying the trades his low price strangles. Too few sellers or too few buyers: the same power, the same restriction of quantity, the same waste, in mirror.

That market power harms invites the question of what restrains it, and three answers have contended, each the subject of plates to come. The first is entry: the hope that a monopolist's fat profit will lure rivals into his market, whose competition will erode his price back toward cost — so that power, where entry is easy, is self-correcting, and lasts only as long as the barriers that keep rivals out. The second is the law: antitrust, the state's hand breaking the great combinations, forbidding the cartels, and policing the conduct by which firms acquire and keep their dominance. And the third, subtlest, is time and invention: the Schumpeterian faith that every monopoly is temporary, doomed to be overthrown by the next innovation, so that the prize of a transient dominance is the very spur that drives the progress, and the cure for today's monopoly is tomorrow's. Between these — entry, law, and innovation — the argument over market power turns; and beneath them lies the deeper matter the flagship will reach, that what we are really speaking of, in all of it, is alternatives: power is the want of them, competition their abundance, and a fair price nothing but the price that the freedom to find another compels.

The argument remains open. What follows is an attempt to fix its exact edges.

In closing

This volume took up the last of the price's failures, and the most direct. The others struck at what the price could carry — the knowledge it could not convey, the costs it could not count, the shared things it could not defend. The power strikes at the price itself, seizing it from the market and giving it to a firm: where there are too few on one side — too few sellers, or too few buyers — the price is no longer taken by all but made by one, and made to serve the one against the many.

The volume traced the anatomy of that power and the means against it. The monopolist, facing the whole demand alone, holds back his wares to lift his price, and the trades he strangles are the deadweight loss of his dominion; the monopsonist does the same in mirror, buying too little to pay too little. Such power persists, the volume showed, only behind barriers — the scale that fits one firm, the patent, the network, the brand — for where entry is free the monopolist's profit summons the rivals who compete it away. Where the barriers hold, the law has reached in: a century of antitrust, swinging from the breaking of the great trusts, through the creed that only the consumer's welfare counts, to a new alarm at the bigness of the platforms. And over all of it hangs the Schumpeterian question, whether the monopoly we condemn today is not the prize that lured its own creation, soon to be swept off by the next innovation in the ceaseless churn of creative destruction.

And then the volume turned on power itself, and found it to be a simpler and stranger thing than its anatomy suggested. Power is not, at bottom, a matter of size or number; it is a matter of alternatives. The monopolist has power over his buyers because they have nowhere else to go; the monopsonist, over his sellers, for the same reason; and the competitive price was never fair because the sellers were many, but because the buyer could always turn to another — exit, the standing possibility of walking away, was the whole of the discipline. So a fair price, the volume found, is not a property of the good, nor of its cost, but of the bargaining position: it is what the freedom to find another compels, and it dissolves the instant one side is trapped. The question what a fair price is worth, when one side has nowhere else to go, has therefore a hard answer: there is no fair price then, only the price of being trapped — and the work of competition, of entry, of antitrust, and of invention is, every one, the work of giving the trapped party somewhere else to go. With this the failures stand complete. The price cannot convey what is hidden; cannot count what is unowned; cannot defend what is shared; and cannot discipline what it cannot contest — four failures, four edges of the great coordinator, beyond each of which the price must be helped by something else: a device, a right, a covenant, a rule. The price was never the whole of the market, and the market was never the whole of economic life. The failures map the edges of the price; what lies beyond them — the institutions that scaffold it, the behaviors that underlie it — is the rest of the table, and the rest of the science.

If power is only the other side's want of an alternative, and a fair price only what the freedom to leave compels, then every monopoly is a door held shut, and every remedy — the rival, the law, the invention — a way of prying it open. The question is no longer whether the price is fair, but whether the one who pays it is free to walk away. Is he?

Volume the Tenth of Numéraire, a periodic table of economic life: twenty-six primitives gathered in six families, each turned inside out at the seam where the seminar and the essay usually part — with the rigor of the one and the grace of the other — and each scrupulous about the exact edge of what can be known.
