Plate viii in print

The Covenant

the third way: Ostrom's discovery that communities can govern a commons themselves, by rules of their own making — neither the state nor the fence, but the binding faith of those who share it.

Between the state that commands and the market that divides lies a third thing the dichotomy hid: that those who share a resource may, by rules of their own making and watching of their own doing, govern it themselves — and so they have, for centuries, without ruin.

— the discovery of this plate, after Elinor Ostrom, 1990

The volume has laid out the tragedy and the two escapes the age allowed it: the Leviathan that commands the commons from above, and the fence that divides it into private parcels. Both concede the heart of this family — that the price, left alone, fails — and both answer the failure from outside the community of users: the state by ruling them, the market by dissolving the thing they share into things they separately own. The flagship asks whether these are truly the only two doors, and answers, on the strength of evidence the parable never consulted, that there is a third — and that finding it overturns not just the cures but the tragedy itself.

For two centuries the argument over the commons ran between two poles, and the parable of the tragedy fixed them. Hardin himself, having drawn the ruin, saw only two ways out: a mutual coercion that the users agree to lay upon themselves — the state, compelling restraint — or the conversion of the commons to private property — the market, parceling it. The whole subsequent debate inherited this dichotomy. Either a power above the users must command what they may take, supplying from outside the discipline they cannot supply themselves; or the commons must cease to be common, carved into private holdings each owner will tend. State or market, Leviathan or fence, regulation or privatization: these exhausted the field, and they shared a premise so deep it was never argued — that the users themselves, left to govern what they share, are helpless, doomed by the tragedy to ruin unless ruled or divided from without.1

1. The orthodox dichotomy: Hardin (1968) and the tradition saw only two escapes from the tragedy — state coercion (a restraint the users agree to impose on themselves) or privatization. Both impose a solution from outside the community of users, and both rest on the unargued premise that the users cannot govern themselves. The Leviathan and the fence (The Leviathan, The Enclosure) exhaust the conventional field.

Elinor Ostrom doubted the premise, and did what the theorists had not: she went to look. Across decades she and her colleagues gathered the records of real commons — the high mountain pastures and forests of the Swiss and Japanese villages, governed in common for four hundred years and more without ruin; the irrigation systems of Spain and the Philippines, whose waters generations of farmers had shared by intricate and durable rule; the inshore fisheries that lobstermen and villagers had kept from collapse. And she found, again and again, the thing the dichotomy said could not exist: communities that had governed their commons themselves, for centuries, sustainably — neither by the state's command nor by the market's fence, but by their own self-made institutions, their own rules, their own watching, their own slow-built order. The tragedy, the supposed iron law, was contradicted by the plain historical fact of commons that had not been tragedies at all, but had endured, well-governed, longer than most states and most firms.2

2. Elinor Ostrom, "Governing the Commons" (1990; Nobel 2009, the first woman so honored), assembled empirical cases of common-pool resources governed sustainably for centuries by their users themselves — Swiss alpine pastures, Japanese village forests, Spanish and Philippine irrigation, inshore fisheries — without state control or privatization. The supposed iron law of the tragedy was refuted by durable, self-governed commons that outlasted most firms and states.
⁂

Here the inversion stands forth, the deepest the family has reached, for it overturns not a cure but the disease. The tragedy of the commons, Ostrom's evidence shows, was never a property of the commons; it was a property of a particular helplessness assumed in the telling. Recall how the parable runs: each herdsman, alone, mute, reckoning only his own gain, adds his beast — and so all are ruined. But every term of that picture is an assumption, and a false one for most real commons. The herdsmen are not alone but a community; not mute but able to speak, to argue, to agree; not strangers meeting once but neighbors bound for life and across generations; not helpless to bind themselves but able to make rules, to watch one another, to punish the greedy and reward the faithful. Restore to the users the powers the parable stripped from them — speech, memory, the capacity to promise and to enforce — and the dominant strategy of ruin is no longer dominant, for the herdsman who overgrazes today is the herdsman his neighbors will shun, fine, or exclude tomorrow. The tragedy was the property of an impoverished game — anonymous, silent, one-shot, ungoverned — and real communities do not play that game; they play a richer one, in which cooperation, far from being irrational, is the very thing their institutions are built to secure.3

3. The inversion: the tragedy is not a property of the commons but of the parable's assumptions — users imagined as solitary, mute, anonymous, one-shot, and unable to bind themselves. Real communities are none of these; they communicate, persist, remember, and can make and enforce rules. Restoring these capacities (the repeated game with communication and sanctions — The Sanction) dissolves the dominant strategy of overuse, and cooperation becomes sustainable. The tragedy describes an impoverished game few real commons play.

Ostrom did not romanticize the commons, and this is the second half of her achievement: she showed that self-governance, while possible, is neither automatic nor easy, and she set down the conditions under which it works. The commons that endured shared a recognizable anatomy. Their boundaries were clear — who might use the resource, and how much, was defined, so that the thing was not open to all the world. Their rules were made by those who lived under them, and fitted to the local conditions no distant authority could know. They were monitored — by the users themselves, or by monitors accountable to them — so that cheating was seen. They enforced graduated sanctions — a mild penalty for a first lapse, escalating for the stubborn — so that the greedy were checked without the community fracturing over every infraction. They had cheap and ready ways to resolve the disputes that inevitably arose. And they were left alone enough by the larger state to make and keep their own rules. These are not a formula that guarantees success; they are the marks that working commons tend to share — the institutional labor, patient and particular, by which a community makes cooperation hold. Self-governance is not the absence of rule but a demanding form of it, raised from within rather than imposed from without.4

4. Ostrom's design principles — the conditions common to enduring self-governed commons: clearly defined boundaries (who may use it); rules matched to local conditions and made by the users; monitoring by users or their accountable agents; graduated sanctions; low-cost conflict resolution; recognized rights to self-organize; and, for large systems, nested (polycentric) governance. Not a formula but the anatomy of success: self-governance is demanding institutional work, not the absence of rule.

The significance of this reaches beyond the commons, and it is the flagship's largest claim. The whole of economics, faced with the price's failures, had reached reflexively for one of two instruments: the market, to be mended or extended, or the state, to command where the market could not. The two prior failures of this family were met so — the externality by Pigou's corrective tax, which is the state, or Coase's tradeable permit, which is a constructed market; the asymmetry by devices that were, in the end, market workarounds or regulatory mandates. Market and state: between them, the discipline assumed, lay all the means of coordinating human affairs. Ostrom revealed a third — the community, governing itself; coordination that is neither exchange nor command, neither the buying-and-selling of the market nor the ordering-and-obeying of the state, but the mutual binding of those who share a fate: agreement, monitoring, reciprocity, and trust, the slow social technologies by which people govern what they hold in common. This is the covenant — the self-made, self-enforced compact of a community to steward what no one of them owns — and it is a pillar of human coordination as real as the other two, older than either, and indispensable where both fall short.5

5. The flagship's largest claim: self-governance is a third mode of coordination beyond market and state. Economics typically meets the price's failures with either the market (mended or extended — Coase's permits) or the state (command — Pigou's tax). Ostrom reveals a third: the self-governing community, coordinating by agreement, monitoring, reciprocity, and trust rather than by exchange or command. The covenant is a distinct and ancient pillar of coordination, indispensable where market and state fall short. Cf. the Institutions and Behaviors families.
⁂

The claim must be stated with care, for it is neither that the commons always escapes its tragedy nor that the covenant can replace the market and the state. Ostrom's own work is emphatic that self-governance often fails — that many commons are ruined, that the conditions for success are exacting and frequently absent, that a community lacking clear boundaries or the right to make its own rules or the means to monitor and enforce will indeed succumb to the tragedy the parable foretold. Nor does the covenant scale without limit: it thrives among the few who know one another and falters among the many who do not, so that the largest commons — the atmosphere, the oceans, the global climate, where the users are billions of strangers across every border — strain its powers to and past the breaking point, and there the state and the market and painstakingly nested layers of governance must do what no single community can. The exact claim is the liberating one and no more: that the tragedy is not a law but a contingency; that its escape is possible, and has been achieved, by neither command nor enclosure but by self-governance; and that the dichotomy of market and state, which had seemed to exhaust the options, omitted a third that is sometimes the best and sometimes the only one that fits.6

6. The honest edge: Ostrom does not claim the commons always escapes its tragedy (many fail; the success conditions are exacting and often absent) nor that self-governance replaces market and state. The covenant scales poorly — thriving among the few who know one another, faltering among the many — so the largest commons (atmosphere, oceans — The Planet) strain it past breaking, requiring state, market, and nested governance. The claim is only that the tragedy is contingent, its escape possible by a third way the dichotomy omitted.

With this the failures stand nearly whole, their argument complete in its essential shape. The price, the great coordinator, fails in three regions, and this family has mapped them. It cannot convey what one side hides: that was the asymmetry, the price corrupted as a signal by private knowledge. It cannot count what no one owns: that was the externality, the price silent about costs that fall outside the boundary of property. And it cannot defend what is shared: that is the commons, the price unable even to engage a resource that none can be charged for and all deplete — overused as a common cost, underbuilt as a common benefit, and governable, where it is governable at all, only by command, by enclosure, or by the covenant of those who share it. Three failures, three limits of the price as an instrument of coordination — knowledge, ownership, and sharing — and three regions of economic life where the price must be helped, corrected, or set aside, and something other than the price must do the coordinating it cannot.7

7. The family completed: the price fails in three regions — it cannot convey what is hidden (The Asymmetry, the corrupted signal), cannot count what is unowned (The Externality, the partial ledger), and cannot defend what is shared (The Commons, the ungovernable common-pool and public good). Three limits of the price as coordinator — knowledge, ownership, sharing — each requiring coordination by something other than the price. The Failures map the boundary of the market itself.

And so the failures, which might have read as an indictment of the price, are better read as a map of its edges — and of the country that lies beyond them. For where the price fails, the volume has shown, something else coordinates: the devices that convey what the price cannot signal, the rights that enclose what it cannot count, the covenants that govern what it cannot defend. These are not the absence of order but other forms of it — and they are the subject of all the table has yet to treat. The institutions that scaffold the market and supply what it cannot — the firm, the contract, the money, the tax, the rule — are a family still to come, and they are, every one, answers to the failures this family has charted. And the behaviors that underlie the covenant — the choosing, the trusting, the cooperating, the norm-keeping that lets neighbors govern what they share — are the family that ends the table, and the deepest foundation of all, for the covenant rests at last not on price nor on law but on the human capacity to keep faith with one another. The price was never the whole of economic life; it was only its most powerful single instrument. The failures are the family that marks where that instrument reaches its limit — and where the rest of the science, the institutions and the behaviors and the long human work of cooperation, must take up what the price lays down. The meadow need not be overgrazed; whether it is overgrazed depends on whether those who share it can govern themselves — and that, the question the commons leaves us, is the question the whole second half of the table exists to answer.8

8. The close looks beyond the failures to the rest of the table: where the price fails, other forms of coordination arise — the devices, rights, and covenants of this family — which are the subject of the Institutions (the firm, contract, money, tax, rule — the scaffolding the market needs) and the Behaviors (choice, trust, cooperation, norm — the foundation the covenant rests on). The failures map the price's limits; what lies beyond is the rest of the science. The commons leaves the table's animating question: whether those who share can govern themselves.
