The Commons

what no one owns

The commons is the last of the failures, and the deepest — the place where the price, which the asymmetry left unable to convey and the externality unable to count, is revealed unable even to engage. Its subject is what no one owns and all may use: the fishery and the pasture, the aquifer and the atmosphere, the open library of code — resources that one user's taking diminishes, yet from which no user can be barred. Of such things the textbook tells an old and bleak story, the tragedy of the commons: that each user, gaining the whole of his own taking and bearing only a sliver of the cost, will take and take until the shared thing is ruined, and that the only escapes are the state, which commands, or the fence, which privatizes. The volume's question is the one the parable poses and then forecloses: why is the shared meadow always overgrazed, and need it be? Its twelve plates follow that question from the tragedy through its mirror, the public good too little supplied, and the logic of collective action beneath them both, to a discovery that overturns the parable itself: that the tragedy was never a law of the commons but a property of a particular helplessness, and that communities have, again and again, governed what they share without ruin and without a master — by a third way the dichotomy of market and state had hidden from view.

The argument of the volume

To see why the commons defeats the price, one must see what the price requires to work. A price is a toll at a gate: to charge it, one must be able to bar those who do not pay, so the good must be excludable. And a price rations a scarce thing among rival claimants, each of whose use denies the thing to another. Ordinary private goods have both properties: the apple I buy is mine, you are excluded, and my eating it leaves none for you. But the world holds goods that lack one property or the other. The fish in the open sea are rival — each netted fish is one fewer for the next boat — yet non-excludable, for no one can fence the ocean; this is the common-pool resource, the commons proper. The light of the lighthouse is non-rival — one ship's use of it dims it for no other — yet also non-excludable, for it shines on every passing hull; this is the public good. A fourth case completes the square: a good non-rival like the lighthouse, shared without using up, yet excludable like the apple — the swimming pool, the toll bridge, the members' library, which many may enjoy together but from which any may be barred. This is the club good, and of all the shared things it is the one where a gate can still be built and a price still charged; the volume takes it up early, as the boundary of the failure. But in the common-pool and the public alike, non-excludability defeats the toll: there is no gate at which to charge, and so the price, the market's whole engine, cannot turn.

From the failure of the gate flow the two great pathologies, which are one pathology in mirror. Where the shared thing is rival and unowned — the commons — it is overused, ruined by a stampede of takers: each herdsman who adds a beast to the common pasture gains the whole of that beast and bears only his share of the resulting overgrazing, so each adds, and the pasture is grazed to death, though all would be better had all restrained. Where the shared thing is a benefit none can be charged for — the public good — it is underprovided, starved by a strike of shirkers: each citizen who might fund the lighthouse benefits whether he pays or not, so each waits for another, and the light is never built, though all would gain were it. Overuse of the common cost, underprovision of the common benefit: the tragedy of the commons and the free-rider problem are the same divergence — between what serves the one and what serves the group — seen from its two sides. And beneath both lies a structure the theory of games would name: a trap in which each, reasoning soundly, chooses the course that ruins all.

That the price cannot govern the commons has long seemed to leave only two who can. The first is the state — the Leviathan that, standing above the users, commands what they may take and provides what they will not, by quota and by tax and by public works. The second is property — the fence that carves the commons into private parcels, so that each owner, now bearing the whole cost of his own overuse, restrains himself as the herdsman would not. Regulate or privatize; the state or the market; coercion from above or enclosure from within — between these two the argument over the commons ran for a century, and both, note, concede the central point of this family: that the price, left alone, fails, and must be either replaced by command or rebuilt by the manufacture of property. But there is a third possibility, which the quarrel of state and market obscured, and which the volume builds toward: that the users themselves, neither commanded nor divided, might govern what they share — and that the tragedy thought to make this impossible was an artifact of the very assumptions that declared it inevitable.

The argument remains open. What follows is an attempt to fix its exact edges.

In closing

This volume closed the third family by taking up the price's last and deepest failure. The asymmetry showed the price unable to convey what one side hides; the externality, unable to count what no one owns; the commons shows it unable even to engage a whole class of goods — the shared and the collective, rival but unownable, or beneficial but unchargeable — for the price is a toll, and where no gate can be built, no toll can be charged.

The failure, the volume showed, has two faces. The common pasture, rival and unowned, is overgrazed, each herdsman taking the whole of his beast and bearing but a sliver of the ruin, until the field that fed all feeds none; the public lighthouse, beneficial and unchargeable, goes unbuilt, each who might fund it waiting for another, until the common interest, which all share, none defends. Overuse and underprovision, the tragedy and the free rider, are one divergence in mirror — and beneath them, the logic of collective action: that the larger the group that shares a good, the weaker each member's hand in securing it. Against this the age set two cures, and only two. The state might command the commons from above, by quota and public works; or property might carve it from within, the fence giving each user the whole cost of his own excess to bear. Regulate or privatize: between the Leviathan and the enclosure the argument ran, and both conceded that the users, left to themselves, were helpless.

And then the volume turned on the parable itself, and found the helplessness to be the parable's assumption rather than the commons' fate. Elinor Ostrom went and looked, at pastures and forests and fisheries and irrigation works governed in common for centuries, and found the tragedy averted not by the state and not by the fence but by the users themselves — bound by rules of their own making, watched by their own monitoring, held by their own graduated sanctions and their own slow-built trust. The tragedy, she showed, was the property of a particular helplessness — of users imagined as mute, anonymous, and unable to bind themselves — and where that helplessness is overcome, where the users can speak and watch and promise and punish, the meadow need not be overgrazed at all. So the shared meadow is overgrazed when those who share it cannot govern themselves, and not otherwise; and the question whether it must be is answered, against the parable, with a qualified and hard-won no. With this the failures stand complete: the price cannot convey what is hidden, cannot count what is unowned, and cannot defend what is shared — three limits of the great coordinator, three regions of economic life where the price must be conveyed, corrected, or governed by something other than itself. And that something — the signal devised, the right assigned, the covenant kept — is the subject of all the table has yet to treat: the institutions that scaffold the market, and the behaviors that underlie the covenant. The price was never the whole of economic life. The failures are the family that marks its edges; what lies beyond them is the rest of the science.

If the meadow is overgrazed only when those who share it cannot govern themselves, then the tragedy of the commons is not a fact of scarcity but a failure of community — and the question is no longer how to price the meadow, nor who shall command it, but whether those who share it can yet learn to keep faith. Can they?
