The Hostage

the bond, the collateral, the hostage staked and forfeited on breach; how a promisor binds himself by what he puts at risk.

The promise gains weight when refusal puts something at risk.

— the argument of this plate, after Williamson (1983)

A promise concerns what someone will do after the moment in which we decide to trust him. The difficulty lies in that interval. A supplier begins work, a buyer advances money, a partner learns a trade whose value depends on the partnership continuing. When the time for performance arrives, the person who benefited from this reliance may find refusal attractive. The promise alone has left that later choice intact. One answer is to change what refusal would cost. A deposit, a bond, or an investment valuable within the relationship puts something of the promisor's own at hazard. This is the economic hostage: an exposed asset whose loss can make fidelity serve the interest of the person who pledged it. The promise gains weight because the future choice has acquired a consequence.

The argument continues the inquiry begun in The Mast, where commitment changes a decision that words alone cannot settle. Consider an agreement that gives each party a gain of ten if both perform. After one party has performed, the other can obtain eighteen by refusing its own promised contribution. With no further consequence, refusal brings an extra eight. Now suppose that this second party has lodged a bond of twelve, returned on performance and forfeited on a correctly established refusal. Its payoff from refusal becomes six; keeping the promise still yields ten. The bond has changed the later comparison. It has not made the promisor generous or removed the possibility of disagreement. It has made a particular deviation less rewarding under a particular rule for releasing the pledged asset.1

1. The illustration is new. Payoffs are net of the ordinary costs of performance; the bond is returned in full on compliance, with no financing cost in this first comparison. Refusal pays 18 − 12 = 6, against 10 for performance. A bond of eight gives indifference, while a smaller bond does not deter the specified deviation.

The arrangement depends on more than the amount written beside the word bond. Suppose wrongful refusal leads to forfeiture only half the time. A risk-neutral promisor now expects to keep twelve from refusing: eighteen less one-half of twelve. The original safeguard no longer suffices. Raise reliable detection to three-quarters, and the expected payoff falls to nine, below the ten from performance. What matters is the credible consequence at the point of choice, including the probability that it will actually follow. A nominally enormous obligation owed by an empty shell may supply little protection. Conversely, a modest sum already in dependable custody can matter greatly. The institution that identifies the event and controls the asset belongs inside the explanation of credibility, rather than being an administrative detail added after the economic work is done.2

2. Expected refusal payoff is 18 − qB, with risk neutrality, forfeiture probability q and bond B. For B = 12, q = 0.5 gives 12 and q = 0.75 gives 9. Deterrence requires qB > 8 for strict preference. These assumptions exclude erroneous forfeiture, delay, and litigation cost; the following paragraphs restore the institutional questions.

Williamson's 1983 treatment makes the productive purpose of commitment central. A supplier may invest in capacity that loses value if a particular customer withdraws. A safeguard against withdrawal can support a better initial bargain and a more useful investment. His model also exposes a second danger: the party holding the pledge may contrive the event that permits it to keep the asset. This complicates the apparently simple advice to demand more security. A safeguard against one party's opportunism can create an opportunity for the other's. The remedy has to consider both sides of the relationship.3 In our example, an adjudicator who accepts a false claim of nonperformance would turn the twelve from assurance into a prize. Better custody, evidence, and review can therefore increase confidence without increasing the amount placed at risk.

3. Oliver E. Williamson, “Credible Commitments: Using Hostages to Support Exchange”, American Economic Review 73 (1983), pp. 519–528, especially §II.B and §III.A. The article distinguishes protection of transaction-specific investment from the hazards of contrived cancellation, uncertain valuation, and incomplete agreements.

There is consequently no universal rule that the ideal pledge must be worthless to its recipient. A payment compensating a supplier for a real investment serves a different purpose from an asset exposed merely to demonstrate resolve. Williamson explicitly distinguishes these functions: recipient value matters in his investment model, while a separate screening example considers a pledge valuable only to its owner.4 The design question is what behavior and what loss the arrangement addresses. If a builder has incurred a verified expense in reliance on an order, a cancellation payment can distribute that expense. If the only purpose is to discourage a false declaration, enriching the person who judges the declaration may introduce a needless conflict. The same physical asset can support different mechanisms, and the word collateral does not by itself tell us which one is operating.

4. Williamson, §II.B, pp. 524–525, and §III.A.1, pp. 526–527. The recipient-value result in the investment model and the separate screening illustration have different assumptions. Neither supplies a universal prescription for the form or legal enforceability of a deposit.

A pledge can also exclude someone whose performance would have been reliable. To lodge twelve today, the promisor must possess twelve or obtain financing. Suppose tying it up costs three over the life of our agreement. The gain from faithful performance falls from ten to seven, still positive. If the financing cost is eleven, the same opportunity yields minus one and will be declined under these assumptions. Perfectly deterring refusal would then fail to produce the exchange. The absence of a pledge is therefore ambiguous: it can reflect unwillingness to be bound, inability to finance the safeguard, or objection to the institution controlling its release. Reputation, staged performance, smaller advances, and outside guarantees can address different parts of that difficulty. Each must be assessed through its actual costs and incentives, without confusing wealth with trustworthiness.5

5. Financing costs are additional real or opportunity costs to the pledgor in this illustration: 10 − 3 = 7 and 10 − 11 = −1. This participation comparison is separate from the incentive comparison after the bond has been posted. No claim is made that the stated costs are observed market rates.

The hostage gives the promise a material history: something has been placed where a later choice can endanger it. But its success depends on a chain of decisions about valuation, custody, evidence, and release. More exposure can strengthen a commitment, burden an honest participant, or reward an abusive claimant. The useful bond is the one that makes the intended cooperation possible while keeping those consequences in view. It carries part of the work that an unsupported assurance could not do. It cannot decide in advance every circumstance in which the relationship will continue, change, or properly end. The next plate, The Gap, begins where that limit becomes visible: the parties have bound themselves, yet their agreement still leaves something to be settled.
