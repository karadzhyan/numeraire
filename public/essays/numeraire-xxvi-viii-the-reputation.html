Plate viii in print

The Reputation

the capstone: an eleventh-century coalition sustained long-distance trade with no court at all, on a single unwritten rule — cheat one of us, and you deal with none of us.

Long-distance trade asks a merchant to place his goods in the hands of a distant agent he cannot watch — and an eleventh-century coalition solved it with no court at all, by a single unwritten rule: cheat one of us and you deal with none of us, so that a good name, collectively kept, came to be worth more than any theft, and commerce ran on reputation where the law could not reach.

— the argument of this plate, after Avner Greif, 1993

The volume has anatomized the unwritten rules — convention, trust, reciprocity, and the grammar of the norm — but a doubt may linger: can such rules, unwritten and unenforced by any state, really do serious economic work, or are they merely the soft tissue around the hard skeleton of law and contract? Avner Greif answered the doubt with history, and the answer is the flagship of the volume. He reconstructed how an eleventh-century community of traders carried on long-distance Mediterranean commerce — among the most demanding forms of exchange, requiring a merchant to trust distant agents with his goods across months and seas — with almost no recourse to courts or law, relying instead on a structure of reputation and collective sanction built entirely from unwritten rules. The case shows that the most load-bearing rules of a market can be the very ones no one ever wrote down; and it gives the question the volume was set — which of the market's rules are written, and which merely understood — a deeper answer than it first appears to admit.

The trade Greif studied posed a hard problem. A merchant in one city who wished to sell his goods in another, across the Mediterranean, could not sail with every shipment; he had to entrust his goods to an agent in the distant port, who would sell them on his behalf and remit the proceeds. But the agent, once in possession of the goods and far from his principal's sight, faced a temptation as old as commerce itself: to embezzle — to report a low price and pocket the difference, or simply to abscond with the whole consignment. The merchant, months away and unable to verify what had truly happened in a foreign market, had little hope of catching the cheat, and less of recovering through a foreign court whose law he did not share and whose officers he could not command. This is the fundamental problem of long-distance trade, and it is a commitment problem of exactly the kind the volume of the game described: the agent cannot simply promise to be honest, because once the goods are in his hands, honesty is no longer in his interest, and both parties know it in advance. Without some solution to it, the trade cannot take place at all, and the gains it would have realized are lost to everyone.1

1. The problem: a merchant selling abroad must entrust goods to a distant agent who sells and remits. But the agent, holding the goods far from sight, is tempted to embezzle — underreport the price, or abscond. The principal, months away, can rarely catch him and cannot use a foreign court. This is a commitment problem (the volume of the game): the agent cannot credibly promise honesty, since once he holds the goods honesty no longer pays, and both know it. Unsolved, the trade — and its gains — simply does not happen.

The Maghribi traders solved it without law, through what Greif called a coalition. The merchants of this community — a group of Jewish traders originally from the Maghreb, the Muslim west, whose dealings across the Mediterranean are preserved in a vast trove of surviving letters — formed a tightly connected group within which information traveled freely. Letters carried news of who had dealt fairly and who had cheated, and a reputation, once made, was known to all. The decisive feature was the form the sanction took. An agent who cheated any single merchant was not merely dropped by that one victim; he was boycotted by the entire coalition, every member refusing thereafter to employ him. This is multilateral punishment, and it transforms the agent's calculation utterly. To cheat one merchant might win a one-time gain, but it would cost the agent his livelihood with all of them, for ever after; weighed against the whole future stream of honest employment, the single theft is no temptation at all. Honesty became the agent's own best policy — not from virtue, but because the collective sanction made the long-run reward of a good name exceed the short-run prize of a betrayal. The merchants needed no court; they had made cheating unprofitable through the structure of their own community.2

The power of the arrangement lay precisely in its being collective rather than individual, and the contrast is instructive. Suppose each merchant punished only the agent who had cheated him — a bilateral arrangement. Then an agent who had wronged one merchant would still be free to deal with all the others, losing only a single relationship; and as a merchant neared the end of his dealings with a given agent, the agent, foreseeing that the relationship would soon end anyway, would have less and less to lose by cheating, so that the discipline would fray exactly when it was most needed — in just the way the volume of the game found the cooperation of a repeated encounter unravelling as its final round came into view. Multilateral punishment closes both gaps at once. The cheat loses not one relationship but all of them together, a penalty heavy enough to deter even a large temptation; and because the coalition endures even as individual partnerships come and go, the agent always has a long future of employment at stake, so the discipline never frays. The unwritten rule — cheat one, and deal with none — drew its strength from binding the whole community into a single enforcer, and so achieved what no merely bilateral promise ever could.3

3. Its power was in being collective. Under bilateral punishment, a cheat loses only his victim and keeps every other relationship; and near a relationship's end he has little left to lose, so discipline frays just when most needed (the volume of the game: cooperation unravelling as the last round nears). Multilateral punishment closes both gaps — the cheat loses all relationships at once, and the coalition endures as partnerships come and go, so a long future is always at stake. Binding the whole community into one enforcer did what no bilateral promise could.

From the case Greif drew a large claim about what an institution really is. We are inclined to think of institutions as formal things — laws, courts, written rules, organizations with charters and officers — but the Maghribi coalition had none of these. What held it together was a system of shared belief and expectation: each agent believed that if he cheated, the news would spread and the coalition would shun him; each merchant believed that the others would join the boycott; and because all held these beliefs, the beliefs came true and the institution worked. An institution, in Greif's account, is at bottom a self-enforcing pattern of expectations about how others will behave — a structure of the same conditional, expectation-borne kind that the grammar of the previous plate described, only operating now at the scale of a whole economy. The formal apparatus of law, where it exists, is one way to stabilize such expectations; but it is not the only way, and it is not the deepest, for the formal rules themselves work only when people expect them to be enforced and expect others to comply. Beneath every written institution lies an unwritten one — a shared belief that makes the writing bite.4

4. Greif's large claim: an institution is not essentially formal. The coalition had no charter, officers, or enforcement arm — only shared belief: each agent believed cheating would spread and bring a boycott; each merchant believed the others would join; so the beliefs came true and the institution worked. An institution is a self-enforcing pattern of expectations (the grammar of the previous plate, at economy scale). Law is one way to stabilize expectations, not the deepest, for law too works only when people expect compliance. Beneath every written institution lies an unwritten one.

Greif sharpened the point by a comparison that has become famous. Set beside the Maghribi traders the merchants of Genoa, who faced the same problem of the distant agent but came from a different cultural starting point — individualist where the Maghribi were collectivist. The Genoese did not build a coalition of multilateral shunning; they moved instead toward formal and individualist solutions — the written contract, the court, legal enforcement, and in time the impersonal institutions of the Western legal tradition. Two communities, then, faced the same economic problem and arrived at two different institutional answers, each following from the beliefs and expectations the community already carried. And the long-run consequences, Greif argued, diverged profoundly. The Maghribi system, efficient within a closed and homogeneous group, did not scale: it depended on everyone knowing everyone, on information circulating reliably within a bounded community, and it could not easily extend to trade with strangers outside the coalition. The Genoese system of formal law, costlier to erect at first, could expand without natural limit, reaching to encompass dealings among strangers across an open and growing economy — and it was this impersonal, legal path, Greif suggested, that lay nearer the root of the eventual rise of the Western economy. The unwritten rules sustained sophisticated commerce; but the choice between unwritten and written foundations helped decide which economies could grow.5

5. Greif's famous comparison: beside the Maghribi set the Genoese, facing the same problem from an individualist starting point. They built not a shunning coalition but formal, individualist solutions — written contracts, courts, the Western legal tradition. Same problem, two answers, each following from inherited beliefs. Unwritten rules sustain commerce; the choice of foundation decides which economies grow.

The Maghribi case has been pressed and debated, and its limits mark the limits of the unwritten rule itself. Reputation mechanisms of the Maghribi kind work, scholars have observed, only under conditions: the community must be bounded enough that information about conduct reliably circulates and reputations are genuinely known; it must be stable enough that the future shadow of lost dealings is long; and its members must share enough — language, faith, expectation — to trust the news they receive and to coordinate their punishment. Where these conditions hold — in a guild, a close diamond district, an ethnic trading network, a tight commercial community — reputation can govern with great power, and such private orderings flourish to this day, often outperforming the formal law they quietly supplant. But where a market grows large and diverse, where traders are strangers who will never meet again and whose conduct cannot be tracked, the reputation mechanism strains and thins, and something more impersonal — law, courts, the formal contract of the volume of the contract — must take up the load it can no longer bear. Some historians have even questioned how purely reputational the Maghribi order truly was, finding in the records more recourse to formal authority than the cleanest version of the story allows. The unwritten rule is mighty within its reach; but its reach has an edge, and beyond that edge the written rule must begin.6

6. The case has limits that mark the limits of the unwritten rule. Reputation works only where the community is bounded enough for information to circulate, stable enough for a long future shadow, and homogeneous enough to trust news and coordinate punishment — a guild, a diamond district, an ethnic network. There it governs powerfully, often besting formal law. But where a market grows large and traders are strangers untracked, reputation thins, and law and the formal contract (the volume of the contract) must take the load. Some historians question how purely reputational the Maghribi order was. Beyond its edge, the written rule begins.

With the flagship the volume can answer the question the author set it — which of the market's rules are written, and which merely understood — and the answer inverts the natural assumption. We are inclined to think the written rules are the real ones, the foundation, and the unwritten rules a soft and dispensable supplement. Greif's traders show the opposite. The unwritten rules came first, and can stand alone, sustaining real and sophisticated commerce with no writing at all; the written rules, where they arise, are built atop a foundation of unwritten ones and depend upon them, for a law works only because people share the unwritten expectation that it will be obeyed and enforced. Most of what makes a market function — the trust that a deal will be honored, the reputation that rides on a man's conduct, the reciprocity that sustains a relationship through lean seasons, the convention that lets strangers anticipate one another, the countless understandings that no contract records and no court enforces — is unwritten, and it is this that does the silent, load-bearing work. The written contract and the court are the visible tip of the structure; the great mass of it, below the waterline, is merely understood. A market is governed far less by its laws than by its norms, and its laws themselves rest upon those norms.7

7. The answer inverts the assumption. We think the written rules are real and foundational, the unwritten a soft supplement; Greif shows the reverse. Unwritten rules came first and can stand alone; written rules rest atop them, for a law works only because people share the unwritten expectation that it will be enforced. Most of what makes a market function — trust, reputation, reciprocity, convention, the understandings no contract records — is unwritten and does the load-bearing work. The contract and court are the visible tip; the mass below the waterline is merely understood.

This is, in the end, both reassuring and unsettling. Reassuring, because it means that order does not wait upon the state: where law is weak or absent, communities can and do build their own unwritten institutions and trade and prosper within them, as they have done throughout history and across the world. Unsettling, because the unwritten foundation, as the grammar of the norm warned, is made of shared belief, and shared belief can erode — and when it does, no written rule can fully take its place. A society whose trust decays, whose reputations cease to matter, whose reciprocity curdles into a general suspicion, cannot simply legislate the loss away, for the very law it would pass to do so depends on the expectations that have failed. The unwritten rules are at once the cheapest and the most precious of a market's institutions: cheapest because they need no enforcement beyond mutual expectation, most precious because, once lost, they cannot be bought back at any price, but only slowly rebuilt by the long sediment of dealings kept and faith honored. What Greif's traders understood in their bones — that a good name, collectively kept and collectively enforced, is worth more than any contract — is the oldest wisdom of commerce, and it remains the foundation beneath the foundation.8

8. The lesson is reassuring and unsettling. Reassuring: order does not wait on the state — where law is weak, communities build unwritten institutions and prosper. Unsettling: the unwritten foundation is shared belief, which can erode, and then no law can replace it, for the law itself depends on the failed expectations. The unwritten rules are cheapest (needing only mutual expectation) and most precious (once lost, not bought back, only slowly rebuilt). That a good name, collectively kept, beats any contract is the oldest wisdom of commerce — the foundation beneath the foundation.

Which of the market's rules are written, and which merely understood? The merely understood are the deeper and the more numerous, and they bear the greater load. Avner Greif's eleventh-century traders carried on long-distance commerce across the Mediterranean with no court to enforce a contract and no state to punish a cheat, relying on an unwritten rule of pure collective power — cheat one of us, and deal with none of us — that made honesty its agents' own best interest, and built, out of shared belief and common sanction, an institution as effective as any law. The written rules of a market, where they exist, are the visible scaffolding; the unwritten rules — trust, reputation, reciprocity, convention, the thousand things merely understood — are the structure they are bolted to, and beneath every law lies the shared expectation that gives the law its force. So the norm closes the behaviors on the note the family began, with the discovery that the most powerful forces in an economy are often the least visible: that markets run on springs of conduct beneath the prices, on the strategic logic of the game and the unwritten constitution of the norm; and that a society's deepest economic wealth may be the one thing it cannot purchase — the faith, held quietly in common, that the others will keep faith too. With the norm, the table arrives at the last of its twenty-six elements, and the long argument reaches the ground on which economics itself must finally rest: not on the cold machinery of price and quantity alone, but on the trust, the reciprocity, and the unwritten understanding without which no machinery would turn at all.
