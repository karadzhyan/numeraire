Plate viii in print

The Plan

the deepest turn: the firm as planning inside the market, the visible hand where the invisible fails — and the discovery that market against plan was always a false choice, the firm a moving frontier between them set by the cost of coordinating.

Where the visible hand of management has come to coordinate the flow of goods, it has done so because it could do, more cheaply and more surely, what the invisible hand of the market could not — and so the planned firm has spread through the very heart of the market economy.

— the thesis of this plate, after Alfred Chandler, 1977

The volume has explained the firm — why it exists, where its boundaries fall, what it is made of, what it is to own one. The flagship steps back to the widest frame and asks what the firm is, set against the whole of the economy — and finds it to be a thing so familiar we have stopped seeing it, and so consequential that mistaking it has misled a century of argument. The firm is planning. Not planning as a metaphor, but planning in the fullest sense: the conscious, central, authoritative direction of resources, the very thing the market was supposed to make unnecessary — alive, pervasive, and indispensable, in the heart of the market economy itself.

We have said it before but not yet faced it: inside a firm, the price does not operate. The engineer is not paid a price by the marketing department for each drawing; the factory does not sell its output to the warehouse at a negotiated rate; the divisions do not bid against one another for the firm's capital in an open internal auction, or not usually, and where they seem to, it is a simulation the center controls. Resources move within the firm by direction — the manager's decision, the budget's allotment, the plan's command. Alfred Chandler called this the visible hand: the hand of conscious management, which in the great firms of the modern economy came to coordinate the flow of goods through production and distribution where once the invisible hand of the market had done it, and did so because it could do the job more cheaply and more reliably than the market could. Where the price families saw coordination achieved by no one, the firm achieves coordination by someone — a manager, a hierarchy, a plan. The firm is the visible hand made institution: the patch of the economy where coordination is conscious, deliberate, and directed.1

1. Alfred Chandler (The Visible Hand, 1977) documented how, in large modern enterprises, the "visible hand" of salaried managerial coordination replaced the "invisible hand" of market coordination across many activities, because administrative direction could coordinate high-volume production and distribution more efficiently. Within the firm, resources move by managerial direction, budget, and plan — not by internal prices. The firm institutionalizes conscious, deliberate coordination.

And here is the fact that should startle, once the firm is seen as planning: most of a market economy, by volume of activity, is not market at all. The open market — the buying and selling between separate firms and persons, coordinated by prices — is only the visible surface; beneath it, the great bulk of economic activity goes on inside firms, coordinated by management, untouched by any price. The transactions within a single great corporation — among its divisions, its plants, its departments, its workers — number in the billions and are governed, every one, by plan rather than by price; and such corporations, taken together, account for the larger part of all production. The economy we call a market economy is therefore, by the measure of where its activity actually happens, mostly a planned economy — an archipelago of planning so vast that the islands are larger than the sea. The market coordinates the spaces between the firms; the firms, which are most of the economy, coordinate themselves by plan.2

2. A striking implication: in a "market economy," most economic activity by volume occurs within firms — coordinated by managerial planning — not in open markets between them. The internal transactions of large corporations (among divisions, plants, departments) are governed by plan, not price, and such firms account for the bulk of production. By the measure of where activity happens, a market economy is substantially planned — the planned "islands" exceed the market "sea." The firm, Coase's island, is seen to dominate the map.
⁂

Now the inversion, and it overturns the defining economic argument of the twentieth century. That argument was posed as a war between two whole systems: the market economy, coordinating everything by price, against the planned economy, coordinating everything by central command — capitalism against socialism, the price against the plan — and history is said to have decided it, the command economies having collapsed and the market having won. But the firm reveals the dichotomy to have been false from the start. There is no pure market economy, and never was: every market economy is honeycombed with planning, riddled with firms, each a little command economy, and the greater part of its activity is planned, not priced. And there is no pure planned economy either, for even the most total command economy had black markets, side deals, and prices it could not suppress. Every real economy is a mixture of price and plan; the question was never which system to have, for we have always had both; the question is only where, within any economy, the boundary between them falls — how much is coordinated by price and how much by plan. The firm is the institution where that boundary is drawn, transaction by transaction, and the great ideological war was, at bottom, a quarrel over a line that every economy must draw somewhere and no economy can draw at either extreme.3

3. The inversion: the twentieth century's market-versus-plan dichotomy (capitalism vs. socialism, price vs. central command) was false. No economy is purely one or the other — market economies are pervaded by planning (firms), and command economies could never suppress all prices. Every economy mixes price and plan; the real question is where the boundary between them falls, not which "system" to adopt. The firm is where that boundary is drawn, transaction by transaction.

What sets the boundary — what decides whether a given activity is coordinated by price or by plan — is, as the whole volume has shown, the relative cost of the two modes. Where using the market is cheap — the good standard, the price easy to find, the contract simple, no specific assets, no hold-up — the price coordinates best, and the activity is bought across a market. Where using the market is dear — specific assets, complex and uncertain coordination, hold-up hazard, the constant need to adapt to the unforeseen — the plan coordinates best, and the activity is folded into a firm. The boundary falls where the two costs meet, and it falls in a different place for every activity: a firm buys its standard inputs on the market and plans its specialized core, prices what it can cheaply price and directs what it cannot. So the mixture of price and plan in any economy is not an ideological choice but a vast, decentralized optimization, each firm drawing its own boundary by the local comparison of costs, the whole pattern summing to the particular blend of market and planning that the economy's technology of coordination happens to make cheapest. The line between price and plan is drawn by the cost of coordinating — and it is drawn anew wherever that cost changes.4

4. The boundary between price and plan is set by their relative coordination costs (the volume's central logic): activities cheap to transact in the market (standard goods, simple contracts, no specificity) are priced; those costly to transact (specific assets, uncertainty, hold-up, constant adaptation) are planned within firms. Each firm draws its own boundary by local cost comparison; the economy-wide mix of market and planning is the aggregate of these decentralized choices, set by the prevailing technology of coordination, not by ideology.

But if the plan beats the price inside the firm, the old question returns with force: why not extend the plan to the whole economy, as the command economies tried — why not run the nation as one great firm? The answer, which is also the deepest reason those economies failed, is that planning's costs rise with its scale until they overwhelm it. The knowledge a plan requires — of what is needed where, of what each resource could best do, of the countless local circumstances that bear on every decision — is not given to any central mind, but is dispersed among millions, much of it tacit, fleeting, knowable only to those on the ground, in the moment of decision. A firm can gather and act on the knowledge of a department, even a vast corporation; but no center can gather the knowledge of a whole economy, and the attempt to plan everything must founder on the impossibility of knowing everything. This was Hayek's argument against central planning, and the firm does not refute it but bounds it: the price is the irreplaceable coordinator across the economy as a whole, precisely because it needs no center to know everything, summarizing in itself the dispersed knowledge no planner could collect — while the plan is the superior coordinator within the firm, where the scope is small enough that a manager can know what he must. The firm and the failed command economy are the same institution at different scales, and the difference between them is whether the planning stopped where knowledge ran out. The command economy's error was not to plan, for all firms plan; it was to plan past the point where the knowledge to plan well could be had.5

5. Why not plan the whole economy as one firm? Because planning's costs — above all the knowledge it requires — rise with scale. Hayek (1945) argued that the knowledge an economy needs is dispersed, tacit, and local, knowable only to those present at the particular time and place, and cannot be centralized; the price system coordinates precisely because it requires no one to know everything. The firm does not refute this but bounds it: plan where scope is small enough to know what is needed (the firm); price where it is not (the economy). The command economy planned past the limit of knowledge.
⁂

The picture of the firm as planning must be qualified, lest it suggest a little tyranny, for the firm's plan is unlike the command economy's in one decisive respect: it is bounded by exit. The command economy conscripted; its subjects could not lawfully leave. The firm commands only those who have agreed to be commanded, and who may, at any time, withdraw their agreement and walk away — the worker can quit, the supplier can refuse to renew, the manager can resign, and the owner himself answers to creditors who can call their loans and shareholders who can sell. The firm's authority, real as it is within its bounds, rests at every point on the consent of the governed, and that consent is held only so long as the firm offers each party better than his alternatives — better than his exit. So the firm is planning by consent, an island of voluntary hierarchy, its plan disciplined not from within, by the price it has switched off, but from without, by the market it sits in: by the standing freedom of all its members to leave for a rival, which is the Power's lesson returning in a new place. The plan inside the firm is real, but it is a plan its subjects have chosen and may unchoose, and that is what keeps it, unlike the command economy's, both efficient and free.6

6. The firm's planning is bounded by exit, unlike the command economy's conscription: the firm commands only consenting members, each free to leave (workers quit, suppliers decline, owners answer to creditors and shareholders). Its authority rests on offering each party better than their alternatives — disciplined from without by the market (the standing freedom to exit, the Power's lesson) rather than from within by the suppressed price. The firm is voluntary hierarchy: planning by consent, kept efficient and free by exit.

With the firm, the table has crossed a threshold, and it is worth naming. The families before this one studied the price: first its triumphs, in the wage and rent and rate and premium and spread, and then its four failures, in the power and externality and asymmetry and commons. This family studies what stands where the price cannot — the institutions, the built structures by which a market that the failures showed cannot stand on the price alone is raised into a working economy. And the firm is rightly the first of them, for it is the most general: the institution that arises wherever the price is too costly to use, replacing the failed or expensive market with conscious organization. It is, as an earlier plate showed, a machine for solving all four of the price's failures at once, by the simple expedient of replacing the market that failed with a plan. The institutions still to come are the other scaffolds: the contract, which binds the parties the firm gathers and the strangers it deals with; the money, which counts and stores the value the price expresses; the tax, which funds the public goods the commons could not; and the rule, which disciplines the power the market could not contest. Each is a way of doing what the price alone cannot — and the firm, the planning inside the market, is the first and the deepest of them.7

7. The firm opens the Institutions family, marking the table's turn from the price (its triumphs in the Prices, its limits in the Failures) to the institutions that scaffold a market unable to stand on the price alone. The firm is the most general institution — arising wherever the price is too costly, replacing the failed market with conscious organization (a single device against all four failures). The remaining members: the contract (binding parties), money (counting value), tax (funding public goods), rule (disciplining power) — each doing what the price cannot.

The firm, then, is not the opposite of the market but its complement and its creature: the planned coordination the market itself calls forth wherever its own price grows too costly to use. And the boundary between them is no fixed thing but a moving frontier, redrawn by every change in the cost of coordinating. When that cost falls — when information is cheap and contracts are easy, when search is instant and reputation travels and a platform can match a buyer to a seller in a keystroke — the market grows cheaper to use, the firm's advantage shrinks, and the firm dissolves: it outsources, it subcontracts, it franchises, it disaggregates into a cloud of contractors coordinated by the market it had replaced. When that cost rises, the firm grows and integrates. The gig platform and the subcontractor are the frontier in motion, the market reclaiming ground the plan had held, because the cost of using the market has fallen. So the firm is a living boundary, the moving line between the price and the plan, and where it falls in any age is one of the deepest facts about that age's economy — a fact set not by ideology but by the technology of coordination. The question the firm leaves is therefore not the dead one, markets or planning, but the living one: where, in this trade and this age, the line between them should fall, and which way the cost of coordinating is pushing it. And that question — where the market ends and the plan begins — is answered, like the fair price before it, not by the price alone but by the institutions we build; of which the firm is the first, and the contract, the money, the tax, and the rule the rest.8

8. The close: the firm is the market's complement and creature — planned coordination called forth where the price is too costly — and its boundary is a moving frontier set by the cost of coordinating. As that cost falls (cheap information, easy contracting, matching platforms), firms dissolve (outsourcing, subcontracting, the gig economy — the market reclaiming ground); as it rises, firms integrate. Where the price/plan line falls is a defining fact of any economy, set by the technology of coordination, not ideology. The firm is the first institution; the contract, money, tax, and rule follow.
