The Gold

commodity money and the gold standard; whether money must be anchored to a thing of worth or may float on confidence.

The anchor holds by making adjustment fall somewhere else.

— the argument of this plate, after the recovered Fetters

After the Ledger has made money a matter of promises, gold offers what appears to be an escape from promising. Here is something a minister cannot summon with an entry in an account, something that can be weighed, stored, and carried beyond the minister's reach. To define a currency as a weight of this metal is to give its holder a claim with an apparent outside test: bring the note and receive the gold. The earlier Fetters called that test both a discipline and a chain. The two descriptions name the same constraint from different positions. The question is what a promise to deliver metal secures, what it leaves unsettled, and who must adjust when the demand for redemption presses against the ability to honor it.

A gold standard fixes a monetary price of gold, not the price of everything gold can buy. Suppose one ounce is redeemable for twenty units and a basket costs ten. The ounce commands two baskets. If the basket later costs twenty while the redemption rule holds, the same ounce commands one. Convertibility has been maintained exactly; purchasing power has changed. The distinction matters because an anchor to one commodity is often asked to do the work of an anchor to all goods. Gold's scarcity can constrain monetary issue, and its other uses can support a demand for it, but neither property makes the relative value of gold against bread, shelter, and labor immutable. An economy can keep faith with the weight printed on its money and still experience inflation or deflation in the things its people need.1

1. The redemption example distinguishes a fixed gold price from a fixed consumption basket: 20/10 = 2 baskets, then 20/20 = 1. It is hypothetical and does not describe a historical price series.

The promise also requires an institution capable of redemption. Imagine a bank with 100 ounces and 4,000 units of redeemable liabilities at twenty units an ounce. Its metal can meet 2,000 units of claims immediately. It may hold sound loans for the rest and remain solvent, but it cannot deliver 200 ounces at once from a vault containing 100. That is not a peculiarity of paper money; it is the familiar distance between assets that mature and obligations payable now. Historically, the managed gold standard used bank deposits, notes, reserve currencies, and credit alongside metal, as Bordo's account emphasizes. The strict coin economy and the actual system of redeemable promises were different things. To invoke gold does not abolish the Run. It specifies the asset into which those waiting at the door wish to escape.2

2. Michael D. Bordo, “The Classical Gold Standard: Some Lessons for Today” (1981), pp. 2–7, distinguishes the pure standard from its managed forms. The vault example is original arithmetic: 100 × 20 = 2,000 immediately redeemable units.
⁂

Between countries, fixed gold definitions establish a corresponding exchange parity. In a hypothetical pair, an ounce redeemable for twenty crowns in one country and five pounds in another implies four crowns to the pound, before the cost of moving and converting the metal. If one country's payments drain its reserves, maintaining the promise requires some adjustment: obtaining funds, selling assets, attracting capital, or reducing the domestic expenditure that generates the outflow. The gold does not dictate which families surrender income or which firms postpone investment. Those consequences arrive through institutions and policy. The earlier Fetters is strongest at this point: the commitment that restrains opportunistic issue can also restrict an authority facing unemployment or a banking panic. What sounds like an automatic rule from the vault can be a difficult political choice outside it.3

3. The exchange example gives 20/5 = 4 crowns per pound before transaction costs. Bordo’s account, pp. 4–6, describes the roles of credit, capital flows, central banks, and departures from the simple adjustment rule.

The discipline is real precisely because the promise can become costly. If honoring it were always compatible with every domestic objective, it would bind nothing. But a costly promise is not necessarily an optimal one under every circumstance. Let an illustrative government compare two options during a crisis: defending a parity costs twelve units of lost output, while changing it costs seven units through disruption and damaged confidence. Within those assumptions, change is cheaper by five. Reverse the costs and the judgment reverses. Neither calculation gives an historical estimate or licenses effortless repudiation; it identifies what a serious defense of a monetary constitution must establish. The advocate of a binding rule must explain why its commitment benefit exceeds its adjustment cost. The advocate of discretion must explain how discretion will be prevented from becoming a repeated escape from every inconvenient promise.4

4. The crisis comparison is a decision problem, not a verdict on any particular abandonment of gold: 12 − 7 = 5; reversing the costs reverses the result. Both the disruption of changing the rule and the burden of maintaining it belong in the comparison.

Fiat money relocates that problem rather than resolving it by declaration. Without a fixed gold claim, the authority has room to respond to a changing demand for money; it also has room to issue more than people willingly hold at stable prices. The choice is therefore not between a substance wholly free of politics and a fiction wholly dependent on it. A gold regime needs institutions to maintain convertibility, while a fiat regime needs institutions that give its restraint credibility. Moreover, the rule chosen now shapes the adjustment people will expect later. A business choosing the currency of its debt, a worker accepting a long contract, and a saver holding balances all make plans around that expectation. The monetary constitution reaches beyond the day's price of gold into the arrangements by which tomorrow's goods are already claimed.5

5. The argument develops the recovered The Fetters and the Margin Standard plan. A metallic commitment and a discretionary monetary system each require institutions; their constraints and failure modes differ.

Gold answers one question firmly: what specific thing may the holder demand in exchange for this unit? It does not answer every question about the unit's value, the liquidity of its issuer, or the distribution of the costs of defending it. The original plate's two faces remain: discipline in the commitment, rigidity when the commitment meets a shock. A society choosing its money must decide which obligations to fix and which adjustments to permit, knowing that some burden will fall somewhere. The next plate turns to the official who makes those decisions after the anchor has ceased to settle them. The Governor inherits the task that gold could constrain without ever eliminating: keeping a common money credible while the economy using it continues to change.
