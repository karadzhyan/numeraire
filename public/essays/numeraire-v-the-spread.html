The Spread

the price of standing between

The spread is the price of standing between — the broker's cut, the bid and the ask, the dealer's turn, the platform's take. It is the fifth of the prices, and the one that completes the family, for it is the price of intermediation itself: what is kept by the one who stands between a buyer and a seller who do not meet directly. It looks like the plainest of tolls, a friction that competition and technology should grind toward nothing. Its twelve plates follow the spread from the ancient suspicion that the middleman is a parasite who produces nothing, through the modern theory of why intermediaries exist and what their spread is paid for — the search for a counterparty, immediacy, the risk of inventory, the menace of the better-informed — to the discovery that turns the whole family inside out: that there is no single price for a thing, but a price to buy and a price to sell, and the gap between them is the cost of trade itself, earned and levied in a proportion that, the volume's question runs, no one can cleanly divide.

The argument of the volume

Of the prices in this family, the spread is the one we are quickest to resent. The others are paid to someone who does something — who labors, lends, owns, bears risk; but the middleman, it has always seemed, merely inserts himself between two parties who could have dealt directly, and skims a cut for the trouble of standing in the way. The merchant who buys at one price and sells at another, the broker who takes a slice of every trade, the platform that levies a fee on every exchange it hosts — all appear to collect a toll on a transaction they did not make and a value they did not create.

What the economics of intermediation found is that the suspicion mistakes the service for its absence. The middleman is paid, the theory shows, for things the two parties cannot easily do for themselves: for finding a counterparty, so that neither must search; for standing ready to trade now, so that neither must wait; for holding the thing in inventory and bearing its price in the meantime; for vouching, across the gap of ignorance, that the goods are sound; and for absorbing the risk of trading against someone who knows more. The spread is the price of these, and at its purest the middleman is no parasite but the maker of the market — the one who, by quoting a price to buy and a price to sell, makes it possible to trade at all where otherwise there would be only two strangers unable to find or to trust each other.

And yet the spread is never only that. For the same position that lets the middleman provide the service — his standing in the one place both sides must pass — lets him charge more than the service costs, and the more completely he commands the passage, the larger the toll he can levy above the value he creates. The spread is thus, like the wage and the rent and the rate and the premium before it, a fused thing: part the earned price of a real service, part the levied toll of a controlling position, and the line between them, as the volume's subject has it, never quite where either side claims it lies. The discovery beneath it completes the family's lesson with a final turn upon the family's own subject — that there is no single price at which a thing trades, only a price to buy and a price to sell, so that the very notion of the price, which the wage and the rent and the rate and the premium all presuppose, dissolves, at the last, into the gap between two prices, and the one who stands in the gap.

The argument remains open. What follows is an attempt to fix its exact edges.

In closing

This volume took up the price we are least inclined to think deserved — the middleman's cut — and found in it both a vindication and a deeper unease. The spread is real, and at its core it is earned: the one who stands between is paid for finding, for waiting, for holding, for vouching, for bearing the risk of the better-informed — services the two parties cannot cheaply render themselves. The plates in print followed the spread from the ancient charge of parasitism to its rehabilitation as the price of the market's own existence.

Intermediation, the volume showed, exists because trade is not free. Buyers and sellers must find one another, and the middleman spares them the search; they must wait for a counterparty, and he stands ready now, selling immediacy; he holds in inventory what he buys before he sells it, and bears its price, earning a premium for the risk; and he trades, always, in the shadow of those who know more than he does, and must widen his quote to recoup from the many what he loses to the few. Each of these is a real cost, and the spread that covers them is the honest price of making a market where there would otherwise be none.

But the position that enables the service also enables the toll, and the two cannot be told apart in the number itself: the more completely the middleman commands the passage between the sides, the more he can levy above what the service is worth, and the question the volume cannot answer from the spread alone — when he earns his cut and when he merely levies it — is the same indeterminacy the whole family has taught. The wage is part worth and part power; the rent is part contribution and part position; the rate is part fact and part decision; the premium is part skill and part luck; and the spread is part service and part toll. In each, a payment presents itself as the just return to something done and conceals a portion that is merely the return to a place held. And the spread closes the family by turning its lesson upon the family's own word, for it reveals that there is no single price for a thing — only a price to buy and a price to sell, and the one who stands between them — so that the price, which every other element took as its subject, is itself the last of the things this table holds up to the light and finds to be less single, less given, and less innocent than it first appears.

If there is no one price but only a price to buy and a price to sell, and if the one who stands between earns part of the gap and merely levies the rest, then when we pay the spread, how much are we paying for the market to exist — and how much for permission to enter it?
