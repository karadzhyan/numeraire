The Decree

the law against the trust: a century's swing from the breaking of the great monopolies, through the creed that only the consumer's welfare counts, to the new alarm at the bigness of the platforms — and the question of what, if anything, concentration costs beyond price.

Mere bigness is itself a danger — to the small who must deal with it, to the rivals it can crush, and to the republic whose government it may come to overmatch — and a free people will not leave it unchecked.

— the concern of this plate, after Louis Brandeis, 1914

Where market power is sheltered by barriers and cannot be eroded by entry, one force remains that might check it: the law. For more than a century, the state has set itself against the power of the firm — breaking the great monopolies, forbidding the cartels, policing the conduct by which dominance is won and kept. This is antitrust, the deliberate public effort to preserve competition against the private drive to escape it; and its history is a long swing of the pendulum between two views of what, exactly, it is for.

The law was born of alarm at bigness. In the late nineteenth century, as railroads and oil and steel consolidated into vast combinations — the trusts — that could crush rivals, dictate to suppliers, and bend governments, a democratic public came to fear them not merely as overchargers but as powers, concentrations of private might that threatened the independence of the small producer and the integrity of the republic itself. Louis Brandeis gave this fear its fullest voice: bigness, he argued, was a danger in its own right, quite apart from any price it charged — a danger to the small businesses and farmers forced to deal with it, to the rivals it could destroy, and to the self-government of a people whose elected state might find itself overmatched by private corporations larger than itself. The first antitrust laws answered this alarm. They forbade the combinations and conspiracies that restrained trade, and they armed the state to break the trusts apart — and break them it did, shattering the great oil and tobacco combinations into pieces. The original purpose was structural and political: to keep economic power dispersed, because dispersed power was the condition of a free economy and a free polity alike.1

1. U.S. antitrust (the Sherman Act, 1890; Clayton Act, 1914) arose from public alarm at the trusts — vast combinations in oil, steel, and railroads. Louis Brandeis articulated the structural-political concern: bigness as a danger in itself — to small producers, to rivals, and to democratic self-government — independent of prices charged. Early enforcement broke up combinations (Standard Oil, American Tobacco, 1911). The original aim was to keep economic power dispersed, for both economic and political freedom.

Against this structural tradition there arose, in the latter half of the twentieth century, a powerful counter-doctrine that would remake antitrust entirely. The Chicago school argued that the law had lost its way in chasing bigness for its own sake, and that it should have one aim and one only: the welfare of the consumer, measured by price and output. Bigness, on this view, is not a danger but often a virtue — large firms are usually large because they are efficient, and to break them up is to punish success and deny consumers the low prices that scale affords. Most of the practices the structural tradition had condemned as anticompetitive — the mergers, the exclusive dealings, the predatory threats — were, the Chicago economists argued, either efficient or self-defeating, and the market, left alone, would correct most monopoly without the state's clumsy hand. Antitrust should therefore intervene rarely, and only on proof of harm to consumers — higher prices, lower output — not on the mere fact of concentration. This doctrine, rigorous and elegant, swept the field: for a generation, antitrust contracted to the policing of price, bigness was no longer suspect, and the great structural project of dispersing power was largely abandoned.2

2. The Chicago school (Bork's The Antitrust Paradox, 1978; Posner) reoriented antitrust around a single goal — consumer welfare, measured by price and output. On this view bigness usually reflects efficiency, scale benefits consumers, and most condemned practices are efficient or self-correcting, so antitrust should intervene rarely and only on proof of consumer harm, not concentration as such. This doctrine dominated U.S. antitrust for a generation, narrowing it to price effects and setting aside the structural-political tradition.
⁂

In recent years the pendulum has begun to swing back, driven by the rise of a kind of firm the Chicago doctrine seemed unable to grasp: the great digital platform. A handful of these — in search, in social connection, in online commerce — have grown to dominate their markets utterly, and yet they often charge their users nothing, or even pay them in free service, so that the consumer-welfare test, which looks for high prices, finds no harm and no occasion to act. To a new generation of critics, the neo-Brandeisians, this revealed the poverty of the consumer-welfare standard: it had narrowed antitrust to price while the real harms of concentration had moved elsewhere — to the foreclosure of rivals denied access to the platform, to the surveillance of users whose data is the true price, to the political power of firms that shape discourse and overmatch governments, to the fragility of an economy dependent on a few indispensable chokepoints. These critics call for a return to Brandeis: a revival of the structural concern, a willingness to act against bigness and concentration themselves, before any rise in price, on the ground that the old broad dangers — to rivals, to suppliers, to democracy — are real and present again. The argument is fierce and unsettled, the law itself now contested between the consumer-welfare standard and the structural revival.3

3. The neo-Brandeisian movement (Lina Khan's "Amazon's Antitrust Paradox," 2017) argues the consumer-welfare standard fails the platform age: dominant platforms often charge low or zero prices (so the price test finds no harm) while inflicting other harms — foreclosing rivals, surveilling users (data as the real price), wielding political power, creating systemic fragility. It urges reviving Brandeis's structural concern — acting against concentration itself, before price effects. Antitrust's purpose is now actively contested.

Beneath the swinging pendulum lies a question the law has never finally settled: what is competition policy actually for? If its aim is only the consumer's pocketbook, then the Chicago standard follows — act only where price rises, tolerate the bigness that lowers it, trust the market to correct the rest. If its aim is broader — the dispersal of power, the protection of the small producer, the openness of markets to entry, the health of a self-governing republic — then the structural tradition follows, and concentration itself becomes a proper target, price or no price. The two are not merely different tests but different visions of why a society polices market power at all: the one economic and narrow, prizing efficiency above all; the other political and broad, prizing the dispersal of power and willing to pay something in efficiency for it. Most of the disagreement over particular cases — this merger, that platform, this practice — is at bottom a disagreement over this prior question, which is not an economic question at all but a question about what kind of economy, and what kind of polity, a free people wishes to have.4

4. The deep dispute is over antitrust's purpose: a narrow economic aim (consumer welfare and efficiency — the Chicago view) versus a broad political-economic aim (dispersing power, protecting small producers and market openness, safeguarding democracy — the Brandeisian view). These are competing visions, not just competing tests; the narrow prizes efficiency, the broad accepts some efficiency cost for dispersed power. Most case-level disputes reduce to this prior, partly non-economic question.

Whatever its aim, the law against market power labors under hard limits, which temper any hope placed in it. It is slow: a major antitrust case takes years, often a decade, by which time the market may have moved past the remedy. It is clumsy: courts and agencies must judge, from the outside, whether a practice is efficient or exclusionary, a merger benign or dangerous, and they err in both directions — striking at efficient firms, missing real monopolies. It is captured: the firms it polices are wealthy and organized, and they shape the law through lobbying, litigation, and the revolving door, while the diffuse public it protects can barely organize at all — the very logic of collective action by which concentrated interests defeat broad ones. And it confronts, in tacit collusion and in the network monopoly, forms of power it was not built to grasp. The law is a real check on market power, and at times a decisive one — the broken trusts, the forbidden cartels, the blocked mergers are no illusions — but it is a blunt and partial instrument, and the power it cannot reach is left to the slower correctives of entry and invention.5

5. Antitrust's practical limits: it is slow (cases take years, markets move on); clumsy (courts judge efficiency vs. exclusion from outside, erring both ways); subject to capture (regulated firms are concentrated and organized, the protected public diffuse — the collective-action logic of The Commons); and ill-fitted to tacit collusion and network monopolies. It is a real but blunt and partial check; power beyond its reach falls to entry and innovation (The Gale).

The decree is the law's long answer to market power: the trust broken, the cartel forbidden, the merger blocked, the conduct policed — a century's effort to preserve by public force the competition that private firms forever seek to escape. Its history is the swing of a pendulum between two purposes — the structural alarm at bigness that founded it, the consumer-welfare creed that narrowed it, and the structural revival that now contests it again — and beneath that swing lies an unsettled question about what a society polices power for at all. It is a real check and a limited one: decisive against some forms of power, helpless before others, and always slow. Entry erodes power where barriers permit; the law breaks it where they do not; and the longest view, still to come, holds that invention will overthrow it in time regardless. But beneath entry and law and invention alike lies the single fact the flagship now reaches — that all of them are ways of giving the trapped party somewhere else to go, and that market power, at bottom, is nothing but the absence of that somewhere. The next plate is the flagship, and the bottom of the matter: power as the want of an alternative, and a fair price as only ever what the freedom to walk away compels.
