Plate xii in print

The Tide

globalization and the world labor market; whether opening to trade and offshoring lowered the labor share of the rich economies.

A larger field of production changes both the product and the alternatives of its claimants.

— the argument of this plate, after Grossman and Rossi-Hansberg, and Elsby, Hobijn and Şahin

A factory can cross a border while its owners remain at home. The wages formerly paid in one country are then paid in another, and the profits may return to the same shareholders. This movement gives the question of the share an international dimension. The measure still needs a boundary: the labor share of domestic production compares domestic compensation with domestic value added. Foreign wages enter another country's account, while residents' foreign investment income belongs in the distinction between domestic product and national income. A lost job, a lower price and a larger dividend can arise from the same reorganization and reach different households. Opening to trade can therefore change the size of the product, the location of its production and its division. Each change requires its own explanation before the movement of a national share can be attributed to globalization.1

1. Elsby, Hobijn and Şahin, The Decline of the U.S. Labor Share (2013), §III.C, pp. 33–35, defines the relevant domestic income net of imported inputs. Piketty and Zucman, Wealth and Inheritance in the Long Run (2015), §15.2.1, pp. 1308–1310, and §15.5.2, pp. 1347–1349, distinguishes domestic capital from national wealth and the corresponding foreign income.

Moving a task abroad changes both sides of the domestic share's fraction. In an illustrative business with no intermediate purchases, sales of a hundred finance sixty in domestic compensation and forty in other domestic factor income. Suppose a task that cost twenty in domestic wages is replaced by an imported input costing ten, with sales and every other payment held fixed. Domestic compensation falls to forty. Domestic value added falls to ninety, because the ten paid for the imported input is deducted from sales; the remaining domestic income rises to fifty. Labor's share falls from sixty percent to forty divided by ninety, about forty-four percent. This is a stipulated accounting comparison, with no response by output, prices or displaced workers. It shows a possible route into the statistic. Counting the import payment as domestic value added would describe a different and erroneous fraction.

The productivity gain from obtaining a task more cheaply can also expand demand for the work that remains. Grossman and Rossi-Hansberg make this channel explicit in their 2008 theory of offshoring. Firms combine tasks, some performed domestically and others abroad; making existing offshore tasks cheaper can lower the cost of using the associated domestic factor. Under specified competitive conditions, that gain can raise its wage. Their analysis also identifies relative-price and labor-supply effects, which can offset the productivity effect. Its direction therefore cannot be read from the fact that a task has moved. In the preceding illustration, the ten saved could finance expansion, appear in lower prices or remain in the residual; the fixed-output comparison deliberately leaves those responses out. If compensation and domestic value added instead grew in the same proportion, the domestic labor share would remain constant even as its numerator rose.2

2. Grossman and Rossi-Hansberg, Trading Tasks: A Simple Theory of Offshoring, American Economic Review 98 (2008), introduction and §§I–II.A, pp. 1978–1988; conclusion, pp. 1995–1996. The productivity result belongs to their specified technology and equilibrium; relative-price and labor-supply effects enter separately. Their model's restrictions and the sectoral pattern of gains matter. The business accounts above are independent illustrations.

The ability to move production can affect a bargain before production moves. Suppose a firm and its workforce can divide a hundred, the workers can obtain twenty elsewhere, and the firm initially has no alternative return. If they divide the gain above their respective alternatives equally, the workers receive sixty and the firm forty. Now give the firm a credible outside option worth twenty, leaving the workers' alternative and the joint hundred unchanged. Equal division of the remaining gain gives fifty to each. The shift follows from the alternative available on disagreement under the stipulated bargaining rule. A relocation threat that yields nothing after its costs leaves the first calculation intact. Workers may also gain alternatives through trade. The effect depends on whose options improve, by how much, and on the institutions through which those options influence the bargain.3

3. The bargaining calculation is this plate's illustration: worker receipts are 20 + (100 − 20 − 0)/2 = 60, then 20 + (100 − 20 − 20)/2 = 50. It develops the outside-option inquiry of The Conflict. Grossman and Rossi-Hansberg (2008), p. 1983, note 13, expressly separates its competitive task-trade model from bargaining or monopsony channels. The two mechanisms require different assumptions.

Adjustment can leave particular workers and places bearing losses that an aggregate gain does not repay to them. Autor, Dorn and Hanson studied changes in American local labor markets over 1990–2007, relating their initial industry specialization to subsequent exposure to Chinese imports. Their 2013 paper used Chinese import growth in other high-income markets to help isolate the supply-driven component, subject to the identification assumptions they state. They reported lower employment and earnings, and greater use of transfer programs, in more exposed local markets. This is evidence about differentiated local outcomes in a particular historical episode. It does not by itself calculate the national labor share or net national welfare. The distinction matters for the person whose job disappears: benefits to consumers or owners elsewhere do not constitute a replacement wage, and the time required to find another livelihood belongs in the account of adjustment.4

4. Autor, Dorn and Hanson, The China Syndrome: Local Labor Market Effects of Import Competition in the United States, American Economic Review 103 (2013), introduction, pp. 2121–2125, and §VIII, pp. 2158–2159. The authors distinguish local adjustment costs from the aggregate gains predicted by trade theory. Their instrument requires assumptions about supply and demand shocks; this plate reports the historical study rather than a universal or current estimate.

The evidence directed at the labor share is suggestive about trade while remaining narrower than the whole question. In their 2013 study of the United States, Elsby, Hobijn and Şahin found that industries with greater increases in import exposure tended to have larger declines in payroll shares. They explicitly declined to interpret that cross-industry relationship as causal. They also left the underlying channel open: substitution toward imported tasks and the pressure on industry wage premiums are different explanations. The findings support investigating globalization alongside technology, market structure and institutions; they do not assign every decline in every rich economy to the opening of trade. Nor would a lower share in one country imply an equal increase in another. The composition of production changes in both, and wages abroad can rise while remaining a smaller fraction of the value produced there.5

5. Elsby, Hobijn and Şahin (2013), complete §IV.C, pp. 39–43, especially pp. 40–41 on alternative channels and pp. 42–43 on the correlation and its causal limit. The paper discusses how the share could decline in both origin and destination countries; it does not establish that outcome for all countries. No regression coefficient is treated here as an identified global effect.

The tide changes the alternatives available to households, firms and states, and its benefits require a distribution as surely as its costs do. Cheaper inputs can enlarge what can be produced; movement can weaken one claimant's position and strengthen another's; displaced work can carry a loss through years of a person's life. A national share gathers part of this history into one fraction. The account of its causes must preserve the people, institutions and geographical boundaries that the fraction compresses. The volume began with the division of a product. It ends with the conditions under which people can claim that product, retain it and take it elsewhere. The next inquiry turns toward the decisions through which they do so: how an available alternative becomes a choice.
