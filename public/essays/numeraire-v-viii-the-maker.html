Plate viii in print

The Maker

the market-maker who quotes two prices where we imagined one, and the discovery that there is no single price — only a bid, an ask, and a gap that is the cost of trade itself, earned and levied in proportions no one can cleanly divide.

There is no one price at which a thing trades, but a price to buy and a price to sell; the gap between them is what it costs to trade at all, and it is the maker's wage for making the market exist.

— the thesis of this plate, after Fischer Black, 1971

Every plate so far has anatomized the spread — what it pays for, what it protects against — while leaving unexamined the figure at its center: the market-maker, the one who quotes the two prices. The volume's question, and the family's last lesson, turn on what he really is. The ancient charge called him a parasite, skimming a cut of a value others made. The modern theory has shown him, plate by plate, to be something nearer the opposite. But the truth the flagship must state is stranger than either, and it begins with a fact so familiar we have stopped seeing it: that he quotes two prices, not one.

We speak, throughout economics and throughout this table, of the price of a thing — as if each good carried a single number, its value, at which it changes hands. The market-maker's quote reveals that this is a fiction. What exists, at any moment, is not one price but two: a price at which you can sell now, the bid, and a higher price at which you can buy now, the ask. There is no transaction at the number in between — the midpoint, the supposed true price, is an average of two real figures and is itself the price of no actual trade. Every real exchange happens at the bid or at the ask, never at the mythical single price; and the gap between them, the spread, is not a deduction from a pre-existing value but the very thing that stands where we imagined a single value to be. The price of a thing, looked at closely, is not a point but an interval — and the middleman lives in the interval.1

1. The central inversion: there is no single price; a market shows a bid (the price to sell) and an ask (the price to buy), and the "midpoint" between them is the price of no actual trade. Every real transaction occurs at bid or ask; the spread is not a deduction from a true price but occupies the place where a single price was imagined. The price is an interval, not a point.

Seen this way, the market-maker is not a charge upon the market but its author. There is no pre-existing price for him to skim a margin from; rather, by committing to buy at the bid and sell at the ask, he brings the two prices into being, and with them the possibility of trading at all. Before he quotes, there are only would-be buyers and would-be sellers who may never find one another, never agree, never trust the deal; after he quotes, anyone can trade at once, at a known price, with a counterparty always present. The spread is the price of that — of the market's existence as a place where one can reliably transact — and the maker earns it by underwriting the whole arrangement with his standing readiness, his inventory, and his exposure to those who know more. The despised middleman turns out to be the maker of the market: not a tollbooth on the road, but the builder of the road itself.2

2. The maker constitutes the market rather than taxing a pre-existing one: by committing to bid and ask he creates the two prices and the possibility of immediate, reliable trade where before there were only unmatched, mutually suspicious would-be traders. The spread is the price of the market's existence, underwritten by his readiness, inventory, and informational exposure (plates iii–v).
⁂

Here the inversion the table exists to make stands complete, and it overturns the very charge the volume opened with. The middleman appeared to be a parasite levying a toll on a value he did not create; he turns out to be the maker of the market in which value can be realized at all, paid for a service — immediacy, warehousing, the absorption of informational risk — without which the trade could not occur. The figure and the ground exchange places as they did across the whole family: what looked like an exaction upon exchange is the very thing that makes exchange possible. And yet — and this is where the flagship refuses the easy vindication — the reversal is not the end of the story, because the maker's position is double-edged. The same standing-between that lets him make the market lets him tax it; the builder of the only road owns the only road.3

3. The inversion completes and rhymes with the family: what seemed an exaction on exchange is what makes exchange possible (the figure/ground swap of The Wage, The Rent, The Rate, The Premium). But unlike a simple vindication, the maker's constitutive role is double-edged — the position that lets him make the market also lets him tax it.

For the market-maker stands, by definition, in the one place both sides must pass, and a position of that kind, the volume on rent established, is a source of income all its own. Where many makers compete, the spread is driven down toward the cost of the services it covers, and the toll component vanishes: the maker earns, and only earns. But where one maker, or a colluding few, command the passage — the sole exchange, the dominant dealer, the platform everyone must use — the spread can be held far above the cost of the service, and the excess is a rent, a toll levied on access to the market by whoever controls the market. The same number, the spread, now contains two things of utterly different character: a fee, earned by providing the immediacy and bearing the risk; and a rent, levied by occupying the indispensable position. And from the spread alone, one cannot tell which is which.4

4. The toll component: the maker occupies a chokepoint position (compare The Rent, plate viii, The Position), which yields rent. Competition among makers drives the spread toward the cost of services (pure fee); monopoly or collusion holds it above cost (the excess is rent, a toll on access). The spread fuses an earned fee and a levied rent, indistinguishable in the number itself.

This is the volume's question, and now its answer. When does the middleman earn his cut, and when does he merely levy it? The economics of the spread replies that he almost always does both at once, and that the line between the earned fee and the levied rent is not merely hard to find but, from the spread itself, impossible to draw — for the fee and the rent are the same money, collected in the same act, by the same person, in proportions that depend on the unobservable degree of his market power and the unobservable cost of his service. One can say that more competition shifts the mix toward fee and more dominance toward rent; one can sometimes estimate the mix in the aggregate, across many markets. But for any particular spread — this dealer's, this platform's, this broker's — the question of how much was earned and how much levied has no answer the spread can give, because the earned and the levied leave the identical trace: a margin, collected by the one in the middle. The line is, as the volume's subject warned, never quite where either side claims, because in the single number it is not, in truth, anywhere at all.5

5. The open question answered: the middleman almost always both earns and levies, and the line between fee and rent cannot be drawn from the spread itself, since both are the same money collected in the same act, their proportions turning on the unobservable degree of market power and cost of service. Competition shifts the mix toward fee, dominance toward rent; but for any single spread the decomposition is unidentifiable — earned and levied leave the identical trace, as do skill and luck in The Survivor.
⁂

The careful statement matters here as everywhere, for the cynical reading and the credulous one are both wrong. It is not true that the spread is mere extraction — the services are real, and in competitive markets the spread is driven down to pay for them and no more, a genuine and modest price for a genuine and valuable thing. Nor is it true that the spread is pure earned fee — where position is commanding, a part of it is rent, a toll on access that no service justifies, and the holder of the position has every incentive to call the whole of it earned. The truth is the fusion, and the indeterminacy of the fusion: the spread is both, in a proportion that shifts with competition and that the number conceals. The practical wisdom that follows is neither to abolish the middleman nor to trust him, but to widen the road — to multiply the makers, lower the barriers to standing between, and so compete the rent out of the spread until what remains is only the fee. The cure for the toll is not the destruction of intermediation but the competition that turns rent back into service.6

6. The honest edge: the spread is neither pure extraction nor pure earned fee but a fusion whose proportions shift with competition and which the number conceals; the holder of position will claim it is all earned. The remedy is not abolishing intermediaries but multiplying them — competition drives the spread toward the cost of service and competes out the rent.

And so the fifth price completes the family by turning its lesson upon the family's own word. The wage, the rent, the rate, and the premium were each a price, and each, examined, proved a fused and partly hidden thing. The spread is the price of the very act of pricing — what it costs to convert a thing into a transaction — and in examining it we find that the word the whole table has leaned on, the price, names something that, at the close, is not there. There is no single price at which a thing trades; there is a bid and an ask, and the one who stands between, quoting both. The wage was the price of labor, the rent the price of position, the rate the price of time, the premium the price of risk — but each of those prices, too, is in truth a spread, a gap between what one side will pay and what the other will take, with someone, somewhere, standing in the gap. The table set out to anatomize prices, and its first family ends by discovering that a price is not a number the world hands us but an interval we negotiate across, and that in every interval there stands a maker, earning and levying in proportions we cannot see.7

7. The family's subject turned on itself: the spread is the price of pricing — the cost of converting a thing into a transaction — and examining it dissolves "the price" into a bid, an ask, and the maker between. Every price (wage, rent, rate, premium) is itself a spread, a gap between what one side will pay and the other take, with an intermediary in the gap. A price is an interval negotiated across, not a number given.

With the spread, the first of the table's six families is complete, and its unity can be stated. The five prices — the wage of labor, the rent of position, the rate of time, the premium of risk, the spread of standing between — are not five unrelated tolls but five faces of a single discovery: that a price, any price, is the residue of a contest, dressed as a fact; that each conceals, beneath its appearance of just desert, a portion that is power, or position, or decision, or luck, or the rent of the indispensable place; and that the work of an honest economics is to hold that portion up to the light, in the full knowledge that it can rarely be cleanly separated from what was truly earned. The Prices are the table's first family because they are the most familiar, the numbers we meet every day and trust the most; and the lesson of the family, now complete, is that the more ordinary a price appears, the more it has to hide. The remaining families take up what the prices were always about — the quantities they move, the failures they conceal, the institutions that set them, the totals they sum to, and the minds that meet across them — but the prices come first, and end here, with the one who stands between, and with the discovery that there was never a single price for him to stand between at all.8

8. The family complete: the five prices are five faces of one discovery — a price is the residue of a contest dressed as a fact, concealing a portion that is power, position, decision, luck, or the rent of place, rarely cleanly separable from what was earned. The Prices, the most familiar family, teach that the more ordinary a price seems, the more it hides. The remaining families — the quantities, the failures, the institutions, the aggregates, the behaviors — build on this foundation.
