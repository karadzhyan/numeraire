Plate viii in print

The Burden

the deepest turn: the law names who pays, but the market decides who bears — the state can tax, but it cannot choose whom to burden.

Lay a tax where you will; trace it through the economy, and you find it has come to rest not where the law set it, but on whatever cannot move — and a corporation, being no one, bears nothing: its people do.

— the thesis of this plate, after Arnold Harberger, 1962

The volume has shown what a tax does to a market — it drives a wedge, destroying trades and surplus; how the burden divides between the two parties to a trade — by their elasticities, not the law; how a tax may be best designed — balancing efficiency against equity; and the one tax that mends rather than wounds — the corrective. The flagship asks the question all of these have circled, the question the author sets at the volume's center: who really bears a tax? And it finds the widest gap in all of policy between intention and effect — that the party the law names as payer is rarely the party that bears the burden, because the burden, once imposed, slides through prices to settle where the law never intended. The state can choose whom to tax; it cannot choose whom to burden.

Distinguish two things confused in every tax debate. The legal, or statutory, incidence of a tax is who the law names as the payer — who writes the check, who remits the tax to the state. The economic incidence is who actually bears the burden — whose real income falls because of the tax. We assume these are the same: that the person taxed is the person burdened. They are almost never the same. For the person who writes the check does not simply absorb the tax; he changes his behavior in response — raises his price, lowers his offer, buys less, sells less — and in doing so passes some or all of the burden to others. The tax is levied at one point but borne at another; the check-writer is a collection point, not necessarily the bearer. Who really pays is an economic question, not a legal one — and the law, which can name the payer, cannot name the bearer.1

1. Legal (statutory) incidence is who the law names as payer — who remits the tax. Economic incidence is who actually bears it — whose real income falls. They are almost never the same: the check-writer changes behavior (raises price, lowers offer) and passes the burden on. The mechanism, the plate on the shift showed, is the price. A tax on a sale raises the price the buyer pays and lowers the price the seller receives, and the burden divides between them in proportion to their relative inelasticities — to which side is less able to escape. The side that can readily change its behavior, the elastic side, escapes the tax by changing it: the buyer who can do without buys less, the seller who can sell elsewhere does, and so they shed the burden onto the side that cannot. The burden settles on the inelastic — on whoever is stuck, whoever cannot reduce his exposure to the taxed transaction. So incidence is governed by elasticity, which is a fact about the market, not the law: the burden flows like water to the lowest point, settling on the most inelastic party, finding its level regardless of where the tax was poured in. And the burden does not stop at the two parties to the taxed trade; it ripples outward, the way a weight pressed on one part of a web tightens strands far away, the tax on one market raising and lowering prices and wages and returns across many, until it comes to rest on parties the legislator never named or imagined.2

2. The mechanism (The Shift): a tax raises the buyer's price and lowers the seller's; the burden divides by relative inelasticity, the elastic side escaping (changing behavior), the inelastic side stuck and bearing it. Incidence is set by elasticity — a market fact, not a legal one. The burden flows "like water to the lowest point," settling on the most inelastic, and ripples beyond the taxed trade through many markets (prices, wages, returns) to parties the legislator never named.

The principle overturns the surface of every tax. The payroll tax, half levied on the employer and half on the worker, is in economic truth borne almost entirely by the worker, whose wage is lower by nearly the whole tax — for labor is inelastic, and the employer passes the tax back in lower pay. The corporate tax, levied on the corporation, is borne by some mix of its workers, in lower wages, its customers, in higher prices, and its shareholders, in lower returns — but never by the corporation itself, which is not a person who can bear anything, only a nexus through which the burden passes to people. Here Arnold Harberger's analysis was decisive: tracing the corporate tax through the whole economy, he showed it comes to rest not on some abstract entity but on the owners of capital broadly, or on labor, depending on how readily capital and labor can move — for a corporation, being no one, bears nothing, and its tax, like every tax, finds its way to people who can. The tax on the landlord may be borne by the tenant, where housing is scarce and tenants are stuck, or stick to the landlord, where the land is fixed. In every case, the question who pays, in the legal sense, is nearly irrelevant to who bears, in the economic sense — and the political fight over whom to name as payer is, very often, a fight over an illusion, since the naming does not decide the bearing.3

3. The principle overturns the surface of every tax. Payroll tax (split employer/worker): borne mostly by the worker (inelastic labor; wages fall). Corporate tax: borne by some mix of workers, customers, shareholders — never "the corporation," which is no person, only a nexus passing the burden to people. Harberger (1962) traced the corporate tax through the whole economy to capital broadly or to labor, by their mobility. Landlord tax: tenant or landlord, by elasticity. Legal "who pays" is nearly irrelevant to economic "who bears"; the political fight over naming the payer is often an illusion.

Here the inversion turns to its sharpest point. The tax seems the most direct exercise of the state's power: the state simply commands, by law, that this person shall pay this sum — no exchange, no bargain, no consent, only the naked exercise of authority. And yet the tax is the policy whose true effect most escapes the state's control. For the state can compel the payment — it can name who writes the check, and enforce it — but it cannot compel the bearing; the moment the tax is imposed, the market reallocates the burden through prices, by the iron logic of elasticity, to wherever it will settle, and the legislator's intent about who should bear it counts for nothing. The state can choose whom to tax; it cannot choose whom to burden. This is the widest gap in all of policy between intention and effect — that the most authoritative-seeming act of the state, the bare command to pay, has an incidence the state does not determine and often does not even foresee. The hand that levies the tax does not place the burden; the market places it, and the market is not taking orders.4

4. The inversion: the tax seems the most direct exercise of state power — a bare command to pay, without exchange or consent. Yet its true effect most escapes the state's control: the state can compel payment (name and enforce the check-writer) but not bearing (the market reallocates the burden by elasticity, the legislator's intent counting for nothing). The state can choose whom to tax, not whom to burden — the widest gap between intention and effect. The hand that levies the tax does not place the burden; the market does.

The lesson is both deflationary and constructive. Deflationary: much of the politics of taxation — the endless fights over whether to tax the corporation or the individual, the employer or the worker, the seller or the buyer — is a contest over legal incidence that does not determine economic incidence, and so is often beside the point; to tax the rich by taxing the things they sell may, through shifting, tax the poor who buy. Constructive: if one wants a tax to be borne by a particular party, one cannot achieve it by naming that party as payer; one must instead find a tax whose economic incidence — given the elasticities — falls where one wishes. The only tax that surely falls on its intended bearer is a tax on something perfectly inelastic, which cannot shift: a tax on land, whose supply is fixed, borne wholly by the owner, as the plate on the land will show; or a lump-sum tax on the person himself, borne by him because he cannot change his personhood. Everything else shifts — and the art of incidence is to tax the inelastic if you want the burden to stay put, knowing that everything elastic will pass the burden along to whoever, in the end, cannot.5

5. The lesson is deflationary and constructive. Deflationary: much tax politics fights over legal incidence (corporation vs individual, employer vs worker) that does not determine economic incidence — taxing "the rich" via what they sell may tax the poor who buy. Constructive: to make a tax fall on an intended bearer, one cannot name them as payer; one must find a tax whose incidence (given elasticities) falls there. Only a tax on the perfectly inelastic surely stays put: land (borne wholly by the owner — The Land) or a lump-sum tax on the person. Everything elastic shifts.

Step back, now, from incidence to the tax's purpose, for the family's sake. Whoever bears it, the tax does something no other institution can: it funds the things the market cannot fund itself. The volume on the commons showed that public goods — defense, the rule of law, basic research, clean air — are underprovided by the market, because each person can free-ride on the contributions of others; and the only general remedy at scale is the tax, the compulsory contribution that removes the choice to free-ride. So the tax funds the public goods the commons could not, and — this is its place in this family — it funds the very institutions on which the market rests: the courts that enforce the contract, the power that backs the money, the protection of the property that makes exchange possible. The tax, which we resent as a burden on the market, is in this light the price of the market itself: there is no enforced contract without a court, no court without a state, no state without a tax. The market does not precede the state and suffer its exactions; the market is built on a platform the state provides and the tax pays for. Taxes, a great jurist said, are the price of civilization — and he meant not a price for goods we might decline, but the price of the ordered society in which a market can exist at all. The burden, wherever it finally settles, buys the arena in which all the paying and the bearing happen.6

6. Whoever bears it, the tax funds what the market cannot fund itself: public goods the market underprovides through free-riding (The Commons), and the institutions the market rests on — courts that enforce the contract, the power that backs the money, the protection of property. The tax is the price of the market itself: no enforced contract without a court, no court without a state, no state without a tax. The market is built on a platform the state provides and the tax pays for. "Taxes are the price of civilization" (Holmes) — the price of the ordered society in which a market can exist.

And here is why the tax must be compulsory, which is the feature we most resent. The goods the tax funds are public — non-excludable, enjoyed by all whether they pay or not — and so they cannot be funded by voluntary contribution, which free-riding would defeat: each, reasoning that his own contribution is negligible and that he will enjoy the good regardless, declines to give, and the good goes unfunded. The only way to fund a public good is to remove the choice not to pay — to compel. So the coercion at the heart of the tax is not a regrettable excess but the enabling feature: a tax one could decline would be no tax, defeated by the same free-riding that defeats voluntary provision. The compulsion is the institutional admission that we will not pay for the foundation of our own prosperity unless we are made to — that the public good requires not persuasion but the removal of the option to refuse. The tax is the one place the market's own logic, voluntary exchange, fails and must be replaced by command; and that is exactly why it belongs among the institutions, the structures that do what the market cannot.7

Why the tax must be compulsory: the goods it funds are public (non-excludable), so they cannot be funded voluntarily — free-riding defeats it (each declines, reasoning his share is negligible and he will enjoy the good regardless). The only way to fund a public good is to remove the choice not to pay. The tax is where the market's voluntary-exchange logic fails and must be replaced by command — hence its place among the institutions.

The tax, then, is the fourth of the institutions: the compulsory claim by which the state takes the resources to fund the foundation the market rests on — and the policy whose burden, once imposed, the state cannot place, for the market reallocates it through prices to wherever the elasticities send it. The law names who pays; the market decides who bears; and the gap between them is the widest in all of policy between intention and effect. With the tax, the state has its revenue — but the tax has exposed something the family must now confront. The tax is an exercise of the state's power to compel, and we have seen that this power, even at its most direct, is not unlimited: the state can command payment but not bearing, can tax but not burden. What, then, is the nature and the limit of the state's power — the power that backs the money, enforces the contract, and levies the tax? What makes its commands legitimate, and what restrains them? That is the question of the last institution, the one on which the firm, the contract, the money, and the tax all finally rest: the rule — the law, and the power behind it, which both wields the authority the market cannot supply and, in the rule of law, binds that authority itself. The family turns, at the last, to the rule.8

8. The tax is the fourth institution: the compulsory claim funding the foundation the market rests on, whose burden the state cannot place (the market reallocates it by elasticity). The law names who pays; the market decides who bears. The tax exposes a limit on even the state's most direct power — it can tax but not burden. That is the question of the last institution — the rule (the law and the power behind it, which both wields authority the market cannot supply and, in the rule of law, binds that authority). The family turns to the rule.
