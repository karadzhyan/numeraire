Plate vi in print

The Land

Henry George; the land value tax, borne wholly by the owner and distorting nothing, because land cannot move.

The site cannot leave; the claim upon its rent can change hands.

— the argument of this plate, after Henry George and the earlier Painless

The land stays where it is when the tax collector arrives. A machine can be made elsewhere, a business can move, a worker can decline the additional hour; the site beneath them cannot be carried into another jurisdiction or manufactured in greater quantity because its rent has risen. Here the volume's argument about escape reaches its cleanest case. If the supply of the thing taxed is fixed, the tax can take from its owner without reducing that supply. Henry George made this distinction the foundation of his proposal: leave the products of labor and investment with their makers, and draw the public revenue from the value of the ground. The proposition is powerful because it identifies a particular surplus. Its power depends on keeping that surplus distinct from everything built upon it.

Consider a hundred identical sites, all available for use, with competitive bidding fixing the annual rent of each at ten thousand units. Impose an annual tax of two thousand per site while holding the demand for sites and the available supply unchanged. The users still compete for the same hundred locations. Their bids still support ten thousand; the owner retains eight thousand and sends two thousand to the treasury. His wish to charge twelve thousand does not create a tenant willing to pay it. In this deliberately fixed-supply comparison, the tax lowers the owner's return by its full amount. The receipt names the owner, and the economic burden stays with him. This is the incidence principle of The Shift at its limiting case, where the taxed factor has no margin along which to withdraw.1

1. The numerical incidence example fixes land supply, demand, use, and the tax at 2,000 per site. It is an illustration of the fixed-base model, not a universal prediction for rents following a combined tax, spending, and planning reform.

The crucial boundary runs between the site and its improvements. A bare location and the apartment building upon it are different economic objects even when one deed conveys both. The building can be enlarged, maintained, allowed to deteriorate, or never built. A tax that rises when a room is added changes the reward to adding it; a charge on the site's value independently of that room need not do so. Imagine an addition yielding three thousand a year after operating costs and requiring an annualized capital cost of twenty-five hundred. A further six hundred assessed because the addition exists turns its five-hundred surplus into a loss of one hundred. A site-value charge unchanged by the addition leaves that particular investment margin intact. Calling both charges property taxes conceals the distinction on which the argument rests.2

An expected continuing tax also enters the price paid to acquire the land. Suppose a site's perpetual annual rent is ten thousand, the discount rate is five percent, and there is no expected growth. Its untaxed value is two hundred thousand. A permanent annual charge of two thousand lowers the net rent to eight thousand and, under the same assumptions, the purchase price to one hundred sixty thousand. The existing owner experiences a forty-thousand capital loss when the previously unexpected charge is announced. A later purchaser pays less for the taxed stream. The new purchaser still remits each year's tax, but that payment alone does not measure the reform's loss to him: the acquisition price has already adjusted. A tax can be borne in the value of an asset before the collector receives the first installment.3

3. The capitalization example uses a constant perpetual flow and a 5% discount rate: 10,000/0.05 = 200,000; 8,000/0.05 = 160,000. An annual charge fixed in money differs from a percentage tax on the post-tax sale value. For the latter, V = rent/(r + tax rate).

George's claim about justice begins where this arithmetic ends: much of a location's value is made by the community surrounding it. A new station, reliable courts, nearby employers, and the accumulated work of a city can enrich the owner of an unchanged parcel. Taking a share of that increase for common purposes has a different moral footing from charging a builder for the improvement she supplied. Yet a sound account of the reform must also identify the people holding the claims when it arrives. Someone may have bought recently with borrowed money; someone else may own a valuable home and receive little current income. The absence of a lost-supply triangle does not erase those positions. Transition arrangements, payment deferral, and the use of revenue determine how a reform's gain and loss reach households.4

4. Schwerhoff, Edenhofer, and Fleurbaey, Equity and Efficiency Effects of Land Value Taxation (2022), introduction and §§2.1–2.2, distinguish a neutral base from distributional consequences and explain capitalization. Their working paper is a model-based analysis, not a guarantee for every implementation.

Putting the principle into an assessment roll requires the state to value the site separately from the owner's work. Location, permitted use, access, and comparable transactions provide evidence; imperfect measurement leaves room for dispute. The clean model also holds other conditions fixed. Spending the proceeds on a station may raise nearby rents, and a change in planning permission may change what a site can support. Those effects belong in the whole policy comparison rather than being attributed mechanically to the tax alone. A levy that mistakenly reaches new improvements, exceeds the usable rent of marginal sites, or changes with the owner's investment no longer inherits the fixed-base result without further argument. The practical task is to preserve the distinction that made the theoretical tax attractive.5

5. This plate develops the land-tax argument of The Painless and the separate Ground plan. The earlier text and figure remain available. The stronger real-world claims in the plan are examined here under stated assumptions.

The land tax gives the volume an answer to a question that ordinary transactions made look impossible: revenue can be drawn from a fixed rent without discouraging the creation of the underlying site. It also exposes why efficiency cannot finish the public decision. The old owner, the new buyer, the resident with little cash, and the recipient of public services meet the same reform in different places. A precise tax base makes those encounters intelligible; it cannot decide how their claims should be weighed. The next plate moves from the thing taxed to the schedule imposed upon it. Once a society has chosen income as its base, it must choose how the claim shall rise as income rises—and learn to distinguish the rate on the next unit from the share taken of the whole.

The Tariff is now set between this opening plate and The Shift; the latter continues the account of domestic incidence.
