The payment records who receives the income; the argument asks what made the payment necessary.

— the argument of this plate

At the end of the year, the toll is entered as income. The accounts record that someone received it; they do not announce how much was needed to maintain the passage, how much repaid the cost of making it, and how much the holder could collect because there was no adequate way around. A bridge makes the problem visible. It requires construction, inspection, repair, and capital committed before the first traveler arrives. Yet a right to control the only crossing can support a further payment, measured by the traveler's lack of alternatives. Both can pass through the same booth. The volume has followed this mixture from the field to the machine, the street address, the protected occupation, and the position others must use. Its closing question concerns the whole distribution of income: how much of what appears as a reward for producing is a return to controlling access, and who receives that return when it is paid?

The definition of rent makes the question precise by requiring an alternative. A payment contains rent to the extent that it exceeds what is needed to retain the resource in the use under consideration. The phrase matters. An acre fixed in the world can still be moved from one economic use to another; a worker with a rare skill can leave one employer for another. The relevant alternative can therefore change the measured surplus. Time changes it too. The income of an installed machine may exceed the expenses that can still be avoided today while remaining necessary, in expectation, to repay the investment that brought it into existence. Marshall's quasi-rent supplies this discipline, and his wider account recognizes elements of rent inside wages and elements of productive return inside payments called rent. The category follows the economic comparison. It cannot be read from whether the recipient carries a wage slip, a dividend statement, or a lease.1

1. Alfred Marshall, Principles of Economics, eighth edition (1920), book V, chapter IX, §§2–4 and note 5. The distinction between current use and prospective investment is developed in The Quasi; the relevant alternative is the question of The Excess.

The national accounts consequently provide a starting point rather than a ready-made total of rent. The amount left after employee compensation includes the cost of using buildings, equipment, and intangible assets, as well as whatever surplus remains beyond those costs. Depreciation accounts for capacity that has been used up. A required return can compensate the provision of finance under uncertainty. Neither becomes pure rent merely by appearing on the capital side of the division. Conversely, an income classified as compensation can include a scarcity premium or a share of a firm's protected surplus. The familiar labor–capital boundary and the boundary between necessary return and rent cross one another. Housing adds another complication because much of its measured income is the value of services owner-occupiers provide to themselves. As The Ground showed, that can be central to a historical change in income shares without becoming a direct count of payments to landlords. The distribution has to be opened far enough to reveal which resource, which cost, and which claim each category contains.2

2. Matthew Rognlie, “Deciphering the Fall and Rise in the Net Capital Share: Accumulation or Scarcity?”, Brookings Papers on Economic Activity, Spring 2015, pp. 5–8 and 12–14, explains gross and net income, mixed income, and imputed housing services. An income classification does not identify its recipient's opportunity cost or establish the fraction that is economic rent.

One way to open that account is to estimate the cost of capital directly and examine the remainder. Simcha Barkai did so for the U.S. nonfinancial corporate sector from 1984 to 2014, combining estimates of the capital stock with required rates of return. In his decomposition, the declining labor share was accompanied by a declining share of capital costs, while the share of pure profits rose. The result directs attention toward surplus beyond the estimated cost of supplying productive inputs, rather than treating every dollar missing from labor's share as another dollar spent on machinery. Its measurement depends on what counts as capital and what return it requires. Omitted intangible investment or a mistaken cost of finance can move the residual, questions the paper examines explicitly. A measured profit residual therefore supplies evidence for the inquiry into market power; it does not identify every rent recipient or prove that every profitable enterprise has withheld a useful alternative. The substantial claim is that the division between labor and capital can conceal a further division within the return to ownership.3

Pure profit is constructed after employee compensation, estimated capital costs, and net production taxes. The paper's discussion of omitted capital and required returns matters to interpreting that residual; the sector and period do not constitute a total of rent in the entire economy.

The ownership of the claim then determines where the surplus goes, but the date of acquisition determines who received the gain. A new restriction that raises expected receipts can make an existing title more valuable immediately. Its owner can sell that expected stream to a buyer, who thereafter may earn only an ordinary return on the higher price paid. The customer can continue paying a scarcity charge even though the latest owner has no extraordinary yield on the purchase. Marshall recognized the danger of allowing a capitalized monopoly value to become its own justification: a moderate return on an expensive claim does not explain why the underlying claim should remain expensive. Nor is the division exhausted by the shareholder and the customer. Some surplus may be shared with employees, suppliers, or lenders through their bargains with the enterprise. Following the toll into household inequality therefore requires both the ownership of assets and the distribution of claims upon their income. A rise in the value of a protected position can enrich its existing holder without adding an equivalent amount of productive capacity for society.4

4. Marshall, book V, chapter IX, §2 and note 2, distinguishes the capitalized value of scarce services from the cost of creating an additional supply, and discusses the use of inflated capitalization to defend protected returns. The extension to the timing and sharing of gains follows the same distinction. It does not imply that all asset appreciation is a gain from exclusion.

The social cost of a toll depends on the conduct that sustains it as well as the transfer it makes. If the crossing's capacity is genuinely fixed, a payment for access may allocate something scarce even while its proceeds are distributed unequally. If the holder keeps an additional worthwhile crossing from being built, the loss includes journeys and exchanges that never occur. If rival claimants spend resources chiefly to obtain the exclusive permission, some of the prospective surplus may be consumed in the contest before any traveler pays it. These are different effects, and adding the toll itself to all of them would count a transfer as though it had vanished. The same care is needed where the prospect of a return helps create the passage. A policy that intercepts an unexpected surplus after construction can have different consequences from a rule anticipated by everyone deciding whether to build. The remedy must follow the mechanism: enlarge the available alternatives where exclusion has made them scarce, preserve useful capacity, and distinguish the return that supports creation from the advantage maintained by denying others a way through.5

5. Marshall, book V, chapter IX, §1 and note 5, follows the change from installed capacity to replacement and stresses the role of expected quasi-rents in investment. The distinctions among a transfer, foregone trade, and resources consumed in seeking a privilege keep separate the effects investigated in The Scarcity and The Capture. No single tax or entry rule has the same consequences in all these cases.

The volume ends with a distinction that is economically demanding and politically consequential. A price for access can convey real information about a scarce thing while leaving open who should receive the proceeds. An owner can have paid honestly for a title whose value depends on an exclusion that ought to change. An enterprise can perform useful work while also collecting a surplus from the position its success has secured. None of these propositions absolves the toll from examination; together they identify what must be examined before the accounts can be read as a verdict on contribution. The field, the license, and the gate reveal how an income can survive without a matching act of production by its current recipient. The task is to establish that portion, trace the alternatives foreclosed, and decide how the benefits of access should be shared. The next volume takes up the most persistent remaining claim: the payment for the use of capital through time. It asks what the lender's waiting and risk require, and how much of the rate is itself the price of a position others cannot escape. The argument remains open.
