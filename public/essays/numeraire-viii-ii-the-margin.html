The Margin

the marginalists; diminishing marginal utility, and want made at last to govern a price.

The loaf that sustains life and the loaf that adds little to a full cupboard are the same good at different margins of want.

— the argument of this plate, after Jevons and Menger (1871)

A hungry person buys a loaf because bread answers an urgent want. A second loaf may be welcome, a third useful for tomorrow, a fourth difficult to use before it spoils. The loaves can be alike while their additions to the person's circumstances differ. This was the opening the marginalists found in the old problem of value: an indispensable good can command a modest price because the decision concerns another portion of an available supply. The importance of having bread at all and the importance of having one loaf more are separate questions. The first can reach as far as life itself. The second depends on how much is already secured, on the purposes still unsatisfied and on what obtaining the extra loaf requires the buyer to give up. Want enters the account at that point of adjustment. To make it explain a price, the inquiry must follow both sides of the choice.

Jevons gave the distinction a mathematical form by separating total utility from the utility of a small addition. A total is the satisfaction attributed to the whole quantity consumed; its marginal utility is the change in that total when the quantity changes slightly. The two can move in different directions. Total satisfaction may continue to rise while each successive addition contributes less, just as a journey can continue upward while its gradient becomes gentler. In his account of food and water, the later increments answer less pressing wants than the earlier ones. Beyond satisfaction, further consumption may become burdensome and reduce the total. The comparison holds the relevant circumstances together: a quantity used over a given period, by a particular person, with the other conditions of its use understood. Tomorrow's first meal is not simply today's fifth. A good that completes a useful combination may also have a different contribution from one that merely repeats what is already available. Diminishing marginal utility gives a powerful account of satiation within such a comparison; its application requires identifying the want and the increment.1

The margin also explains which satisfaction is lost when one interchangeable unit disappears. Menger imagined a farmer distributing grain among the maintenance of life, health, seed, animals and less urgent uses. If one bushel is lost, the farmer can rearrange the remaining stock and preserve the purposes he considers most important. The sacrifice falls on the least important use that the available quantity had allowed him to secure. No particular bushel is intrinsically the life-saving one; another can take its place. Each identical bushel has the same marginal importance because losing any one permits the same rearrangement. A loss of the entire harvest would have quite another significance. The contrast between the whole supply and a removable part resolves the apparent contradiction in a necessity's low valuation without requiring us to believe that its necessity has vanished. As the stock contracts, progressively more important uses become exposed. Scarcity changes what hangs on the next unit, even when the person and the physical good remain the same.2

A buyer who pays for the extra unit must compare its contribution with the contribution of the money surrendered. Suppose bread and the other goods bought with the remaining budget can vary in small amounts. At an interior optimum, an additional dollar spent on bread yields as much utility as an additional dollar spent elsewhere. If bread yielded more, transferring a little spending toward it would improve the choice; if it yielded less, transferring spending away would do so. The condition equates marginal utility per dollar across the uses that remain active. It does not equate an unconverted quantity of satisfaction with a number of dollars. In a utility representation with money available for other purchases, willingness to pay for a little more bread is the ratio of bread's marginal utility to the marginal utility of that money. At a boundary, the buyer may devote nothing to one good, and the corresponding equality gives way to an inequality. The budget, the prices and the available alternatives belong inside the explanation from the beginning.3

III, “Distribution of Commodity in different Uses,” derives equality of marginal utilities for alternative uses of a common stock and explicitly considers allocation of the entire stock to one use. The expenditure formulation here translates that reasoning into a smooth budget problem: for positive purchases, Ub/pb = Um when the other good has unit price. A simple example shows when the declining utility curve can be read directly as a demand schedule. Let the utility assigned to bread be twelve times its quantity minus the square of that quantity, and let each dollar retained for other purchases add one unit to the same index. Give the buyer a budget of thirty dollars and allow divisible quantities. At four dollars per unit of bread, the preferred purchase is four units: bread contributes thirty-two utility units, and the fourteen dollars remaining contribute fourteen more. At that point the marginal contribution of bread is four, matching its price because the contribution of another dollar is one. Reduce the price to two dollars and the purchase rises to five units, leaving twenty dollars for other uses. Here the declining marginal-utility schedule does give inverse demand. The example makes that result possible by holding the marginal value of the other expenditure constant and leaving enough of it in the budget. Remove those assumptions and the movement of purchasing power and the relation between goods must also be examined.4

Price 4 gives (b, m) = (4, 14) and total utility 46; price 2 gives (5, 20) and total utility 55. These illustrative index values are not measurements of a person's feeling. The second derivative with respect to bread is −2, and the remaining expenditure is positive in both cases.

The price at which many people actually trade also depends on the goods available and on the alternatives of those supplying them. The buyer's marginal valuation helps describe a demand side; it cannot by itself fix the quantity that a market will deliver or the terms on which sellers will deliver it. The Scissors placed those adjustments together, while the present plate follows the want entering one side of them. Even within demand, monetary willingness reflects the buyer's means. A pressing need accompanied by little purchasing power may produce a smaller bid than a less urgent desire backed by a larger budget. The conversion from a utility index into a willingness to pay therefore gives the price a precise behavioral use without making it a common measure of the intensity of everyone's needs. The bread purchase can reveal what the buyer will sacrifice under the available terms. It cannot, by that observation alone, tell us how much another person's hunger ought to count.5

5. The distinction follows from the marginal ratio in note 3 and from the budget constraint. Changes in income or in the marginal value of other consumption can change a monetary valuation. It makes no interpersonal comparison of utility. The separate Comparison examines the additional judgments needed to compare persons; the market-equilibrium treatment remains in the Margin volume's Scissors.

The marginalists thus made a hidden want useful to a theory of observable exchange by asking what an increment changes. Their achievement survives the unresolved question of what the utility numbers measure. A buyer can be represented as choosing the best affordable bundle, and the representation can be relabeled by a strictly increasing transformation without changing which bundle is best. Such a relabeling can alter the size of the marginal utilities and even the curvature of the chosen index, while preserving the trade-offs that govern the same purchase. The marginal condition can therefore have content even when a particular numerical scale has no independent claim to measure pleasure. That is the problem taken up by The Order. The loaf has brought want to the point of a decision; the next inquiry asks how much of the supposed magnitude is needed to describe that decision at all.
