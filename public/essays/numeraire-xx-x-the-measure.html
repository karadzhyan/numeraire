The Measure

the national accounts; what it means to add up a nation's product, and what the great sum leaves out.

A sum becomes useful when its boundary is understood.

— the argument of this plate, after the national accounts

A nation’s product becomes a single number only after someone has decided what belongs in the sum. Bread, surgery, machinery and a night at the theatre arrive in different units; prices give them a common expression, while accounting rules decide which transactions record new production and which merely pass an existing thing along. Gross domestic product is the resulting measure of production within an economy during a period. It lets a society ask whether its activity has expanded, what it makes, and where the expansion occurred. Those questions are indispensable to this volume. They also differ from asking whether people are happier, whether the gains are widely shared, or whether the activity can continue. The number acquires its authority from a disciplined boundary. Understanding growth requires knowing both what that boundary reveals and what a larger total leaves undecided.

The first discipline is to count the product once, however many hands it passes through. Suppose a domestic forester sells timber for forty and a furniture maker turns it into a table sold to a household for one hundred. With no other purchased inputs, imports or product taxes in this example, adding both sales would report one hundred and forty for a table worth one hundred. Value added gives the same final total without duplication: forty at the forest and sixty at the workshop. The expenditure on the table and the value created along its production chain are two views of one activity. If the timber instead comes from abroad for twenty, domestic value added is eighty; final expenditure of one hundred less the imported input gives the same result. Subtracting imports keeps foreign production out of the domestic sum. The subtraction itself says nothing about whether buying that input was advantageous.1

1. Bureau of Economic Analysis, Measuring the Economy (December 2015), pp. 2–5 and the Domestic Income and Product Account discussion at pp. 8–11. The timber examples are hypothetical. Production, final expenditure and income are linked by accounting identities; separately estimated GDP and gross domestic income can differ statistically. The figures assume no inventories, product taxes or other inputs.

The production boundary includes important work without a sale, while excluding much work performed inside the household. In the 2008 System of National Accounts, most unpaid domestic services for one’s own household lie outside the central accounts: cooking, cleaning and caring for children remain valuable even when no entry records them. Paying someone outside the household to provide the care brings a market service into the measure. Yet the boundary is broader than the cash register. Housing services are attributed to people living in their own homes, and government services can be valued through their costs when comparable market prices are unavailable. Goods produced for own use also fall within the stated production boundary. These conventions aim to make comparisons useful and practicable. They make it possible for measured production to change when the organization of an activity changes, even where the benefit experienced by a family changes little.2

2. System of National Accounts 2008 (published 2009), §§6.23–6.38, pp. 97–100; BEA (2015), pp. 2–5. This cites the specified historical framework, not a claim that it is the latest edition. The household exclusion concerns services for own use; owner-occupied housing and paid domestic staff are explicit exceptions. Imputed rent prevents a mere change between renting and owning the same dwelling from changing measured housing services.

Growth in the money total must then be separated from growth in the quantity of production. An economy making one hundred identical loaves at two each has nominal output of two hundred. If the same loaves sell for two and a fifth, nominal output rises by a tenth although production has not changed. If it makes one hundred and twenty loaves at that higher price, nominal output rises to two hundred and sixty-four: a thirty-two percent increase combining twenty percent more loaves with ten percent higher prices. Valuing the later loaves at the original price isolates the twenty percent volume increase. An actual economy contains changing mixtures of products, so the separation requires price and quantity indexes rather than a literal count of loaves. A rise in expenditure establishes a rise in the money value spent; establishing growth in production requires the additional work of removing price change.3

3. BEA (2015), complete “Quantity and price estimates” discussion, pp. 14–15. The one-good example has nominal growth 1.2 × 1.1 − 1 = 0.32 and volume growth 0.20. BEA’s chained-dollar components need not add to their aggregate away from the reference year; the elementary fixed-price example is not a description of that aggregation method.

The words gross and domestic each impose another limit on what the total tells us. Gross production includes the part needed to replace capital consumed in producing it. With output of one hundred and consumption of fixed capital of twenty, net domestic product is eighty; if the capital charge were forty, the same gross total would leave sixty net. Domestic locates production within the economy, while national income follows income accruing to its residents, including the relevant flows across borders. Neither is simply the balance in household bank accounts. Dividing GDP by population creates an average of production per person, without assigning that amount to each person or describing the middle household. The aggregate can rise while particular people lose income. The accounts can illuminate these differences through their other measures, but one headline total cannot perform every part of their work.4

4. BEA (2015), “Output” and “Income,” pp. 4–5; SNA 2008, §§6.8–6.9, p. 95. The capital-charge figures are hypothetical. Consumption of fixed capital is an economic accounting measure, not necessarily the depreciation in a company’s historical-cost books. Domestic production, residents’ income, household income and the distribution of that income answer distinct questions.

Welfare asks a wider question because production can respond to harm as well as relieve a pre-existing want. A damaged home requires rebuilding; medical treatment responds to illness; cleanup uses labor and equipment after pollution. Those activities can count as production, and can be valuable given what has happened, while the disaster or illness leaves people worse off than they would otherwise have been. This does not make destruction itself a contribution to GDP, or guarantee that a disaster raises the total: lost production may outweigh repair work. It establishes why the value of new activity cannot by itself measure the change in well-being. The SNA explicitly recognizes this gap, including environmental harms that do not enter the transactions it records. A useful assessment therefore places the production account beside the condition of people, assets and ecosystems, rather than asking a production total to supply their complete valuation.5

5. SNA 2008, complete §H, “The SNA and measures of welfare,” §§1.75–1.84, pp. 12–13. The discussion distinguishes additional production that remedies a harmful event from the welfare comparison with a world in which the event never occurred. Its account of unpaid services and externalities expressly rejects interpreting GDP as a comprehensive welfare measure.

The measure makes economic growth visible by giving the comparison a consistent object. That achievement survives the limits of the object. We can ask how much more was produced after allowing for prices, how much replaced what wore out, how the income was distributed, and what happened outside the production boundary. Each answer sharpens the next question. A larger product can make better lives possible without settling how its possibilities are used; an unchanged product can coexist with improvements the central accounts were never designed to collect. The discipline is to keep the sum and its purpose together. Once that is done, the volume can return to the future of the ascent with a clearer doubt: how much production can keep growing, what its growth costs, and which parts of a good life the climb is meant to serve.
