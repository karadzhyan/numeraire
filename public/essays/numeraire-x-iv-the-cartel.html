The Cartel

the few who would act as one: oligopoly and the collusion that mimics monopoly, the cartel's standing temptation to fix the price and each member's standing temptation to cheat — a prisoner's dilemma the law and the cheater both undo.

A handful of sellers, seeing that together they might charge as a monopolist does, will try to act as one; but each, tempted to cheat on the others while they hold the price aloft, makes their union forever fragile.

— the argument of this plate, after George Stigler, 1964

Between the single seller and the crowd lies the common case the textbooks of pure monopoly and pure competition both skip past: the few. Most real markets are held not by one firm nor by thousands but by a handful — three carmakers, four banks, a half-dozen airlines — and the few present a problem neither monopoly nor competition poses. For the few can see what a crowd cannot: that if they would only act together, holding back output and lifting price, they might share among them the profit a monopolist takes alone. The temptation to collude is the defining fact of the few, and the cartel is its instrument.

What sets the few apart is interdependence. A price-taker in a crowd ignores his rivals, for he is too small to affect them or they him; a monopolist has no rivals to consider. But each of the few is large enough that his actions move the market, and so each must consider how the others will respond — if I cut my price, will they follow, and ruin us both, or hold, and let me take their customers? Every decision becomes a move in a game whose outcome depends on the others' moves, and the price that results is set neither by one firm's will nor by impersonal competition but by the strategic interplay of the few. Economists model this interplay in several ways — the rivals competing in the quantities they produce, or in the prices they post, with sharply different results — but the central fact is the one the cartel exploits: that the few, by coordinating their moves instead of warring, can together approach the monopoly outcome, lifting the price toward what a single seller would charge and dividing the spoils.1

1. Oligopoly (a few sellers) is defined by strategic interdependence: each firm is large enough that its choices affect rivals, so each must anticipate the others' responses. The price emerges from strategic interaction — modeled variously (Cournot quantity competition, Bertrand price competition, with differing outcomes). Crucially, the few can coordinate toward the monopoly price and split the profit, mimicking a single seller — the logic of the cartel. The game-theoretic structure connects to the Behaviors family.

But the cartel carries the seed of its own collapse, and naming why is Stigler's contribution. Suppose the few agree to hold output low and price high, each accepting a quota. The agreement is collectively profitable — together they earn the monopolist's profit — but it is not individually stable, for each member, while the others keep the price aloft, has a powerful incentive to cheat: to sell a little more than his quota at the high price the others maintain, capturing extra profit on the sly. Every member feels this temptation, and every member knows the others feel it, and so the cartel is forever undermined from within — each tempted to chisel, each watching the others for signs of chiseling, the whole arrangement liable to collapse into the price war it was meant to prevent. The cartel is a prisoner's dilemma wearing a boardroom suit: collectively the members do best by cooperating to restrict output, but individually each does best by defecting while the others cooperate, and so the cooperative outcome, though profitable, is unstable. The same logic that overgrazes the commons cracks the cartel.2

2. All face this temptation and all monitor for it, so cartels tend to collapse into price wars. The cartel is a prisoner's dilemma — cooperation (restricting output) is jointly optimal but individually dominated by defection — the same structure as the commons tragedy.

If cheating forever threatens the cartel, what ever holds one together? The answer, Stigler saw, is the ability to detect and punish the cheat — and this turns the cartel's fate on the same conditions that govern any sustained cooperation. A cartel holds where chiseling is easy to spot: where there are few enough members to watch, where prices are public and transactions visible, where demand is steady so that a member's falling sales betray a rival's cheating rather than a market slump. It crumbles where chiseling is easy to hide: where members are many, where deals are struck in secret and prices negotiated case by case, where demand swings so wildly that no one can tell a cheat from bad luck. And it holds, above all, where the members deal with one another repeatedly, so that a cheat today can be punished by a price war tomorrow — the shadow of future retaliation deterring the present temptation. Collusion, in short, is sustained by exactly the machinery that sustains cooperation in the commons: monitoring, and the credible threat of punishment in a relationship that endures. Where that machinery is present, the few hold their price aloft; where it is absent, they fall to competing.3

3. Cartels persist when cheating can be detected and punished: few members, transparent prices, stable demand (so falling sales reveal cheating), and especially repeated interaction (future price wars deter present cheating — the repeated-game logic). They collapse when cheating is hidden (many members, secret negotiated deals, volatile demand). Collusion is sustained by the same machinery as commons cooperation — monitoring and credible punishment in an enduring relationship (cf. the Commons' Sanction).

The cartel need not be a written conspiracy in a smoke-filled room; the few may reach the monopoly price without ever meeting, by tacit collusion — each independently grasping that warring would ruin them all and that matching a rival's high price serves them better, so that the price drifts upward by mutual understanding alone, no word exchanged. A price leader posts, and the others follow; a custom of not undercutting settles in; the few converge on the profitable outcome by reading one another rather than by colluding outright. This poses the law its hardest problem, the subject of the next plate. An explicit cartel — a secret agreement to fix prices — is the plainest of offenses, and the law forbids it absolutely. But tacit collusion is not an agreement at all; it is merely the few, each acting in his own interest, arriving at a high price because each foresees the others doing the same. There is nothing to outlaw, no meeting to raid, no contract to void — only an outcome that looks like monopoly and feels like conspiracy but consists, in law, of nothing but parallel self-interest. How to discipline a market power that arises from no agreement is among the deepest difficulties of competition policy.4

4. Tacit collusion: the few reach supra-competitive prices without explicit agreement, each independently recognizing that not undercutting serves all (price leadership, conscious parallelism). This challenges antitrust, which can forbid explicit price-fixing (a per se offense) but struggles with tacit coordination, which involves no agreement to prohibit — only parallel self-interested conduct producing a monopoly-like outcome. Disciplining agreement-free market power is a central difficulty of The Decree.

The few, then, occupy the vast middle of the economic world, between the monopoly that needs no agreement and the competition that permits none — and across that middle the price can sit anywhere, depending on how well the few can coordinate. Where they collude effectively, by open cartel or tacit understanding, sheltered by barriers and disciplined by mutual monitoring, the price approaches the monopolist's, and the few gouge as one. Where they cannot — too many to watch, demand too jumpy, an outsider always ready to undercut, the temptation to cheat too strong — they fall to competing, and the price sinks toward cost. Most real markets live somewhere on this spectrum, their prices set by the shifting balance between the gain from colluding and the temptation to defect, and the study of oligopoly is the study of where on that spectrum a given market sits, and why. It is the most realistic and the least tidy region of the theory of market power — neither the clean villainy of monopoly nor the clean discipline of competition, but the endless, unstable maneuvering of the few.5

5. Oligopoly occupies the spectrum between monopoly and competition; the price sits anywhere between the monopoly and competitive levels depending on how effectively the few coordinate (balancing the gains from collusion against the temptation to cheat). Most real markets are oligopolies, their outcomes set by this shifting balance, shaped by barriers, monitoring, and demand stability. It is the most realistic and least determinate region of market-power theory.

The cartel is the power of the few who would be one: the temptation, standing wherever a market is held by a handful, to act together as a monopolist and divide his profit — and the instability, standing just as surely, that each is tempted to cheat while the others hold the line, so that the union of the few is forever fragile, sustained only where they can watch and punish one another. It is market power contrived rather than inherited, conspiracy where monopoly is mere solitude, and it shades from the open cartel through the tacit understanding to the parallel pricing that no law can quite name. The plate that follows takes up the law's long war against all of it — the trust broken, the cartel forbidden, the conduct policed — and the doubts that have lately gathered about what that war should aim at.
