The Unseen

Bastiat; the gains from voluntary exchange, real but unseen, and the broken-window fallacy that destruction creates no wealth.

In every exchange there is what is seen — the coin paid, the goods handed across — and what is not seen: the value each party gains by giving up what it wants less for what it wants more. The whole art of the economist is to reckon the unseen, for it is there, in the invisible gain on both sides and never in the visible coin, that the surplus lives.

— the argument of this plate, after Frédéric Bastiat, 1850

The first plate measured the surplus; this one asks where it comes from. The answer is older and simpler than any diagram: the surplus is created by voluntary exchange itself, by the mere act of moving a thing into the hands of someone who wants it more. No new substance is made when a trade occurs, and yet value is created, for both parties end richer in their own reckoning than they began. To see why this is so — and why the gain it produces is so easily overlooked, sacrificed, and denied — is to enter the world of Frédéric Bastiat, the pamphleteer of free exchange, whose great theme was the difference between what an act of commerce shows on its surface and what it accomplishes underneath. His distinction between the seen and the unseen is the key to the whole question of where surplus comes from, and to the errors that forever threaten it.

Begin with the act of exchange. A trade occurs only when each party expects to gain; no one freely gives up a thing for something he values less. The buyer hands over money he wants less than the bread he receives; the baker hands over bread he wants less than the money. Each parts with what he ranks lower for what he ranks higher, and so each, by his own measure, comes out ahead — not at the other's expense, but alongside him. This is the deep source of the surplus: not that anything is manufactured, but that goods are MOVED to the people who value them most, and value is created in the moving. A bushel of wheat worth little to the farmer who has a barnful and much to the townsman who has none yields a gain to both when it passes between them, a gain equal to the gap between their two valuations. Exchange is not a zero-sum shuffling of a fixed stock of wealth, as the old mercantilists half-believed; it is a positive-sum act, a fountain of surplus, springing from nothing more than the difference in what a thing is worth to different people.1

Here Bastiat's central distinction enters. The gain from an exchange, real as it is, is invisible. What the eye SEES when a trade is made is the visible side: the coins counted out, the loaf wrapped and carried off, the ledger entry. What it does NOT see is the surplus: the satisfaction the buyer takes in the bread above its price, the profit the baker keeps above his cost, the two private gains that are the whole point of the transaction and that no money measures because they are precisely what was NOT paid. Bastiat made this the governing idea of his economics: that in every act there is a seen effect, immediate and obvious, and a train of unseen effects, no less real for being hidden, and that the difference between the good economist and the bad is that the one reckons the unseen while the other stops at the seen. The surplus belongs entirely to the unseen. It is felt, never displayed; it is the substance of what trade accomplishes, and it leaves no visible trace. And what is unseen is easily forgotten, easily denied, and easily sacrificed — which is the danger the rest of the plate concerns.2

Bastiat pressed the point home with a parable that has outlived all his other writing. A shopkeeper's careless son breaks a pane of his window. The neighbors, gathering to console him, find a silver lining: the broken pane means work for the glazier, who will be paid, and will in turn spend his earnings, so that the accident, far from a misfortune, sets money in motion and does the town a service. Destruction, on this view, creates prosperity. Bastiat answers that the consolers see only what is seen — the glazier's new business, the six francs that change hands, the bustle of repair — and miss what is not seen: the shoes, or the book, or the savings the shopkeeper would have bought with those six francs had his window stayed whole. The glazier's gain is exactly matched by the cobbler's loss, the one seen and the other invisible; and standing behind both is the plain fact that a window has been destroyed, so the town is poorer by one window and no richer for the repair. The seen activity is real, but it is not creation; it is mere diversion, the same money pushed toward glass instead of shoes, with a pane's worth of wealth annihilated in the bargain. Breaking things does not enrich a people. It only hides the loss behind the visible work of replacement.3

The fallacy of the broken window is the exact mirror of the gains from trade, and seeing the two together is the whole lesson. Voluntary exchange creates surplus that is unseen; destruction and the blocking of exchange destroy surplus that is equally unseen, and the politics of commerce turns, again and again, on the temptation to credit the seen and ignore the invisible loss. Bastiat's lifelong target was the tariff, which he showed to be the broken window dressed as policy. A duty laid on imported cloth protects the domestic weaver, and the weaver is SEEN: his mill stays open, his workers keep their jobs, his town stays loyal. What is NOT seen is the surplus destroyed — the gain that every buyer of cloth would have taken from the cheaper foreign bolt, and the further gain they would have spread through the economy by spending what they saved on other things, employing other hands in trades no one can point to because they were never allowed to arise. The protected producer is a visible beneficiary with a face and a vote; the consumers who lose are a diffuse multitude whose loss, being unseen, finds no defender. So the surplus of the many is sacrificed to the seen interest of the few, and the sacrifice is called protection.4

This is why the surplus, though it is the very thing a market exists to create, is forever in danger. It is unseen, and the unseen has no constituency. The gains from trade are spread thin across a whole population and felt by each as a small ease — a slightly lower price, a little more in the pocket — while the cost of opening trade falls visibly and heavily on a few, who feel it as ruin and organize to prevent it. Every argument that destruction is good for business, that a disaster will stimulate recovery, that a job saved by a subsidy is a job created, that keeping out foreign goods keeps wealth at home, is a version of the consolers at the broken window: a confusion of the seen activity with the unseen surplus, of motion with creation. Bastiat's contribution to the volume is to locate the surplus where it truly lives — in the invisible double gain of voluntary exchange — and to warn that because it lives there, unseen, it will always be tempting to sacrifice it for the sake of something one can point to. The surplus is created freely whenever two parties trade; the next question is how, between those two parties, the surplus they jointly create is divided. The next plate is the terms.5

Frédéric Bastiat answered the question of where the surplus comes from: it is created by voluntary exchange itself, by moving goods to those who value them most, so that both parties gain, each trading what he wants less for what he wants more, and value springs from nothing but the difference in their valuations. This gain, he insisted, is real but UNSEEN — what the eye catches in a transaction is the visible coin and goods, never the invisible double satisfaction that is the surplus. His parable of the broken window shows the mirror truth: destruction creates no wealth, for the seen activity of repair only diverts spending and hides the loss of what was destroyed, while protectionism is the same fallacy in policy, shielding the seen producer at the cost of the unseen surplus of every consumer. The surplus, being invisible and diffuse, has no constituency and is forever tempting to sacrifice for the sake of the seen. It is created whenever two parties freely trade.
