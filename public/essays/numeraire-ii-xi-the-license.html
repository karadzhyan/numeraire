The License

the modern proliferation of permission; occupational licenses and certificates, and the small artificial scarcities that ring-fence a wage.

The permission enters the price of the work; its worth depends on what it protects and whom it keeps outside.

— the argument of this plate

The hands are capable of the work; the permission is missing. A person can know a trade, find a customer willing to buy it, and still be unable to begin because the right to practice has been made conditional on a license. Sometimes the condition supplies a protection that neither party could otherwise obtain reliably. Sometimes it imposes a journey through examinations, training, fees, and delay whose connection to the work is difficult to explain. In either case, the law has entered the supply of the service. It determines which possible providers may become actual providers, and therefore affects the alternatives available to those who hire them. The scarcity in this plate is written into an institution. Its economic importance lies in what that institution adds to competence, what it takes from entry, and how the resulting income is divided between the people admitted, the people excluded, and the public asked to pay.

A certificate and a license can attest to the same skill while making different demands on the customer. Certification identifies a person who has met a standard; in its voluntary form, a buyer may still choose someone without it. Licensure makes the standard a condition of lawful practice. Neither should be confused with a fixed quota of permits. A qualification open to everyone who meets a test may allow the occupation to expand, although the expense of qualifying can slow or discourage entry. A numerical cap can prevent an additional qualified person from entering at all. These differences matter to the rent. A scarce transferable permit can acquire a resale price; a personal qualification may instead affect the worker's pay, the time needed to enter, or the range of services the worker may supply. The common question is how the rule changes the supply of capable work. The answer must follow the restriction itself, rather than assume that every document called a license creates the same bottleneck.1

1. Morris M. Kleiner and Alan B. Krueger, “Analyzing the Extent and Influence of Occupational Licensing on the Labor Market”, IZA Discussion Paper 5505, February 2011, pp. 3–4, distinguishes registration, certification, and licensure. The distinction turns on permission to perform the work, not the credential's title. A quantitative permit cap is a further restriction, analytically separate from a qualification requirement.

The strongest case for a required qualification begins with a failure of information. A customer may be unable to assess an important part of the service before buying it, and the consequences of incompetence may be difficult to repair afterward. Training can improve the work; an examination can reveal something the customer cannot cheaply discover; a public register can make a practitioner accountable. But the same requirements also consume resources. A year spent obtaining a credential is a year that cannot be spent earning in another occupation, and a fee is more difficult to finance for someone with little savings. Even useful training has to be judged against what it costs and the alternatives by which competence might be secured. A licensed worker's higher wage can therefore contain several things at once: payment for better service, compensation for the expense of entry, and a surplus made possible by fewer competitors. Calling the whole difference rent would erase the first two. Calling it all the reward of skill would erase the third. The work of the plate is to separate them without letting the credential settle its own case.

The observed wage advantage gives the question an empirical form, while leaving that separation unfinished. Kleiner and Krueger's national survey found higher wages associated with licensing even after a range of worker and job characteristics were included; the authors expressly treated the wage regressions as mainly descriptive because unobserved differences could remain. Kleiner and Evan Soltas approached the problem through occupations whose licensing differed across U.S. states. Their estimates connected licensing to higher wages and hours among practitioners, but lower occupational employment. Their welfare model also allowed quality and worker selection to raise consumers' willingness to pay. Within the policy margin they studied, the gains did not cover the estimated costs. This is a more demanding test than simply finding that the licensed earn more. The wage gain belongs to those who get through the gate; the expense includes the preparation and the opportunities lost by those who would otherwise have entered. An assessment confined to current practitioners can leave much of the price of the institution outside the account.2

Its findings concern occupations licensed differently across states; they do not establish the effects of abolishing licensing in every profession.

The rule also has a constituency, and that constituency helps determine how the rule is made. George Stigler's theory of regulation treats control over entry as something an industry can seek from the state, alongside subsidies and restrictions on competing products. The gain to an established trade can be sufficiently concentrated to justify sustained organization, while the cost is spread across customers who have little reason to follow a particular licensing proposal closely. Expertise makes the relation harder to untangle. Practitioners know the work and can identify real dangers; they also face the competitors whom a stricter rule may exclude. Their knowledge is useful evidence, but their preferred entry conditions cannot be taken as the public interest without further inquiry. A training requirement related to the hazards of the task and a requirement that chiefly prolongs the newcomer's wait may be administered through the same board and defended in the same vocabulary. The practical test is what additional competence or protection the requirement buys, measured against its burden on entry and access.3

3. George J. Stigler, “The Theory of Economic Regulation”, Bell Journal of Economics and Management Science 2, no. 1 (1971), pp. 3–7, 10–14. This is an account of incentives to obtain and shape regulation, including occupational licensing. It supplies a question about the formation of rules, not proof that every particular licensing body has been captured.

A transferable permission can make reform difficult long after its original beneficiary has departed. Suppose a permit carries a certain annual surplus of 1,000, expected to continue forever, and buyers discount that stream at ten percent. Its value is then 10,000. The person given the permit receives a windfall; a later buyer who pays 10,000 has paid for the expected surplus in advance. If entry is subsequently opened and the surplus disappears, that buyer loses the asset value, while a loan used to purchase it may remain. The restriction can thus continue to burden customers even when its current holder earns only an ordinary return on the purchase price. This does not make the restriction efficient. It explains why removing it can impose a concentrated loss on someone who never received the initial gift. Occupational qualifications are often personal and cannot be sold in this manner, but they create a related division between established practitioners and later entrants. A rule that exempts incumbents from new training costs can reward those already inside while requiring newcomers to finance the protection that keeps entry difficult.4

4. The permit calculation is illustrative: a perpetual certain flow of 1,000 discounted at 0.10 has a present value of 10,000. It assumes the restriction is fully reflected in the purchase price and distinguishes a transferable permit from a personal occupational license. Kleiner and Soltas, 2022 manuscript, p. 4 n. 3, notes that grandfathering can shift licensing costs onto subsequent entrants, consistent with incumbent support.

Judging the license therefore requires a comparison among workable ways of protecting the public. Where a reliable certificate supplies the information buyers need, exclusive permission must justify what it adds. Where a mistaken choice can injure people who never chose the provider, voluntary disclosure may leave the central problem untouched. A competence test, supervised practice, inspection, insurance, and a restriction on particular hazardous tasks address different failures; their burdens and effectiveness have to be compared in the occupation at issue. The licensed wage alone cannot answer that inquiry, and neither can the stated purpose of the law. The question is whether the protection survives a less costly route into competent practice, and whether the people who would gain from that route have been heard alongside those already established. A license earns its place by what it secures for the public. Where it chiefly preserves the value of admission, a portion of the wage has become the income of a protected position. The final plate gathers these positions across the economy and asks how much of the distribution of income follows the work performed, and how much follows the right to let others through.5

5. Kleiner and Soltas, 2022 manuscript, §1, makes the welfare comparison depend on both the cost of mandated investment and the change in willingness to pay for the resulting service. The alternative safeguards here identify distinct institutional choices; their effectiveness must be established for the relevant activity. The existence of a quality benefit alone does not determine whether the chosen entry burden is justified.
