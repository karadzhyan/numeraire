The Norm

the unwritten rules

The norm is the third of the behaviors, and the last of the table's twenty-six elements. It concerns the unwritten rules — the trust, the reciprocity, the done thing that no contract specifies and every functioning market silently requires. Choice studied the single mind weighing its options; the game studied minds entangled in strategy; the norm studies the rules those minds hold in common, the shared expectations that govern conduct where no law reaches. The volume opens with the simplest of these, the convention — a regularity that holds itself in place because each conforms, expects the rest to conform, and prefers to conform so long as they do (Lewis), arbitrary yet, once settled by precedent, as binding as any law. From there it builds. Trust is the silent precondition of nearly every exchange, and Robert Putnam showed it to be a productive asset, social capital, held in common and setting by its radius the very reach of the market (Putnam). Reciprocity is the most primal of the unwritten rules, and its purest form is the gift, which binds where a price would clear the account and let the parties part as strangers (Mauss). And the norm has a grammar, the conditional structure of empirical and normative expectation that makes a rule bind, and that makes it break (Bicchieri). Mark Granovetter widened the lens from the rule to the relationship: economic action, he argued, is embedded in concrete networks of personal ties, so that a market is less a crowd of strangers than a web of standing relationships, and trust grows less from calculation than from acquaintance (Granovetter). The flagship gives the volume its answer: that an eleventh-century trading community sustained sophisticated long-distance commerce with no court and no state, on an unwritten rule of pure collective power — cheat one of us, and deal with none of us — so that reputation did the work the law could not reach (Greif). The question the author set it was this: which of the market's rules are written, and which merely understood? The answer inverts the asking. The unwritten rules are the deeper and the more numerous; the written contract and the court are the visible tip of a structure whose great mass, below the waterline, is merely understood; and beneath every law lies the shared expectation that gives it force. With the norm — the third of the behaviors and the last of the table's elements — the long argument arrives at the ground on which economics must finally rest: not on the machinery of price and quantity alone, but on the trust, the reciprocity, and the unwritten understanding without which no machinery would turn at all.

The argument of the volume

Begin where the volume begins, with the unwritten rule in its purest form. A convention, David Lewis showed, is a regularity in the behavior of a population that holds itself in place: each conforms because the others do, expects the others to conform because each does, and prefers to conform so long as they do — so that an arbitrary practice, once settled by precedent and salience, needs neither law nor agreement to endure, only the expectation, held in common, that it will. Language, money, weights and measures, the side of the road, the manners of a market are conventions of this kind, self-enforcing because in a problem of pure coordination it is in everyone's interest to conform. But the rules that matter most are harder, for they govern situations with the structure of the prisoner's dilemma, where each is tempted to break the rule for private gain even as all benefit from its being kept; and for these, mutual expectation must be reinforced. The first reinforcement is trust. Robert Putnam gave it its economic name, social capital, and showed it to be a genuine productive asset — built through repeated dealing and civic life, yielding a return in cheap cooperation, yet held in common rather than by any individual. His study of the Italian regions found that the reach of a society's trust, rooted in civic traditions centuries old, predicted how well it would govern itself and prosper; for the radius of trust sets the radius of exchange, and where trust is wide, deals close on a word.

Beneath trust lies an older norm, reciprocity, and Marcel Mauss found its purest expression in the gift. A gift is never free: it binds the giver to give, the receiver to accept, and above all the receiver to reciprocate, and its decisive feature is that, unlike a sale, it does not clear the account but opens one — leaving a debt that binds the parties into an ongoing relationship where a price would have let them part as strangers. Reciprocity is the seed of all trust, and it persists inside the market in every relationship of mutual favor, in the relational contract and the repeated game alike. Then Cristina Bicchieri gave the unwritten rule its grammar. A social norm, she showed, is sustained by conditional preferences resting on two expectations at once — the empirical belief that others conform, and the normative belief that others expect us to conform and may sanction us if we do not — and it holds only while both hold. This structure distinguishes the social norm from the pure convention and from the unconditional moral rule, and it explains why norms can be measured by eliciting expectations, switched on and off by cues that make them salient, and tipped into sudden collapse when the beliefs beneath them shift. The unwritten rule, at bottom, is a shared belief about a shared belief — which is the source at once of its power over a community and of its fragility. Beneath the rule lies the relationship, and Mark Granovetter made it his subject: economic action, he argued, is embedded in concrete networks of personal ties, so that a market is less a crowd of strangers dealing at arm's length than a web of standing relationships, in which trust grows from acquaintance and order from the relationship itself.

The flagship gives the volume its answer. Avner Greif reconstructed how an eleventh-century community of traders carried on long-distance Mediterranean commerce — which requires entrusting one's goods to distant, unwatched agents — with almost no recourse to courts or law. They formed a coalition within which a reputation, once made, was known to all, and within which an agent who cheated any single merchant was boycotted thereafter by every member; and this multilateral punishment made honesty each agent's own best policy, since the whole future stream of honest employment outweighed any single theft. From the case Greif drew a large claim: that an institution is at bottom a self-enforcing pattern of shared expectation, and that beneath every written institution lies an unwritten one that makes the writing bite. So the question is answered, and the answer inverts the asking: the unwritten rules are the deeper and the more numerous, and they bear the greater load; the written contract and the court are the visible tip of a structure whose great mass is merely understood. And with the norm the volume reveals what the whole table has been moving toward — that the most powerful forces in an economy are often the least visible, the springs of conduct beneath the prices: the strategic logic of the game, and the unwritten constitution of the norm. With the norm joining the choice and the game in print, the behaviors stand three volumes deep, and the table arrives at the last of its twenty-six elements.

The argument remains open. What follows is an attempt to fix its exact edges.

In closing

This volume took up the norm, the third of the behaviors and the last of the table's twenty-six elements — the unwritten rule, the trust and reciprocity and done thing that no contract specifies and every functioning market silently requires. Its subject was the social fabric beneath exchange: the convention, trust, reciprocity, the grammar of the norm, and the reputation by which communities enforce their unwritten rules with no recourse to law. And the question the author set it was this: which of the market's rules are written, and which merely understood?

The volume built from the simplest unwritten rule to the deepest. David Lewis defined the convention — a regularity that holds itself in place because each conforms, expects the others to conform, and prefers to conform so long as they do — arbitrary yet, once settled, as binding as any law, and needing no enforcement in a problem of pure coordination. Robert Putnam gave trust its economic name, social capital, and showed it to be a productive asset held in common, whose radius sets the reach of the market, the high-trust society cooperating cheaply where the low-trust one cannot. Marcel Mauss found in the gift the most primal norm of all, reciprocity — the gift that opens a debt and binds the parties where a price would clear the account and let them part as strangers. And Cristina Bicchieri gave the norm its grammar, the conditional structure of empirical and normative expectation that makes a rule bind, and that, when the beliefs beneath it shift, makes it break. And Mark Granovetter widened the lens from the rule to the relationship, showing economic action to be embedded in concrete networks of personal ties — a market less a crowd of strangers than a web of standing relationships, in which trust grows from acquaintance more than from any calculation of incentive.

The flagship gave the answer. Avner Greif reconstructed how an eleventh-century trading community sustained long-distance commerce with no court and no state, through a coalition whose unwritten rule — cheat one of us, and deal with none of us — made honesty each agent's own best policy, since multilateral punishment set the whole future stream of honest dealing against any single theft. From it Greif drew the lesson that an institution is at bottom a self-enforcing pattern of shared expectation, and that beneath every written rule lies an unwritten one that gives it force. So the question is answered, and the answer inverts the asking: the unwritten rules are the deeper and the more numerous, and they bear the greater load; the written contract and the court are the visible tip of a structure whose great mass is merely understood. With the norm joining the choice and the game in print, the behaviors stand three volumes deep; and with the twenty-sixth and last of the elements now set in print, the periodic table reaches its final square — the end of the long row of primitives that began with the wage.

The unwritten rules are at once the cheapest of a market's institutions and the most precious. Cheapest, because they need no enforcement but mutual expectation — no court, no police, no written contract — and so they govern vast reaches of economic life at no cost at all. Most precious, because, being made of shared belief, they cannot be bought, and once lost they cannot be legislated back, but only slowly rebuilt by the long sediment of dealings kept and faith honored. A people can write its laws in a season; it cannot write its trust. So the question the norm leaves open is the one that hangs over every market and every age: not whether the unwritten rules matter, for they bear the greater load, but whether a people can keep the faith its prosperity is built upon — whether the reservoir of trust, drawn down by every betrayal and refilled only by the patient honoring of obligations, can be replenished as fast as a restless commerce spends it.
