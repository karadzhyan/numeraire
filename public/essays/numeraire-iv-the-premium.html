The Premium

the price of risk

The premium is the price of risk — what must be paid, over and above a sure thing, to persuade someone to bear an uncertain one. It is the fourth of the prices, and the one that reaches deepest into the self, for it is bound up with how we feel about the unknown and with the stories we tell about who deserves their fortune. Its twelve plates follow risk from the gambling paradox that first revealed why uncertainty has a price, through the two great engines that tame it — the pooling of insurance and the spreading of diversification — to what remains when all that can be diversified away is gone: a price paid only for the risk no one can escape. And they end on the most uncomfortable discovery of all — that the premium we infer from the world's great fortunes is largely a survivor's tale, in which the reward for skill and the reward for mere luck, having left the identical trace, can no longer be told apart.

The argument of the volume

Of the prices in this family, the premium is the one we are most sure we understand. Risk is unpleasant; people must be paid to bear it; the payment is the premium, the extra return a risky venture must promise over a safe one. Insurance, the stock market, the fortunes of those who gamble and win — all seem to display the same plain fact, that the world pays a price for the bearing of uncertainty, and that the price is the just reward of a real and onerous service.

What the theory of risk found beneath that plain fact is less plain. The premium exists, it showed, only because the utility of wealth is concave — because a loss bites deeper than an equal gain — so that the price of risk is rooted not in the world but in the curvature of our wants. It found that most risk can be made to vanish, pooled away by insurance or diversified away in a portfolio, so that the premium is paid only for the slender residue no sharing can touch. And it found that this residue is not a property of any asset but a relationship — an asset's tendency to fail in the states we most fear — so that risk lives not in the thing but in its place in the whole, and the same venture is differently risky to every bearer.

And it found, at the last, that the premium the world seems to display is in large part an illusion of vantage. The fortunes we observe are the survivors of risk; the ruined have left no record; and among those who bore the same uncertainty equally well, who was left standing to collect the reward was settled, in unmeasurable part, by luck. The realized price of risk thus confounds the reward for skill with the reward for a favorable draw, beyond any separating; the average premium history reports is inflated by the disasters that happened not to strike; and the universal habit of reading a great fortune as a monument to its holder's courage is the privilege of the survivor, who writes the account. The question the volume cannot escape — how much of a fortune is courage, and how much is luck — turns out to have, for any single fortune, no answer the fortune itself can give.

The argument remains open. What follows is an attempt to fix its exact edges.

In closing

This volume took up the price we are most certain is deserved — the reward for bearing risk — and found it the most entangled with luck and with self-regard. The premium is real: because the utility of wealth is concave, a fair gamble is a bad bargain, and the risk-averse must be paid to face uncertainty. The plates in print followed that price from its origin in the curvature of our wants to its strange afterlife in the fortunes risk leaves behind.

Risk, the volume showed, is first of all to be tamed rather than borne. Insurance pools it, turning the unforeseeable fate of the one into the near-certain average of the many, and trading each person's dreaded loss for a small sure payment. Diversification spreads it, destroying for free the risk peculiar to each venture and leaving only the risk that all ventures share. What survives both — the systematic, undiversifiable remainder — is all that earns a premium, and it is priced not by how much an asset varies but by how it moves with the common fate, by its tendency to fail when failure is least affordable. Risk, properly understood, is less a quantity a thing possesses than a relationship it bears to the states we fear.

And then the volume turned from the expected to the realized, and the elegance gave way to discomfort. The premium is earned in advance, before the throw; but the fortunes we see come after it, and we see only the winners, the ruined having vanished. In any single great fortune the reward for skill and the reward for luck leave the same trace — a survivor with the winnings — and cannot be told apart; the premium history reports is swollen by catastrophes that did not happen; and the story that a fortune was deserved is mostly told by those who lived to tell it. So the fourth price completes the family's lesson and turns it upon ourselves. The wage flatters us as worth and is partly power; the rent flatters us as contribution and is the toll of position; the rate presents itself as a fact of time and is partly a decision; and the premium presents itself as the wage of courage and is, in its realized form, the wage of survival. Each is a payment that dresses something cooler — a position, a decision, a power, a lucky draw — in the language of desert.

If the reward for bearing risk falls, in the end, to whoever is left standing, and if luck and skill leave the survivor the identical winnings, then when we honor a great fortune as the prize of courage, whom are we honoring — the brave, or merely the spared?
