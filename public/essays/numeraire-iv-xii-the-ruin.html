The Ruin

the gambler's ruin and the question of ergodicity; the gulf between what a bet pays on average and what it does to one who keeps taking it.

A favourable average cannot replenish an exhausted stake.

— the argument of this plate

A favourable average can belong to a wager that destroys the person who keeps accepting it. The average describes a collection of possible outcomes; the person has to pass through one outcome before reaching the next, carrying forward whatever the first has left. A loss can reduce the resources available for a later gain, and an exhausting loss can end the sequence altogether. This is the boundary toward which the volume has been moving. Pooling distributes losses among people; diversification distributes exposure among ventures; a hedge rearranges payments across states. Each can make risk more bearable, but the bearer still requires the capacity to continue. The question of ruin brings that capacity into the calculation. It asks how the stake changes, when the game stops, whether resources can be replenished, and what an average over possibilities can tell someone who must live through them in order.

The classical gambler's ruin makes the stopping rule exact. A player begins with a finite number of units and wins or loses one on each independent toss, stopping when the stake reaches zero or a chosen upper target. Zero is absorbing: there is no borrowing or fresh stake with which to return. In a fair game, a player beginning with ten units and aiming for one hundred has a one-in-ten chance of reaching the target first, and a nine-in-ten chance of being ruined first. The expected gain on each permitted toss is zero, yet the unequal distances to the two boundaries make the chance of ruin large. If the upper target is removed and fair play continues against an indefinitely rich opponent, ruin occurs with probability one. A favourable bias changes this conclusion: with a fixed one-unit stake and a win probability strictly between one-half and one, eventual ruin has a positive probability below one. Thus neither repetition nor a positive expected payoff settles the matter by itself. The size of the available capital, the stake and the conditions for stopping enter the result.1

When gains and losses are fractions of what remains, a positive expected return can coexist with a steadily diminishing fortune. Consider an independent fair coin that raises the entire current stake by fifty per cent on heads and reduces it by forty per cent on tails. Its expected multiplier is one half of 1.5 plus one half of 0.6, or 1.05: expected wealth after n rounds is the original stake multiplied by 1.05n. But a gain and a loss in either order multiply the stake by 1.5 × 0.6 = 0.9. Over a long sequence, the fraction of each outcome approaches one-half, and the compounded multiplier per round approaches the square root of 0.9, about 0.9487. The corresponding long-run growth is negative. Under these assumptions wealth converges to zero with probability one even as its expectation rises. It stays strictly positive at every finite round in this frictionless mathematical example; it nevertheless eventually crosses any fixed positive insolvency threshold below the initial stake. The distinction between reaching zero and becoming unable to meet an obligation gives the calculation its practical meaning.2

2. Ole Peters and Murray Gell-Mann, Evaluating Gambles Using Dynamics, arXiv:1405.0585v2 (5 June 2015), §III and figure 2, use this additive/multiplicative comparison. The exact log growth per round is ½ ln(1.5) + ½ ln(0.6) = ½ ln(0.9) ≈ −0.05268; exp(½ ln(0.9)) − 1 ≈ −5.13%. These are stipulated payoffs, not estimated investment returns.

The question of ergodicity is which average can legitimately stand for which experience. In the multiplicative example, averaging the wealth of an indefinitely large collection of independently playing people at a fixed date produces the growing expectation. The long sequence of a single person has a different growth rate, because returns multiply the wealth that survived the previous round. Rare exceptionally rich paths can pull the first average upward while almost every path eventually declines. This does not make probability cease to work: the law of large numbers supplied the result by averaging the logarithms of the multipliers, which add along the path. The relevant log-growth observable has the convergence property that the raw wealth expectation cannot supply as a description of typical compound growth. Nor does the distinction establish that expected-utility theory requires a person to maximize expected money, or that a long-run growth criterion answers every choice. Expected utility can evaluate a whole path or its terminal result, and logarithmic utility gives the same ranking as expected log growth in the corresponding multiplicative setting. The lesson is to identify the process and the objective before asking an average to decide for us.3

3. Peters and Gell-Mann, §§I and III, define the equality-of-averages property and distinguish additive from multiplicative dynamics. The mathematical distinction is used here without adopting a universal claim that utility theory is displaced. For fixed initial wealth and horizon, maximizing expected log terminal wealth and expected average log growth differs only by additive and positive multiplicative constants.

The fraction committed can change the long-run result even when the underlying opportunity and its odds stay the same. If only a fraction f of the stake is exposed to the fifty-per-cent gain or forty-per-cent loss, with the rest held unchanged, the total multipliers become 1 + 0.5f and 1 − 0.4f. Their average log is the growth criterion; it depends on how much is exposed as well as on whether the underlying wager has a positive expectation. Kelly's 1956 analysis made this distinction precise for repeated bets, choosing a fixed allocation to maximize long-run exponential growth under specified probabilities and odds. Betting everything can maximize expected wealth in a favourable game and still produce eventual ruin; committing a smaller share can preserve the ability to reinvest. But growth optimality is an answer to a particular problem. A finite deadline, consumption needs, costs, uncertain probabilities or changing opportunities can change the objective and the feasible allocations. Even within the model, the growth-maximizing allocation does not guarantee a gain at every finite horizon or spare its holder substantial intervening losses. The size of the wager is part of the wager.4

The capacity to reduce a stake or survive a loss is itself unevenly distributed. Two people can encounter the same proportional hazard while facing very different thresholds: one can wait through a fall, while the other must meet a payment before a recovery becomes possible. A livelihood tied to the venture, a debt falling due, or an inability to replace capital changes what the sequence permits. These circumstances need not appear in the return recorded for the asset. Insurance, limited commitments and access to resources after a setback can alter the path, but their costs and the obligations they create must be counted; moving a loss to a guarantor leaves the question of the guarantor's capacity. The earlier Fortune placed this inequality beside the celebration of courage: the opportunity to take a risk depends partly on having something one can afford to lose. Ruin adds that the opportunity to take the next risk depends on what survives the last. A comparison of successful outcomes that omits these differences mistakes the conditions of participation for an equal test of nerve.5

5. This paragraph develops the surviving Fortune and Survivor inquiries into resources, selection and the interpretation of success. The examples identify constraints omitted from a bare return sequence; they are not estimates of how much of any particular fortune comes from skill, luck or inherited capacity.

The premium ends where the prospect of a reward meets the possibility of losing the means to receive another. Its earlier plates established a real service: risks can be borne by those better placed to carry them, and contracts can make uncertain undertakings possible. Its later plates examined the limits of the evidence from which that service is priced and the fortunes by which it is judged. Ruin completes the inquiry by making survival a condition inside the process. A high average payoff does not ensure a viable path, and a surviving fortune does not disclose the paths that ended before they could be counted beside it. Skill, judgment and the willing commitment of resources can matter greatly; the realized outcome alone cannot allot their shares or turn them into a complete verdict on desert. To understand the price of risk is therefore to ask both what a claim promises across possible worlds and what its bearer can endure in the one that unfolds.
