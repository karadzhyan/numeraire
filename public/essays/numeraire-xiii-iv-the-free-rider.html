The Free Rider

Olson and the logic of collective action: why the larger the group that shares a goal, the less any member will lift to reach it, and why the common interest so often goes undefended.

That all would gain from a thing does not mean any one will lift to win it; for the larger the group that shares the prize, the smaller each one's share of it, and the likelier that each waits for another to bear the cost.

— the logic of this plate, after Mancur Olson, 1965

Beneath the overgrazed pasture and the unbuilt lighthouse lies a single logic, and it governs far more than fisheries and beacons. Wherever a group shares an interest that no member can be excluded from — a clean river, a union wage, an honest government, a stable climate — the group will tend to under-serve that interest, each member hanging back in hope that others will bear the cost. Mancur Olson named this the logic of collective action, and showed it to be a property not of selfishness but of number.

The argument cuts against a comfortable assumption — that a group of people who share an interest will, naturally, act to advance it. Not so, Olson showed. Consider any member of a large group weighing whether to contribute to the common cause — to join the strike, to pay the lobby's dues, to conserve the water, to bear the cost of cutting his emissions. The benefit his contribution produces is spread across the whole group, so that he himself reaps only a tiny fraction of it; the cost he bears alone, in full. With his share of the benefit small and his cost whole, the rational course is to contribute nothing and let others carry the effort — and since every member reasons the same, the contributions that would have served them all are never made. The common interest, precisely because it is common, goes undefended; and the larger the group, the worse the paralysis, for the larger the group, the smaller each member's share of what his effort would win.1

1. Mancur Olson, "The Logic of Collective Action" (1965): a rational individual in a group sharing a non-excludable benefit will not contribute, since his contribution's benefit is dispersed across all while its cost is borne alone. So groups fail to act in their common interest — and the larger the group, the weaker the incentive, since each member's share of the collective benefit shrinks. Shared interest does not imply collective action.

From this Olson drew conclusions that overturned a naive faith in numbers. A small group, he saw, may act where a large one cannot, for in a small group each member's share of the common benefit is large enough to make his contribution worth its cost to him alone — so the few often organize where the many fail. This inverts the democratic intuition that the larger interest must prevail: a concentrated handful, each with much at stake, will out-organize a diffuse multitude, each with little — which is why a small industry seeking a tariff routinely defeats the vast public that pays for it, and why narrow lobbies so often beat broad interests. The dispersed many, though their total stake be greater, cannot overcome the free-riding of their own members; the concentrated few, sharing a prize large to each, can. The logic of collective action thus explains not only why public goods go unprovided, but why politics so often serves the organized minority against the unorganized majority.2

2. Olson's counterintuitive corollaries: small groups organize more readily than large ones (each member's stake in the collective benefit is larger), so concentrated interests (a small industry seeking protection) routinely defeat diffuse ones (the consuming public), despite the latter's greater total stake. This explains the political power of narrow lobbies over broad interests — the diffuse majority cannot overcome free-riding among its own. Cf. the political economy of the forthcoming The Tax.
⁂

If pure self-interest dooms collective action in large groups, how does any large group ever act — how do unions form, lobbies fund themselves, associations endure? Olson's answer was the selective incentive: a private benefit or penalty, given only to those who contribute, that rides alongside the public cause and motivates the contribution the public cause alone cannot. The union wins members not only with higher wages, which it cannot withhold from non-members, but with insurance, legal aid, a hiring hall — benefits reserved for those who join. The professional association offers its journal, its license, its credential, only to dues-payers. The cause is funded not by the diffuse public good it pursues but by the private goods it bundles with membership, or by the social pressure and shame it visits on shirkers. Collective action in large groups, Olson saw, must be bought or coerced at the individual level; it does not arise of itself from the shared interest, however great, but must be bolted onto a scaffold of private reward.3

3. Olson's "selective incentives": large groups overcome free-riding by attaching private, excludable benefits or penalties to contribution — union insurance and hiring halls, an association's journal and credential, or social pressure and shame. The public cause is funded by the private goods bundled with membership, not by the shared interest itself. Collective action must be individually motivated, not assumed.

Olson's law is iron in the situation it describes — large, anonymous, one-shot, mute — but that situation is not the only one, and the conditions that loosen its grip are exactly those the flagship will press. Where the members are few enough to know one another, where they deal not once but repeatedly, where they can talk, watch, and remember, the calculus shifts: a contribution today may be repaid in others' contributions tomorrow, a defection today punished by others' defection or scorn hereafter, and the shame of being seen to shirk among people whose regard one needs becomes a selective incentive that no one had to design. The free-rider problem is most lethal among strangers who meet once and part; it is most tractable among neighbors who meet always and remain. Olson, intent on the large anonymous group, saw mainly the iron; but the loosening points the way out, toward the communities that govern their commons precisely by being small, durable, and watchful — the subject the flagship will reach.4

4. Olson's logic is strongest for large, anonymous, one-shot groups; it weakens where members are few, interact repeatedly, and can communicate, monitor, and remember — conditions under which reciprocity, reputation, and shame become endogenous selective incentives. This bridges to Ostrom's self-governing communities (The Covenant) and the repeated-game foundations of cooperation (The Sanction): the free rider is tamed not among strangers but among neighbors.

The logic of collective action is, in the end, the most general statement of the family's failure, of which the commons and the public good are special cases. Any shared interest from which members cannot be excluded — a resource not to be overused, a good to be provided, a standard to be upheld, a wrong to be resisted — confronts the same obstacle: that what serves the group does not, of itself, serve the member, and so will not be done unless private incentive is somehow married to collective good. This is why the failure is so pervasive and so stubborn, reaching from the village pasture to the global climate, from the funding of a lighthouse to the resistance of a tyranny: wherever benefits are shared and contribution is voluntary, the free rider lies in wait. And it is why the cures must all, in their different ways, do the same thing — the state by compelling contribution, the fence by dividing the shared into the private, the covenant by binding the members to one another — to close the gap between the interest of the one and the good of the all.5

5. Collective action is the general form of which the commons (shared cost overused) and the public good (shared benefit underprovided) are instances: any non-excludable shared interest faces the gap between individual and group incentive. The three cures each bridge it differently — the state compels (The Leviathan), privatization divides (The Enclosure), self-governance binds (The Covenant). The pervasiveness of the gap is the pervasiveness of the failure.

The free rider is the figure at the heart of the commons: the one who takes the benefit and leaves the cost to others, not from wickedness but from the plain logic of a shared prize and a private price. Olson showed that this logic, far from being a quirk of pastures, governs all collective action, and governs it the more harshly the larger and more anonymous the group — so that the common interest, which all profess, few will pay to defend. With the tragedy, its mirror, and their common logic now before us, the volume turns to the cures the age proposed. The next plate takes up the formal anatomy of a real commons, the fishery, and the two orthodox remedies it has called forth: the quota the state imposes, and the private right the market assigns — command and enclosure, the only two escapes the tragedy was thought to allow.
