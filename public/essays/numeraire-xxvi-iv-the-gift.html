The Gift

Mauss; the gift that binds where a price clears, and reciprocity, the most primal of the unwritten rules and the seed from which all trust grows.

The gift is never free: to give obliges, to accept obliges, and above all to receive obliges one to return — so that where a price clears the account and lets the parties part as strangers, a gift leaves a debt that binds them; and from that debt, endlessly renewed, the whole web of obligation and trust is woven.

— the argument of this plate, after Marcel Mauss, 1925

Beneath trust lies a still older norm, the most primal of all the unwritten rules: reciprocity, the expectation that what is given will be returned. Marcel Mauss uncovered its workings in the gift, and in doing so revealed something the impersonal market tends to hide — that exchange can bind people together rather than leave them strangers. A gift, Mauss showed, is never truly free; it is bound by obligations as strict as any contract, and chief among them the obligation to reciprocate, which turns a single gift into an ongoing relationship. Where the market's price clears the account and dissolves the tie, the gift leaves a debt that holds it fast, and from that debt the whole fabric of trust and cooperation is woven.

Studying the gift economies of archaic societies — the rivalrous feast-giving of the Pacific coast, the ceremonial exchange that circulated among the islands of the western Pacific, the old laws of Rome and of the Germanic and Hindu worlds — Mauss found that what looks like free generosity is in truth a system of iron obligation. Three duties bind it. There is the obligation to give: for one of standing to withhold a gift is to refuse friendship and alliance, almost to declare hostility. There is the obligation to receive: to decline a gift offered is an insult, a refusal of the bond it proposes. And there is, above all, the obligation to reciprocate: to accept a gift and fail to return one, in due time and due measure, is to lose face, to fall into debt and dependence, and in some accounts to court spiritual danger. The gift, far from being the opposite of exchange, is exchange in one of its oldest and most binding forms — and the generosity that adorns it is, beneath the surface, an obligation enforced as strictly as any debt at law.1

The gift, Mauss argued, cannot be understood as a merely economic act, for it is many things at once — what he called a total social fact. In a single motion the gift moves goods (an economic act), creates an obligation that custom will enforce (a legal act), stakes the honor and the rank of giver and receiver (a moral and political act), and, in many societies, carries something of the spirit of the giver that presses for its own return (a religious act). The modern habit of separating the economic from the social — of treating exchange as a cold transfer of goods for money between parties who are otherwise nothing to one another — would have been unintelligible in the world Mauss studied, where to exchange was to enter a relationship, and the thing exchanged carried the relationship within it. The gift reminds us that exchange was social before it was commercial, and that the impersonal market, far from being the natural form of all dealing, is the historical exception, a late and special case in the long record of how human beings have moved goods among themselves.2

Here is the distinction that matters most for the market, and it is a sharp one. A sale is complete in itself: the price changes hands, the obligation is extinguished, and the parties, owing each other nothing further, may part as the strangers they were — indeed the impersonality is the very point, for it is what lets the market reach across the whole world among people who will never meet again. A gift is the opposite. It does not clear the account; it opens one. By leaving the receiver in debt — bound to reciprocate, but at a time and in a form not fixed in advance — the gift creates an ongoing relationship where the sale ends one. This is why a gift and the cash it cost are not the same thing, and why offering money where a gift is expected gives offense: the cash clears the account at a stroke, refusing the very bond the gift was meant to make. The gift purchases not a good but a relationship; the debt it leaves behind is not a defect to be settled and forgotten but its whole purpose, the thread by which giver and receiver are tied together against the future.3

3. The distinction that matters for the market: a sale completes itself — price paid, obligation extinguished, parties free to part as strangers, and that impersonality is the point, letting the market span the world. A gift is the opposite: it opens an account rather than clearing one. Leaving the receiver bound to reciprocate, at an unfixed time and form, it makes an ongoing relationship where a sale ends one. Hence cash offends where a gift is due — it clears the account, refusing the bond. The gift buys a relationship, not a good.

The principle beneath the gift is reciprocity, and it is perhaps the most universal of all social norms: in every known society there runs a deep expectation that a gift, a favor, a kindness — and equally an injury — will be returned in kind. This norm is the seed from which trust and cooperation grow, for a relationship is built precisely by the slow alternation of favors given and returned, each act of reciprocation earning a little more of the other's confidence. And reciprocity did not die when the market arose; it lives on inside and alongside it. Much of what passes for commerce is conducted not as a string of arm's-length spot transactions but as an ongoing relationship of mutual favor and accommodation — the supplier who quietly extends a little credit, the customer who stays loyal through a lean season, the partners who do each other small unbargained kindnesses against the day they will be needed. The relational contracts of the volume of the firm, and the cooperation sustained by the shadow of the future in the volume of the game, are reciprocity at work; even the bond between employer and worker has been read as a kind of gift exchange, effort offered beyond the strict minimum and answered by a wage offered above the going rate. Beneath the cash nexus, the old logic of the gift still quietly runs.4

Mauss's gift thus sets the volume's deepest layer. Reciprocity is the primal unwritten rule, older than the market and persisting within it — the norm that turns isolated exchange into relationship, and relationship into the trust on which the convention of the first plate was built. But reciprocity, trust, and convention have so far been described more than they have been analyzed; we have seen that the unwritten rules exist and that they matter, but not precisely what makes a regularity into a binding norm, how to tell a mere habit from a rule that carries the force of an ought, or why a norm that has held for generations can give way almost overnight. For that the volume needs an anatomy of the norm itself — a grammar that lays bare its parts and the conditions on which it stands or falls. The next plate supplies it. Beneath the market's impersonal exchange lies reciprocity, the most primal of the unwritten rules, and Marcel Mauss found its purest expression in the gift. The gift is never free: it binds the giver to give, the receiver to accept, and above all the receiver to reciprocate, and it is a total social fact — at once economic, legal, moral, and religious. Its decisive feature is that, unlike a sale, it does not clear the account but opens one, leaving a debt that binds the parties into an ongoing relationship where the price would have let them part as strangers. The principle beneath it, reciprocity, is perhaps the most universal of human norms and the seed of all trust, and it persists inside the market in every ongoing relationship of mutual favor — in the relational contract, the repeated game, and the bond of employer and worker. But to know what makes a norm bind, and what makes it break, the volume needs an anatomy of the norm itself. The next plate is the grammar.
