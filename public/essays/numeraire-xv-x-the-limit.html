The Limit

the limits of freedom of contract; unconscionability, duress, and the bargains the law will not enforce though both parties freely struck them.

The parties can promise more than the law will make binding.

— the argument of this plate, after the California Civil Code

The parties can agree on a term and still encounter a court unwilling to enforce it. Their agreement asks more of the legal order than permission to speak: it asks for remedies, judgments, and the use of public authority if someone later refuses to keep the promise. The law must decide which commitments deserve that assistance. Some objections concern how the agreement was obtained. Others concern the oppressive relation between its terms and the circumstances of its making. Still others remain even when both parties understand the bargain and willingly accept it. These questions draw different boundaries around freedom of contract. To understand the boundary, we must identify what the rule protects and what consequence follows from crossing it. The signatures begin the inquiry; they do not supply every answer.

California's Civil Code gives the distinctions a concrete legal setting. It requires free consent and identifies duress, menace, fraud, undue influence, and mistake as defects that can make apparent consent unreal. Its provisions distinguish unlawful detention from specified threats, rather than treating every unwelcome choice as the same wrong. When a contracting party obtains the other's consent through duress or menace, the law provides a ground for rescission. Section 1566 expressly distinguishes consent that is not free from an agreement that is absolutely void. That distinction preserves a role for the protected party in undoing the transaction. An agreement extracted through a threat and an agreement with an unlawful object therefore need not have identical legal consequences, even though both raise objections to enforcement. The purpose, the party entitled to invoke the protection, and the available remedy must be examined together.1

1. California Civil Code §§1566–1570 and §1689(b)(1). The statutory grounds and their specified causal relationships govern; the discussion does not equate ordinary financial need with statutory duress or treat rescission as automatic.

Unconscionability examines another difficulty: a transaction can bear the outward signs of agreement while combining an oppressive process with unreasonably one-sided terms. In Sanchez v. Valencia Holding Company, the California Supreme Court explained that the doctrine has procedural and substantive elements. Oppression or surprise in formation belongs to the first; excessive harshness or one-sidedness in the terms belongs to the second. Both must be present, although their required degrees vary together on a sliding scale. This is a contextual inquiry, not a rule that any unequal bargain must be annulled. The court expressly distinguished substantial unfairness from an ordinary bad bargain. A party's disappointment when an understood risk materializes does not, by itself, establish that the contract was unconscionable when made. The inquiry must return to the circumstances and terms of the agreement whose enforcement is sought.2

2. Sanchez v. Valencia Holding Co., LLC, No. S199119 (Cal. Aug. 3, 2015), complete §II, slip pp. 7–12. Its general principles require both elements, distinguish a bad bargain from unconscionability, and examine commercial context. The general two-element and sliding-scale analysis is reaffirmed in Fuentes v. Empire Nissan, Inc., No. S280256 (Cal. Feb. 2, 2026), slip pp. 6–7. This plate uses the general contract principles; both opinions separately address arbitration.
⁂

The remedy can preserve part of an agreement while refusing another part. Under California's section 1670.5, a court finding a contract or clause unconscionable when made may refuse enforcement of the contract, enforce the remainder without the clause, or limit the clause's application to avoid an unconscionable result. The statute also calls for a reasonable opportunity to present evidence of commercial setting, purpose, and effect. A bare sentence can consequently be insufficient to decide the issue. A provision protecting a party against a genuine commercial exposure may have a different significance from one that exploits a hidden obstacle to relief. Removing a clause and canceling the entire exchange are also different interventions: one can leave the promised delivery and payment intact, while the other can undo the arrangement on which both sides relied. The law's response has to fit the defect it has found.3

3. California Civil Code §1670.5(a)–(b); Sanchez, slip pp. 8–10, including its discussion of legitimate commercial needs. These are available statutory responses, not a claim that severance is required in every case or that an unfair outcome alone authorizes rewriting.

Some limits protect an obligation even against a deliberate attempt to bargain it away. California's section 1668 declares contracts whose object is to exempt someone from responsibility for that person's own fraud or willful injury to another's person or property to be against the policy of the law. Imagine two well-informed parties negotiating a clause that would excuse one of them from responsibility for deliberately defrauding the other in future performance. Better notice and a separately negotiated price do not remove the statutory objection. The issue is the responsibility the clause purports to extinguish. That example differs from asking who bears an innocent delay or an ordinary commercial fluctuation. Risk allocation is part of contracting; permission to abolish every responsibility cannot be inferred from that useful function. The particular rule and the particular liability matter, even where bargaining is fully informed.4

4. California Civil Code §1668. The example concerns a prospective exemption for one's own fraud, within the statute's express terms. It does not generalize to every limitation of liability, settlement of a past dispute, or allocation of commercial risk.

The parties' gains can also omit consequences borne by people who never joined the bargain. Suppose a buyer values a delivery at one hundred and its production consumes sixty. A price of eighty divides the forty units of private gain equally between buyer and seller. If production also inflicts seventy units of harm on a neighbor, the combined account is minus thirty. Both signatories can prefer the transaction while the specified total falls. With harm of twenty instead, that total becomes positive twenty; the neighbor still bears a loss until some arrangement addresses it. These numbers do not decide a lawsuit or measure every value at stake. They expose the missing consent behind the claim that a willing bargain necessarily benefits everyone affected. Public rules may protect interests that the two signatures cannot represent, and their justification requires attention to those interests as well as to the exchange.5

5. The illustration holds all other effects fixed and expresses benefits, resource cost, and outside harm on one stipulated common scale: 100 − 60 = 40; 40 − 70 = −30; 40 − 20 = 20. The price is a transfer. Neither a positive nor a negative total is presented as a legal test of enforceability; applicable duties and remedies require their own legal grounds.

The limit of contract is therefore part of the institution that makes contracting possible. Rules protecting voluntary agreement, rules addressing oppression, and rules preserving responsibilities beyond waiver do different work. Their effects must also be judged: dependable enforcement supports reliance, while enforcement of every written term would allow the machinery of law to magnify the very defects it recognizes. The distinction introduced in The Gap now becomes decisive. A default supplies an answer the parties may replace; a mandatory boundary restricts the answers they may make binding. Within those boundaries, their freedom remains a powerful means of arranging an uncertain future. The next plate, The Contingency, asks how much of that future an agreement could cover if its possibilities could all be named, and what would be required to turn each possibility into a promise.
