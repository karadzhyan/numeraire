The Neutral

the neutrality of money; whether money can move real things or, in the long run, only the prices at which they are quoted.

A change in the measure leaves the loaf alone; a change in the promise may move the hands that make it.

— the argument of this plate, after Hume, King and Watson

Suppose that tonight every monetary figure is doubled: the notes in the drawer, the wage on the contract, the price of bread, the balance of every debt. A worker paid five hundred for a week's work, buying bread at five a loaf, now receives a thousand and pays ten. The wage still buys a hundred loaves. A debt of two thousand becomes four thousand, still worth four hundred loaves. The baker has acquired no flour and the worker no additional leisure; the alteration has passed through their accounts without changing their commands over goods. The earlier Collected plate called money a mask, and this is the sense in which the image holds. Yet the experiment achieves its result by changing every relevant number together. It proves something about a complete change of monetary units. Whether an actual change in the quantity of money leaves the real economy unchanged is a further question.1

1. Invented change of units, with every nominal claim and price scaled by two: 500/5 = 1,000/10 = 100 loaves of wages; 2,000/5 = 4,000/10 = 400 loaves of debt. These ratios are identities under the stated scaling. They do not establish that a monetary injection automatically scales wages, prices and existing debts in this way.

David Hume put both sides of the problem into his essay on money. The abundance of coin, he argued, was different from the abundance of work and goods: a larger quantity of the medium could be absorbed into the prices at which existing things changed hands. But the movement toward those prices occupied an interval, and the interval mattered. In his account, merchants receiving additional gold and silver could employ more people before wages and the prices of provisions had fully risen. The added spending passed through successive hands rather than arriving everywhere as a completed adjustment. Hume also described the distress of the opposite movement, when receipts and employment contracted before the obligations people faced had fallen. His argument already contained a distinction that a simple veil can conceal: comparing two settled monetary scales is different from tracing the route between them, and the route passes through real lives.2

2. David Hume, Of Money (1752), especially the interval argument at printed pp. 30–33 in the retained Scott Library edition. Hume describes sequential spending and delayed price adjustment in a specie economy. This plate uses that reasoning; it does not adopt his numerical historical examples or treat his account as a measurement of modern monetary transmission.

The contract makes the difference tangible. Keep the original debt at two thousand while the price of bread rises from five to ten: repayment now commands two hundred loaves rather than four hundred. The debtor's obligation and the creditor's claim have both changed in purchasing power, although neither signature has moved. A wage fixed for a season can similarly change in real terms as selling prices move; the employer's decision then depends on demand, other costs and the possibility of expanding production. In a workshop with spare capacity, extra orders may call forth extra work. In one already at its limit, they may instead call forth a higher price. These are different responses to different circumstances. To derive a real effect one must identify the term that does not adjust, the person who faces it, and the decision it changes. Doubling the money by itself supplies none of that missing description.

⁂

Long-run neutrality is a more precise proposition than the claim that money never matters. In its familiar output form, it asks whether an exogenous, permanent change in the level of the money stock eventually leaves the level of real output unchanged. It can therefore coexist with a temporary expansion or contraction. The word eventually belongs to a model of adjustment, however; it does not name a universal number of months, and the proposition does not follow from the calendar alone. Robert King and Mark Watson show why testing it also requires assumptions about what is moving money and output. Money may respond to changes in real activity, and more than one disturbance may move both. An observed association cannot identify the effect of the monetary change unaided. Their investigation distinguishes the proposed economic mechanism from the statistical relationship through which someone hopes to discover it.3

3. Robert G. King and Mark W. Watson, Testing Long-Run Neutrality, Economic Quarterly 83/3 (1997), pp. 69–74, 84 and 95. The definition is neutrality of money toward output, not invariance of every real quantity or every household's wealth. The authors' empirical conclusions depend on identifying restrictions, integration assumptions and the limits of their bivariate models; no result is imported here as a universal law.

A second distinction concerns the difference between an amount and a rate of change. A once-for-all increase in the money stock is one experiment; keeping its growth rate permanently higher is another. Superneutrality is the stronger claim that a permanent change in money growth leaves the relevant long-run real quantities unchanged. Ordinary neutrality does not establish it. A continuing inflation can alter the cost of carrying purchasing power in non-interest-bearing money. If the nominal return available elsewhere rises, people have more reason to arrange payments around smaller cash balances, and the arrangement may take time or resources. Robert Lucas analyzes this cost even in economies without monetary surprises. In one of his constructions, time devoted to transactions reduces the time available for production. An economy can thus be past the original price adjustment and still have a real allocation problem associated with the ongoing monetary regime.4

4. King and Watson (1997), the superneutrality discussion at pp. 84–87. Robert E. Lucas, Jr., Inflation and Welfare, Econometrica 68 (2000), pp. 247–248 and §5, pp. 262–269, develops the cash-management mechanism. It concerns specified transactions technology and opportunity costs. Lucas takes the underlying growth rate as independent of monetary policy (p. 254, n. 6); the mechanism is not presented here as an estimate of an effect on long-run growth.

Nor does the settlement of prices restore every balance sheet to its previous owner. The creditor whose fixed claim has lost purchasing power and the debtor whose obligation has diminished may change their spending differently. Matthias Doepke and Martin Schneider show how such asymmetric responses can turn a redistribution that sums to zero into a change in aggregate saving or labor supply. Their model makes the remaining length of people's lives and their capacity to adjust work consequential; it does not infer a common response from a common change in the price level. The effects can persist as the affected households rearrange consumption over time. This is a different argument from a wage that has yet to catch up. It explains why the claim that prices are flexible is insufficient, by itself, to establish that every monetary disturbance washes out of every real decision.5

5. Matthias Doepke and Martin Schneider, Aggregate Implications of Wealth Redistribution: The Case of Inflation, Journal of the European Economic Association 4 (2006), §§2–3, pp. 495–499. The linear production technology fixes factor prices; heterogeneous responses generate aggregate effects. Persistence in their finite-lifetime model ends as affected cohorts leave it. This supports a possible channel, not a claim that a one-time monetary change necessarily alters output forever.

The veil remains useful when it teaches us to ask what lies behind a monetary figure. A higher wage is not necessarily a larger command over goods, and a taller stack of notes is not another harvest. But money also carries promises through time, distributes the capacity to spend, and gives people a means of completing exchanges. The conditions under which those functions leave real allocations unchanged must be shown rather than concealed inside the metaphor. Neutrality supplies a benchmark against which a mechanism can be made visible: the fixed obligation, the cost of transacting, the unequal response to an unexpected gain. None offers a printing press as a substitute for knowledge, equipment or work. They explain instead why the way an economy manages its monetary promises can affect how those real resources are used. The mask does not create the face; its fit can still change the expression.
